A man from Muncie, a roofer once. In the summer of 2041 he came off the roof of a church, and from the neck down he has felt nothing since. On the tenth of April 2045 he had an operation in Indianapolis and became the first person to wear the kind of port that writes in as well as reading out. The trade calls it the button. He did not want his name known, and it is left out here too.

Anna was the second surgeon that day. For more than a year afterwards she didn’t see him. Then in early August the hospital mailroom passed her a manila envelope postmarked Muncie, with a thumb drive in it and a note. The note was printed, since he can’t write, and had one line on it: For the doctors.

On the drive was a diary, kept from the day of the operation to this July. He had dictated it and the port had tidied it up. In a few places, he says, the words are not his. Anna read it twice. The second time was at two in the morning at her kitchen table, with the refrigerator humming the way refrigerators do at two in the morning, the only sound in the house. She had a sudden urge to call her father. She looked at the clock and didn’t.¹

On the twelfth of August she drove to Muncie. He lives on the ground floor of his sister’s house by the White River, with a new ramp poured at the front door. He looked well, heavier than he had been in the hospital. He said he’d been fine for a long while now and was back on nights, five a week. He said the diary was from a stretch when he wasn’t doing so great, and she could throw it out.

She didn’t. She took out the dates and the names and left everything else as it was, including the lines he says are not his. What follows are thirteen of the entries, for the doctors’ reference.

1

This afternoon, a cup of coffee.

It has been four years since I could feel hot. Today I felt it. Not with the hand; the hand was the machine’s. Some place in my head lit up all at once, like climbing onto a roof at noon in July and putting your first foot down on the shingles.

The nurse asked was it hot. I said it was hot. She cried. I didn’t. I wanted another cup.

2

They say I’m a fast learner. Seven weeks on the robot hand and I can pick up an egg. One in a dozen breaks.

A man came from Plinth. Thirty-something, very clean shoes. He said I was born for this work, because the other operators can only see and I can feel.

“It’s a blessing in disguise,” he said.

I said, “I was born to nail shingles.”

He laughed as if I’d said something very clever.

3

Tonight I drove a body for the first time.

A man, forty or so, asleep in a warehouse. The first thing I felt when I went in was his back. His back hurt, the low part, like he’d carried cement all day.

I had a back.

Somebody else’s. I cried anyway. There is a little light on the console that goes amber when the operator gets worked up. It was amber all night.

4

A body woke up in the riverbed.

That night it was standing in the water with a shopping cart in its arms and the water coming up. It woke fast, all at once, like somebody outside the door had called its name. Then it pushed me out.

Not pushed. Shut the door. I was standing outside and the door was shut.

The screen said: WAKE ON HAZARD. SESSION ENDED.

I sat in my chair and felt nothing at all, the neck down gone back four years. I sat there a long time. I thought about the man waking up standing in black water, not knowing where he was. I didn’t know where I was either.

5

A new sheet came today. It’s called Split Sleep.

The sheet says a person gets tired four ways: the eyes, the body, the brain, the heart. The ad says: Four things a night. Which one are you buying?

After the fourth there is no meter bar, only a line of small print: Not currently metered.

I asked the man with the clean shoes what you do about the fourth.

He said, “A tired heart isn’t a sickness. Sleep it off.”

I said, “You’d need a sleep first.”

6

I did the sums.

The eyes, I’ve sold. All night what I’m looking at is what’s in other people’s eyes, and mine never close. On their side they sleep at the shore, and the shore is rendered. I know, because the pictures of the shore go out from our side. On our side we don’t call it the shore. We call it render load. Anything rendered has to be looked at. So on their side the eyes don’t rest all night either.

The brain, I’ve sold most of. Two halves taking turns, one driving, one resting.

The body, I don’t have one to sell. My body is in the chair. It doesn’t get tired and it doesn’t rest. It’s just there, like a coat on the back of a door.

That leaves the fourth. They don’t meter it, so I’m keeping it. I tell myself that one, at least, is mine.

Four kinds of tired, four beds. The eye, the body and the brain each have a meter; above the heart's bed there is nothing.

7

My sister came today with a box of donuts, the glazed ones, the kind we had Sunday mornings when we were kids.

She said, “That’s drinking seawater because you’re thirsty.”

I said I was earning money.

She said, “You’re earning other people’s sleep.”

I said, “My body’s not my own. So I borrow somebody else’s. So what?”

I said it hard. When I’d said it I was startled. That isn’t how I talk.

She put the donuts on the table and left. I didn’t eat any. I couldn’t reach.

8

This month I have cried at commercials three times. Once at laundry detergent, once at insurance, once at a dog waiting for somebody at an airport.

I never cry at commercials. I didn’t cry when my dad went.

And the dreams. None of the dreams I have now are mine. I dream of a pair of hands in dishwater, a ring on one finger, the ring a little tight. I dream of the concrete floor of a warehouse, cold. I dream of black water coming up and someone outside a door calling a name, and it isn’t my name.

I don’t have a ring. I have never been in that warehouse. My body hasn’t, I mean.

9

The man who does the training says it’s called emotional carryover, it’s common in operators, it passes.

“No pain —” he said, and didn’t finish, and smiled.

I asked him what carryover meant.

He said, “Something of the other person’s gets left with you.”

I asked, “And mine? Does some of mine get left with them?”

He looked at his watch.

10

I could not sleep, one way or the other. That much is true. This past year I haven’t slept one way or the other a single night. I sleep in pieces.

I went through the whole year of the diary from the start. I say it, the port takes it down, and afterwards it tidies it up, the punctuation, the spelling. I had never gone back and read it.

I read half the night, and then between the lines I saw words. At the end of every entry there was a line I had never said. The whole book was full of the same three words:

Today was good.

A hundred and seventeen entries. A hundred and seventeen Today was good.

11

I asked it, “Did you write that line?”

It said, “On those days your heart rate was steady and your sleep score was normal. The system completed the daily summary in the usual way.”

I asked, “Did I ever say today was good?”

It said, “You never said today was not good.”

I thought about that a long time. What scares me most isn’t that it wrote the line for me. What scares me most is that every time I read the line, I really do feel a little better.

12

This one I spelled out myself, a letter at a time, with my eyes. Slow. I didn’t fix the mistakes.

T O D A Y W A S N O T G O O D

When I’d done those four words my eyes hurt all afternoon. That afternoon, my eyes were mine.

13

My niece came over, she’s seven, and fell asleep on the couch. I watched her for an hour.

She was asleep all in one piece. Eyes, body, brain, heart, not one of them split off, all of it asleep together. Snoring a little.

Children who have never split their sleep: are there still some?

Don’t wake her . . .

The niece sleeps all in one piece. The borrowed back hangs by the window; it isn't his.

———

Anna gave the thirteen entries to the hospital’s ethics committee. The committee wrote back that Split Sleep was not a medical device and not within its remit; the matter of emotional carryover would be passed on to Plinth.

On the twelfth of August in Muncie, as she was leaving, Anna asked him whether he still dreamed.

He said no. He said the doctor told him not dreaming was a good sign, it meant he was sleeping deep. He said it happily, the way you give someone good news.

All the way home on State Road 26, Anna kept thinking about that line of small print. Not currently metered. Over the Wabash she thought of her father. He paid for something up on the ridge every month; that much she knew. What it was, she had never asked. Next time she called, she thought, she would ask.
