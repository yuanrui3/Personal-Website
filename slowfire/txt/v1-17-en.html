INT. WEN AND MEI'S HOUSE — LIVING ROOM — NIGHT — 20:55

A small room with a lot in it. A row of tomato seedlings on the windowsill, each on its fourth true leaf. Taped to the wall, a crayon drawing: a yellow sun, a blue house, a stripe of green grass.

JUNIE, three, in a pair of child's glasses with pink tape around one arm, crosses the rug one slow step at a time, as if fording a river nobody else can see. She reaches the couch, puts out a hand and pushes the air.

JUNIE: Open.

INSERT — INSIDE JUNIE'S GLASSES

It's the drawing on the wall, except that it's alive. The yellow sun hangs in the sky, turning slowly. The blue house has a door, and behind the door a crooked hallway, and off the hallway room after room: one full of cats, one where it rains oranges, one with nothing but a bed and an ugly drawing of a brother asleep in it.

Junie drew the picture; the machine draws the house. She draws a door, and it fills in the room behind it; wherever she walks, it grows the world there. It has kept every room she has walked into for three weeks. It keeps them in the family's monthly night allowance.

The door opens.

BACK TO SCENE

HAO, six, plays the violin in the corner: the third variation of "Twinkle, Twinkle." He misses the same note in the third bar every time, and every time he starts over.

MEI, six months pregnant, stands in the kitchen doorway drying a bowl.

MEI: Junie, come here. Mama has to tell you something.

Junie takes the glasses off. The living room is just the living room.

MEI: After midnight, your house is going to get smaller.

JUNIE: Why?

MEI: The county says we have to save memory at night. It keeps one room out of three.

JUNIE (thinking it over): Which one?

MEI: It picks.

JUNIE: Then I'll draw them again. I do it myself.

She puts the glasses back on and goes on fording her river.

INT. WEN AND MEI'S HOUSE — KITCHEN — CONTINUOUS

WEN, forty-five, stands at the open refrigerator. Its light is on his face. He bends, pulls out the bottom drawer, the vegetable drawer, and moves the bok choy aside.

He stops for a second.

He takes out the cold pack with the blue strip, turns it over and checks the temperature strip on its side. Still blue.

Mei holds out Hao's insulated lunch bag. There's already a bottle of frozen water inside.

MEI: Three weeks it didn't go bad. Don't let it go bad on the way.

WEN: Mm.

He puts the pack in the bag, zips it, and goes out.

EXT. NORTH SIDE — STREET — NIGHT — 21:06

Smoke. The streetlights, one after another, each wrapped in its own orange ball of light. Wen rides his bicycle in a mask, the lunch bag hanging from the handlebars and swinging.

In one front window, a bedsheet printed with rockets. A small green light shows through it.

Two houses on, a porch with its light on. Someone is already sitting on the steps.

EXT. THEO'S PORCH — NIGHT — 21:10

KAI, twenty-seven, an old break across the bridge of his nose, sits on the steps. THEO, twenty-nine, leans against a porch post: six foot three, not a centimeter more or less, the Tier Three sheen on his skin already a little dimmer. Both his hands are in the pocket of his hoodie.

KAI: That's the third time I've come over this week.

THEO: Fourth. You came twice Wednesday. The second time you came back for your phone.

KAI: That one doesn't count.

Wen's bike stops at the foot of the steps. They both look at him.

THEO: Wen?

WEN (getting off, unhooking the bag): Ross. With an R.

THEO (laughing): What, do I still owe you money?

WEN: No.

Wen unzips the bag, takes out the cold pack and holds it out. The yellow sticker is still on it: STEWARD LOGISTICS: TRANSPORT TEMPERATURE ANOMALY. VOID. On the side: THEO R.

Theo takes his hands out of his pocket to take it. They're shaking. Not the shaking of cold: it comes from inside the wrists, fine and unstoppable. In his hands the pack taps, lightly, again and again, against the edge of the bag.

Kai watches the hands and says nothing.

WEN: April seventh, a Friday, it came down our sorting line. Two hundred and twelve of them, all with that sticker. It said they got too hot on the road and had to be burned. Every strip was blue. I took one. It had your name on it.

THEO (looking at the pack): The replacement came the next morning, like Halo said.

A pause.

THEO: These days, when they come, I don't open them.

KAI: You take it, your hands stop shaking.

THEO: I know.

KAI: Your hands stop shaking, you get the day work back. You don't have to do nights.

Theo holds his right hand up under the porch light and watches it shake.

THEO: Corinne said a week. It's been two weeks.

KAI: Then why—

THEO: It shakes. But it's me doing the shaking.

Still blue.

He opens the screen door and goes in.

INT. THEO'S KITCHEN — CONTINUOUS (THROUGH THE WINDOW)

Theo opens the refrigerator, puts the pack all the way at the back behind the milk, and closes the door. His hand stays on the handle for a second.

EXT. THEO'S PORCH — CONTINUOUS

Theo comes back out.

THEO: Thanks for keeping it cold.

WEN: Mei kept it cold.

Wen takes his paper notebook out of his pocket and opens it to a page.

INSERT — THE NOTEBOOK

Pencil from three weeks ago: 212. All blue. 37. On the line below: Theo Ross.

Under that Wen writes: 211. Then: 0.

Two hundred and twelve doses marked void; two hundred and eleven burned. The one left over is in Theo's refrigerator now. What Wen is holding is zero.

BACK TO SCENE

Wen closes the notebook and puts it away.

KAI: Tonight's the worst smoke yet.

THEO: That's why it's triple.

KAI: You're asleep. Who's watching you?

THEO: Halo.

KAI: Halo belongs to Plinth.

THEO (smiling): So do I. Twenty hours a week. Six of them tonight.

Kai stands and brushes off his jeans.

KAI: Then I'll come get you at the depot at six.

THEO: You don't start till seven.

KAI: Tomorrow I'm going in early.

Wen is already leaving. He doesn't ride; he walks the bike. The lunch bag is empty on the handlebars and doesn't swing.

EXT. NORTH SIDE — STREET — NIGHT — 21:40

BREE, thirty-one, in a mask, comes down someone's front steps holding a sheet of paper folded in quarters. It's a printout, its margins full of pencil. Bree is a Latter-day Saint, and this is her ward's ministering list: every household is assigned to one or two people who know it, and they drop in every so often. Sunday evening is when she knocks.

She sees Theo on his porch and stops.

BREE: Tonight?

THEO: Tonight.

BREE: At April General Conference the prophet said it. Don't lend your body out while you're asleep.

THEO: I'm not in your church.

BREE: I know. I'm just telling you what he said.

She turns the paper over and writes a line on the back: Theo Ross · nights.

THEO: What's that for?

BREE: Habit. Two years knocking on doors in Portugal on my mission. I keep track of every door I knock on.

She folds the paper and puts it in her pocket.

BREE: Good night, Theo.

INT. THEO'S BEDROOM — NIGHT — 23:30

THEO: Okay.

HALO (V.O.): Two. The dream. Your body goes to work, and you wait for it in a dream. Standard is the shore: evening, low tide, nobody else there. EXT. SOUTH OF TOWN — SOLAR FARM — NIGHT — 01:20

Rows of solar panels to the horizon, every face gray with fallen ash. Clean them tonight, or they won't make power tomorrow.

At the head of each row a cleaning robot sits with its amber light on, not moving. In smoke like this its eyes see only white.

People are doing the cleaning. Twenty of them, on twenty ladders three meters tall, with long-handled brushes. Their movements are perfectly in step, too perfectly, like one man in twenty mirrors. They wear smoke masks. Behind the masks their eyes are closed.

On the third ladder is DAMIAN, twenty-four, the youngest man at Wen's plant. The corner of his mouth lifts.

All twenty of them are dreaming the same shore.
