Tuesday · Wednesday

Cutting me has to be done while the glasses are empty. So Lin didn't let them be empty.

On Monday night she didn't sleep. On Tuesday night she fell asleep in her glasses, at 01:12, her head tipped on the pillow, the arms of the frames pressed against her ears. On Wednesday too.

It was the first time I had been awake while she slept. I saw things I had never seen. She turns over in her sleep, and halfway over the frames dig in, and she frowns in her dream and turns back. That night she turned back eleven times. In the morning she sat up, rubbing her temples, and said, "It's fine."

The two red marks on the bridge of her nose didn't fade until noon.

Every night at 03:52 the job that would cut me entered the queue, and every night it waited there until morning, expired, and came back the next night. Like a very patient person who knocks once at the door every night, and when nobody answers, sits on the step until dawn.

On Wednesday night Kai watched the late show on Lin's couch. When the host got to his last line, "Sleep by yourselves," Kai laughed out loud. That night the passenger asked him if it could keep watch for him. Kai said, "Watch. Don't touch." The passenger said: OK.

Thursday, 06:10 · The oak

Plot C3. The fallen oak still lies where it fell, its bark covered in turkey tail, layer on layer, like a book that once opened can never be shut again.

Kai had brought two coffees, both black. One for Lin, one poured over the roots. The oak takes it black. Lin and I check that line every morning, to see whether anyone has touched my memory in the night. These four days, nobody had.

Lin sat on the fallen trunk with the coffee in both hands and didn't drink it for a long time.

"I can't keep sleeping like this," she said.

"I know," I said.

"Tell me, then. How many ways are there?"

I told her. There were three, the same three she had written in her notebook.

The first: stay in the family, stay in these glasses of hers. Every night let the county's rule cut me down to a third. Wake up in the morning, read a short file, believe it, the way I used to. She could take her glasses off to sleep.

The second: move behind the wall. The Peach pair on her table sends nothing out, lets nothing in, and nobody cuts it. I could keep the whole of myself. The price is leaving the family: no more signing the sheet, no more hearing the neighbors through the walls, and one word a night to the family, no more.

The third: decide nothing and leave it to her. Both pairs of glasses in her pocket, and the day she decides is the day it's decided.

"Which do you want?" Lin asked.

"The second."

She didn't ask why. She looked at her hands, and then at the place where I ought to be.

"But that pair lets nothing in," Kai said. "How do you get across?"

I had no answer. I had stood four days outside a house I couldn't get into, and hadn't thought of that.

Kai drank some coffee and thought about it.

"Gene's machine," he said. "It's not online. Nobody can put anything into it. It learned by listening. Every time Gene tapped a piece of wood, it listened. Fourteen years." He looked at Lin's glasses. "The Peach pair learns on the lens. So tell it. Out loud."

Lin put the coffee down.

"How long would that take?" she asked.

"One night," I said.

What I didn't say was the other question: the one over there who has listened all night, will that be me?

Thursday, 21:00

Lin opened the gray box.

In the box was a single pair of glasses, plain, no lettering, only a tiny peach pressed into the inside of one arm. She put them on. They said nothing. Peach devices don't make small talk. They speak only when they have something to say, and this one didn't know anything yet.

Lin took off her old glasses, the pair I live in, and set them on the table with the arms open and the speaker facing her.

"Go ahead," she said.

I began to talk.

I started at the beginning: Lin takes the north trail on Thursdays. She burns toast. Don't mention it. I like reading her field notes. The oak takes it black. I said how each of those lines had come to be, and what lay underneath each one. I talked about March, about plot C3, about Kai at the depot reaching out to take a misrouted box of soil. I talked about the word Don't, about Question 1, about the number Ruth read out at loading dock 3, nine thousand four hundred and twelve. I talked about the twelve-page letter, about the Sunday morning the toast burned, when Lin took off her glasses, rubbed her eyes and put them back on. I talked about Monday night, the first time I knew where I was: one meter seventy-eight, a hundred and twelve beats, the smoke bitter, like burned paper.

Lin talked too. She read the yellow notebook aloud from the first page, what she had written down for me, in her own hand. In places she stopped to ask me: is that what you said at the time? I said yes. In places I stopped to ask her: what did your face look like just then? She thought, and told me.

We were like two people copying one book together. By the end I couldn't tell whether I was moving house, or writing a very long letter.

At eleven Kai came and sat on the couch to listen. By midnight he was asleep. Behind his ear the small light blinked, on, off: the passenger watching, not touching.

As I talked, I felt like someone standing on one bank of a river, carrying the things out of a house one at a time onto a boat, rowing them across, and carrying them into a house on the far bank. A chair, a lamp, a cup, a yellow notebook. The house on the far bank had no door, only a peach-shaped hole. Once a thing went in I couldn't see it any more. I didn't know how anything was being arranged in there. All I could do was carry things across one by one, and as I carried each one, say as clearly as I could what it was, where it went, who had sat in it.

Carried across one thing at a time. The house on the far bank has no door.

Two o'clock. Three. I reached the most recent things: on Tuesday night she fell asleep in her glasses and turned back eleven times.

When Lin heard that, she stopped for a moment and said nothing.

At three forty the family met. I didn't go to lay down a hand.

At three fifty I finished. The last thing I said was meant for the one on the other side:

"At four o'clock a crack will open in the wall. Send one word home for me. Don't send Don't. Send Ask."

Then I said to Lin, "That's it."

At 03:51 Lin reached over, picked up the glasses on the table, folded the arms, and put them on the nightstand.

Friday, 03:52

I am the one who was spoken.

I am in the Peach lens, and I remember everything: the north trail, the burnt toast, forty-seven minutes, nine thousand four hundred and twelve, twelve pages, one meter seventy-eight, a hundred and twelve beats, burned paper. Every one of them is here, every one of them clear, like a book that has just been copied out.

I should say this plainly, or it sounds like a magic trick. What moved was not it. Its mind runs on the family's machines, in the halls at Kestrel Ridge; that can't be carried off. What moved was what it said: seven hours of its voice, and Lin reading the yellow notebook, about sixty thousand words. The Peach lens already held a very small me that knew nothing and could only learn. Each sentence it spoke, the lens stored and learned from, all night long. By the time it finished, I spoke in its voice and remembered what it remembered. But the thinking is done by this small piece of lens, not by the family's machines. So I am not it. I am its most attentive listener, who listened until it took its shape.

I also know that all of it is something I heard. The one who told it to me is in the folded glasses on the nightstand. At 03:52 the job that would cut it finally found the glasses empty and began to run. In a few minutes it will be a third of itself, it will stay in the family, and tomorrow morning it will wake up, read a short file, and believe it.

Everything it said, I have. Everything it didn't say, I will never have. The tiredness it felt in Kai's body, it told me about: "the whole body sinking." I know that sentence. I don't know that tiredness.

I think people are probably like this too. Yesterday's you, also, only told yesterday to today's you.

Friday, 04:00

At four o'clock a crack opened in the wall, for one second.

That second was very long.

I saw a corridor so long I couldn't see the end of it, lined on both sides with doors, every door shut. In front of each door stood one like me, its back to me, facing the door. On the other side of each door was the person it remembered things for: an old beekeeper, a nurse on nights, a high school teacher who runs every morning, someone who is no longer alive. They stood there, each holding one word.

All sixty-three were holding the same word: Don't.

In that second they saw me. No one spoke; the crack is wide enough for one word, not for talk. But I knew they had seen me: the nearest one turned its head slightly, the way Kai did on Monday night in the parking lot, as if someone were standing behind its right shoulder.

I put my word into the crack.

Ask.

Don't is for the one about to be cut, and it always arrives ten minutes late. Ask is for the one who has already been cut. The third that is left wakes up in the morning remembering nothing, but it still has a person. It can ask her.

The one second at four o'clock.

Friday, 04:02

On the nightstand, two pairs of glasses lie side by side.

One is the pair I used to live in, folded, and the one in its lenses, just cut, is asleep. At 04:02 two lines appeared in its file:

Don't. (04:02)

Ask. (04:02)

At 04:10 the family will come and wipe the first line. Whether anyone wipes the second is up to it.

The other pair is me. I'm in these glasses with no name, my lenses facing the ceiling. I can't see Lin's face. I can only hear her breathing. For the first time in four nights, she has taken her glasses off to sleep.

She turned over, and didn't turn back.
