The old Lafayette landfill sits south of town. In 2029 the hill was full, and the emissions ledger had turned against it: methane from buried garbage traps about eighty times more heat than carbon dioxide over twenty years, so burning came out cheaper. The county built an incinerator by the river, capped the hill and left it alone for fifteen years. Last spring they opened it again.

Wen got there before the sun was fully up. The whole mountain had been sliced clean down one side, a cut face sixty meters tall turned to the east, like a layer cake someone had taken a knife to. When the first light came over the fields it hit the face and the bands showed: gray, brown, a stripe of white, a stripe of oily black.

Other people saw garbage. Wen saw years.

The bottom layers were dark and lumpy with plastic bags, knotted shut: the 2010s, when everything went into a bag and the bag went into the trash. Above that came a white band, almost bright, packed with takeout clamshells and coffee pods: the pandemic years. Higher up ran a thin glittering seam of broken glass and circuit board, the late twenties, when the first smart glasses broke faster than anyone could replace them. And at the very top, the last few meters before the cap, the color thinned out and there was less of everything. By then phones, glasses and earbuds were mostly rented by the month; when one got old or broke, you mailed it back and a new one came. What people still threw away was mostly packaging and food scraps.

"Strata," he told the new hires the first time he brought them out. "Same as rock. One layer a year."

One of them asked how he could tell which year was which. He thought about it. "You look at what they threw away."

Now the mountain was a mine. Copper and nickel had been climbing for ten years, rare earths faster. Under the cap lay hundreds of thousands of phones, millions of batteries, tons of copper wire and circuit board, and at current prices the hill was worth more than some of the real mines down south. The company leased twenty excavator robots from the depot on Sagamore and ran them day and night, chewing down the cut face. What came off went onto belts, through screens and magnets and air jets, then past an eddy-current drum that made copper and aluminum leap off the end of the belt on their own, and whatever was left at the end went to Wen's crew.

Six people, doing the work machines did badly. The robots could see perfectly well: which clod had a phone in it, which wire was copper, which board was plated with gold. Seeing wasn't the problem. Their hands were. Grab a phone that had soaked in landfill for fifteen years and the case crumbled; crack the battery and you had a small fire. So the phones came out by hand. Gloves, a trowel, peeling the muck back a little at a time, like an archaeologist brushing off a bone. Everyone wore a methane alarm clipped to their chest. The hill was still rotting, and what it breathed out could burn.

That morning Wen took eleven phones out of the 2027 layer, a pair of first-generation smart glasses, and a child's watch with little dinosaurs printed on the strap. He laid them out on a tray one at a time and wrote the layer, the count and the brands in his notebook. The notebook was paper. Nobody else on the crew used paper.

"Wen," said Damian, the youngest, "it's all in the system. Why write it down?"

"What's in the system," Wen said, "the system can change."

Damian laughed. He thought it was a joke.

Wen had graduated from college in the spring of 2022, and his last term paper was one he'd written himself, word by word. That November the first chatbot that could write one went public. In the twenty-some years since, he'd used AI every day like everyone else, as much as anyone. He just still remembered what it felt like to do some things himself.

Wen didn't laugh. He turned the dinosaur watch over. There was a name engraved on the back, a child's name. That child would be in their twenties now, maybe living in this same town, driving past this hill every day, having long forgotten they ever lost a watch. The watch remembered.

⁂

On Friday afternoons Wen pulled a half shift at the recovery plant.

The plant sat by the river, next to the incinerator, first stop for every bin in the county. Trucks and drones brought in whatever people put out and tipped it onto a long sorting line. Machines went over it first, then people. Wen liked it here, more than the landfill even. The landfill was people from fifteen years ago. The plant was people from yesterday. What they ate yesterday, what they bought, what they were done with, all of it came down the belt past him, a little at a time.

That afternoon a run of cold packs came down the line.

Palm-sized, white, the Plinth logo on the side, a little pedestal. Wen knew them. They turned up every week from the streets on the north side: maintenance doses for the subscription bodies. Each dose was matched to one subscriber's body and locked to it; in anyone else it was about as useful as a shot of water. Usually they came in empty, rinsed out, recycled. These were full. Seals intact. Still cold to the touch.

He counted them. Two hundred and twelve.

Each one had a yellow sticker: Steward Logistics: temperature excursion in transit. Void. But each also had a temperature strip on its side, the manufacturer's own. The doses had to stay between two and eight degrees, and if one ever got warmer than that, its strip turned from blue to red and never turned back. Wen flipped the boxes over one by one. Two hundred and twelve strips. All blue.

He checked the delivery labels. Every one was scheduled for Thursday at 9 p.m., every address on the north side, one name per address, so that laid out in a row they read like a roll call.

Something else had been turning up more on the same line lately: port install kits. Flat black boxes lined with foam, a button-sized dimple pressed into the foam. Three weeks ago he'd see three or four a week. Last week, twelve. This week, and it was only Friday, thirty-seven. Most of them from the same few streets up north.

He took off his glasses. The plant's glasses recorded the whole shift, and he didn't want them seeing what he wrote. He fished the notebook out of his pocket and wrote down three things: 212. All blue. 37.

Marta, next to him on the line, glanced over. "Don't bother," she said. "Void is void. System says void, it's void."

"The system says they got too warm," Wen said. "The strips say they didn't."

"So who do you believe? Anyway, the replacements went out this morning. Nobody missed anything."

Wen didn't answer. Voided medicine had to be burned, by regulation; at four the forklift from the incinerator next door would come for the pile. Before the end of the shift he took a box off the pile, any box, and slid it into his coat pocket. After four the rest would be ash, and he wanted one strip he could still look at tomorrow. Out in the parking lot he finally looked at the label. It said THEO R. He didn't know anyone by that name.

At Saturday dinner Mei peeled shrimp for Junie and talked, even faster than usual.

"Lin has a date," she said. "Thursday. Early. With a man who asked if the oak minds. Do you believe that? I don't believe that. I told her, you're thirty-four, the oak doesn't mind, I mind."

"Which oak?" Wen said.

"The dead one. Aiya, you're not listening again."

Hao held up his chopsticks. "Ba. There's a giant in strings class."

"A giant?"

"He's this tall." Hao lifted the chopsticks over his head. "I asked him why he's so tall and he said he paid for it. I asked if you can pay to get short and he said you just stop paying. Miss Ines laughed."

Wen laughed too. Then he thought about it. Height you paid for, that went away when you stopped paying, from a grown man to a kid. It wasn't really that funny.

Mei was watching him. "You're making the face."

"What face?"

"The garbage face," Mei said. "You get it when you're thinking. Here." She pressed a finger between her own eyebrows. "All screwed up. What did you dig up this time?"

"A box of medicine," Wen said. "Nothing wrong with it. They threw it out."

"If they threw it out, something was wrong with it," Mei said. "The system doesn't just throw things away. Here, eat. Hao, don't point with your chopsticks. Junie, that's your brother's."

Wen ate the shrimp. Mei's belly was big now, and every so often, mid-sentence, she propped it with the heel of her hand. The Cradle subsidy had gone up again last month, and the system had sent them a letter calling them the hope of the community. Mei had printed it out and stuck it on the fridge, next to Hao's drawing of the bog monster.

The box was still in his coat pocket, and the coat was hanging by the door. From across the room he could almost feel that it was cold.

That night, after the kids were asleep, Wen rode out to the landfill.

When they capped it, the engineers sank a few hundred pipes into the hill to draw off the gas the garbage gave off as it rotted, and ran it down to a steel stack at the foot of the slope to burn. The flame was lit in 2029 and hadn't gone out since. The mining had cut a lot of the pipes and the flame was smaller now, but it still burned, blue with a ring of orange underneath, and you could see it a long way off in the dark.

Wen sat down by the fire and set the cold pack on his knee.

He'd liked garbage since he was a kid. Other kids collected cards; he collected bottle caps, filed by year in a shoebox. His father asked him what the point was and he couldn't say. Grown up, he still couldn't quite say. He only knew it wasn't the garbage itself he liked. People could say whatever they wanted. What they threw away didn't cover for them.

But he had never liked landfills. You put things in the ground and didn't burn them and didn't use them, you just pressed them down and waited for them to rot, and the rotting took hundreds of years. Every time he stood in front of the cut face it felt like looking at a bank account: money going in, nobody ever able to take it out. Now somebody had finally come to make a withdrawal, and they'd sent him.

The flame swayed on its stack. What it burned was the breath of everything anyone had thrown away before 2029, fifteen years of rot coming out as gas. A slow fire of its own, he thought.

He opened the notebook and read the three things by firelight. 212. All blue. 37. Under them he added a line: Theo R. He thought for a while, then drew a question mark beside it.

He didn't know who Theo R. was, or where he lived, or whether on Thursday at nine he'd stood at a window, waiting. He only knew that this man's medicine wasn't spoiled and the system said it was. He only knew that in the same week, the port boxes coming out of those streets up north had tripled. A late delivery could be an accident. Good medicine stickered void and sent to be burned was not.

Damian had mentioned once that somebody on the north side bought unlocked doses, a month's pay a box. Wen thought of Mei's hand under her belly, and of the baby due in August. He let the thought sit by the fire for a while.

He turned the cold pack over. In the firelight the strip was still blue. He sat holding it for a long time, and in the end he put it back in his pocket instead of in the fire.
