Voss said he would fetch the clubs himself.

For Mira would say it wasn't light yet, the grass was wet, and he didn't want to hear it. Twenty to six, Sunday 13 August, and it wasn't light yet, not properly; the eighteen holes lay in the thin mist like a map nobody had unfolded, and the sprinklers were still going round, one after another, tick, tick, tick, putting water on a piece of ground that could have been covered in panels. He knew what the ground cost by the hour, in water and in power; tally worked it out for him every morning, per hectare-hour, to four decimal places. He never looked. He paid.

The Citroën stood at the clubhouse door. No microphone in the car, and none on the course. These were the two places in his week where nobody listened. He left his glasses on the passenger seat. The first was a long par four with water on the left. He still used the persimmon driver he'd bought in 2017, when the company was small and Anna was two. The sound of wood on a ball was one of the few sounds left in the world that nobody had recorded. Probably someone had, he thought. Nobody cared.

The ball went out, landed on the fairway, rolled a couple of metres left and stopped.

He walked to it. Walking was half of the thing. There had been a young man carrying the bag once, then a cart, then nothing, and now he carried it himself. A man of seventy-odd kilos carrying a bag of eight, five kilometres, done before full light. Hall 7 had been his again last night, the eighty-fourth night. People in town said he was buying a county's memory; people said he was buying a county. He'd answered neither. He paid. Same logic as the sprinklers, he thought: water on ground that didn't need watering.

Anna had called last week. Not the kind of missed call that had once turned up in Mira's log from nowhere; Anna herself. Four minutes, about a patient, hers. He'd listened and not interrupted. Afterwards Mira asked whether he wanted the call kept and he said no. Mira said, then I'll just remember it. He said, fine.

Second hole. Third. The mist was lifting.

Lin was at Celery Bog.

The bog in August is bad walking, grass to the waist and grasshoppers out of every step. She'd got there at seven; Mei's call at half past six had woken her: it's started. No hurry, Mei said, this is the third, I know better than the doctor, don't come before ten. So she'd come to the bog. Because not coming to the bog meant waiting in a room, and waiting in a room was Mei's job today, not hers.

A row of chanterelles had come up along the fallen oak, egg-yellow, like a row of small trumpets. She crouched and counted. Eleven. She didn't pick them. She took the glasses off and set them on the oak, both pairs, she carried two now whenever she went out, the old ones and the Peach, and felt with her fingers between the chanterelles and the bark. Damp. The oak was still giving off heat; six years down and still being eaten. C3's reading had gone up again this summer. Slow fire. Her grant had been renewed, and on 18 May she had told a pair of glasses first and a person after, which she still found a little funny, and not entirely.

She was thirty-four. Mei was thirty-eight. By this afternoon Mei would have three. She had a sister who had a copy of a cat, she had a pair of glasses with a copy living in them, she had something that asked her each morning what had happened to it yesterday, she had Kai. Kai had a match today. He'd said he'd come to the hospital after, straight after, honestly, the match starts at noon, who plays football at noon, it's to save money, the Plinth bodies are run by a model, the model is billed by the compute-minute, and noon compute is the cheapest there is, once the sun hour's over a match costs twice as much. He'd talked a long time. She'd listened.

She cut one chanterelle with the little knife, the biggest, and put it in the paper bag. For Mei. Mei wouldn't eat it; Mei never ate anything she brought out of the bog, "I'm not a toad"; but she'd look at it and say, aiya. That was enough.

She put both pairs back in her pocket. The old one still asked. The new one listened. She said to her pocket, "Eleven. Note that." Both of them did.

Old Coal died at seven.

Wen knew it that exactly because at half past six, when Mei rang Lin, she was still breathing, lying on the notebook, the paper still moving; and at seven, when he came back from seeing Mei into the car, Mei had insisted on a car to the hospital, "I'm not having it at home, two in the hospital, this one in the hospital, don't think because there's a Cradle now you have it in the kitchen. A good horse doesn't go back to the same field", when he came back the paper wasn't moving.

He didn't call the children. Hao was still asleep, Junie was asleep. He put his hand on her back. Still warm. He found a towel, pale blue, Hao's from when he was small. He lifted her with the notebook and then slid the notebook out; she was light, sixteen years and she had never been this light. The page the notebook lay open at was still that page, The one underneath. She had slept on it for more than a month. There was her fur on the paper.

He put her in the garage, on the bottom shelf of the tool rack, wrapped in the towel. This afternoon. This afternoon, after Mei was done and the children had seen their brother or sister, then. Not one arriving and one going in the same day; the children couldn't take it. Neither could he. One thing at a time.

He went back to the kitchen. The new one, the one Hearth had matched, was on the windowsill, facing out. He stood a while. It turned its head and looked at him. Not at his boots. At him. Then it jumped down, walked to where the notebook had been, sniffed the table edge, and lay down. He didn't know whether this was the one from the recordings or the one underneath. There was no such entry in the recordings, he thought. The cat in the recordings had never had a dead cat.

He wrote in the notebook: 13 August, seven o'clock. Nothing else. Then he went to wake the children.

The match started at noon. Not the league's rule, Plinth's. The match was Plinth's experiment. Most of its nights were still driven by licensed remote operators, and it wasn't yet sure about letting a model drive a body on its own, so it tried one on a football pitch first. A model is billed by the compute-minute, and noon is cheapest. Everyone on the pitch got paid, the sleepers for their bodies, the rest a match fee.

Kai was there by eleven. The old ball field by the river was the same field, the lines freshly painted, with a new relay mast at the touchline, which linked the Plinth bodies to the model running them up at Kestrel Ridge. The county's first official mixed match. The sign-up sheet had three boxes to tick: Self; Plinth, the person asleep and a model doing the running; Port, playing yourself with a copy behind the ear that knew tactics, that knew where every man would be a second from now. Shirts carried numbers only, nothing to say which kind.

Kai was the third kind. Port 0417. The passenger inside, watching, not touching. On the sign-up sheet he had ticked "ported" and written beside it, in his own hand: copy not participating. Nobody asked him what that meant.

Theo was on the other side. Theo was the first kind, playing himself, a hundred and ninety centimetres of template with a port behind his ear switched off for the day and hands that still shook a little, not enough to matter for football. He yelled across at Kai: "Come on then, friend, I'll give you one goal today." Kai yelled back: "Honestly, you could give me two and I wouldn't want them."

Ezra was at the touchline with a clipboard. He hadn't come for the football. He'd come for the three rented bodies and their awake watcher. A rented body, whether a person or the model was driving it, had to have someone awake beside it who could wake its owner if anything went wrong. One watcher for three bodies: enough. #2310 had seven lines now. The seventh he'd written last week: Not being able to tell who is who on the pitch is part of the design. So the watcher has to be able to tell off the pitch.

At twenty to twelve the players sat in a row along the touchline. The rented bodies sat quite still, their owners already asleep and the model not yet loaded, eyes open and looking at nothing. The ones playing themselves drank water. The ported ones drank water too. Once the model was in, you couldn't tell. Ezra looked at the device list: eleven numbers, a word after each. Self. Self. Plinth. Self. Port: observing. Self. Port: tactics. Plinth. Self. Plinth. Self. The list was the only honest thing on the field, he thought.

The sun hour sounded.

At noon exactly every device in the county sounded the one note together, not high, not low, one second long, meaning the power was free now. At the touchline, twenty-two pairs of glasses and a clipboard sounded together, and in the same second the three Plinth bodies came online. In the car park at Celery Bog, Lin's two pairs sounded together in her pocket; she was already on the road. In the delivery room the small dot on the wall went from orange to green, and Mei saw it, between two contractions, and thought, good, the power in this room is free now, I can have the air on, and said to the nurse, turn it up. On the bottom shelf of the tool rack in the garage nothing sounded. On the golf course Voss was on the seventeenth green with nothing in his ears, but he knew it was noon, because the sprinklers stopped. Water at noon half dries in the air before it lands. He holed a two-metre putt. Nobody saw it.

At the Commons, on Wildcat Creek, Denise was having a nap. There were no glasses in her room, so nothing sounded. It was the thing she liked best about the Commons, better than the eight hours: at noon, nobody told her.

Twenty-eight minutes in, something happened.

Nothing big. Their number seven, "Plinth" on the list, its owner asleep inside it and the model up at Kestrel Ridge doing the running, went in on Kai. Not hard, but half a beat late; the ball was gone and the foot still came. The model had the ball right; it knew where the ball would be. It didn't know how slow this leg was. It had learned football on tens of thousands of bodies, and this one was a little slower than their average. Kai went down, nothing wrong, swore once and that was that. He crouched on the grass. Seven stood over him and put a hand down. The hand was warm. The face had nothing on it, not cold: the model ran the legs, not the face. The whistle went. At the touchline the awake watcher stood up and wrote something.

While Kai crouched there, there was a sentence behind his ear. Not a voice. A sentence.

Want help?

He waited a moment. He knew the answer. It was only that this was the first time it had offered help. Since 1 May, since he'd said "Next time, ask first", it had asked only once, whether it could watch over him; he'd said watch, don't touch, and since then it had done nothing and asked nothing more. Three months. He didn't know whether it had been waiting for a moment worth asking about, or waiting for him to be down on the grass.

He said, out loud, quietly: "No. Not today."

OK.

He got up. The ankle was fine. He thought, honestly, it asked. He'd tell Lin this afternoon, or he wouldn't; Lin had other things on this afternoon.

Theo jogged over from the other side and thumped his back: "Come on, friend, you all right? Good. Seven didn't mean it, the guy's asleep, he won't even know he went in on you." And jogged back. Theo was happy today. He'd started nights again after 15 May, one awake watcher for every ten bodies; he said that way he could sleep. He said Ines was teaching him to bow an open string, and the hand still shook. He said these things looking at the ball, not at people.

In the second half Kai scored. He scored it himself, and he knew it, because there was nothing behind his ear. An ugly goal, mishit; their keeper was playing himself and never reacted. He would remember it a long time. Of the twenty-two on the pitch, not one of the others knew who had scored it. The list at the touchline said only: Port: observing.

At three in the afternoon Mei said, aiya.

She said aiya a good many times. Lin was by the bed with both pairs off, side by side on the windowsill. Mei said, what did you take them off for, put them on, keep it, show the child some day. Lin said no. Mei said, you. Then aiya again.

Cradle wasn't there. Cradle is never in the delivery room; Cradle is in the forms. The dot on the wall was green, the sun hour not over yet. The nurse was young, under thirty; Mei asked her whether she had children, she said no, Mei said, if you're going to, go early, this is my sister, she doesn't either, two in one family who don't. Lin said, I didn't say I don't. Mei said, you didn't say you do. Lin didn't answer.¹

She thought of the eleven chanterelles. That tree had been down six years and was still putting things out without knowing it. Being eaten, still warm.

At twelve minutes past four the child came. A girl. Loud. Mei said, aiya, this one's the loudest. Then Mei was asleep. Before she slept she said: name tomorrow. Lin said, all right.

Lin held her. She was heavy. She hadn't known a thing this small could weigh this much. On the windowsill the two pairs lay side by side, neither on. For this hour, nothing was keeping anything. On Mei's account there would be a line later, Cradle's, viable, plus one. The household's night allowance was up a fifth from today. Junie's drawing world would get its two rooms back. Lin knew none of that. She held her.

Voss finished on the eighteenth at half past four.

He'd played badly. He always played badly, ninety-odd, a hundred or so, that wasn't the point. The point was that he'd walked it. He got back to the car and put the glasses on. Mira said: "There was a call this morning. Anna. She said no need to call back, she was only asking whether you'd gone to play."

He sat a while.

"What did you tell her?"

"I said you had."

"What did she say?"

"She said, good."

He started the car. Mira said nothing more on the road, because there was no microphone in the car; she waited at home. He thought: she knew I'd be out playing today; she called because I'd be out. He didn't follow that thought any further. Eighteen holes, five kilometres; he'd gone round in eighty-nine. The first time under ninety.

Wen was in the garage at half past six.

The children had seen their sister at the hospital. Hao said she looked like a sweet potato. Junie said she looked like Junie. Now the children were indoors, Lin was at the hospital with Mei, and he was in the garage on his own. He opened the towel. She was the same, only cold.

He'd picked a spot in the yard, under the windowsill. He knew it wasn't allowed; the county had a rule, pets go to the recovery plant, by the kilo. He worked at the recovery plant. He knew where what went there went. He dug. The ground was dry, August ground, a foot down and it was sand. He put her in, towel and all. He remembered something Lin had said once, about burying and burning, about where things in the ground end up. He'd argued with her about it once. Today he didn't argue. He filled it in.

When he straightened up, the cat on the windowsill was watching him. Facing in. It hadn't faced out again since the afternoon.

He went inside, washed his hands, and under the line in the notebook added another: Twelve minutes past four, a girl. The two lines sat together. He looked at them a while and closed the book.

Hao was in the doorway. "Where's Old Coal?"

Wen said, "Tomorrow."

Hao looked at the cat on the sill, and at the notebook. He didn't ask again. He was six; he knew everything; he knew what tomorrow meant.

The sun hour was long over. Night power was getting dear. Tonight the household's allowance was a fifth bigger; Coal's model and Junie's fourteen rooms both fitted, with a little left. Nobody gave the little to Old Coal. Old Coal had never been on the ledger.
