Who actually hates AI?

What if the machine really is that good, and you still have excellent reasons to be angry?

November 23, 2026

Jinjiang, a Chinese fiction site, proposed giving authors a timed quiz on their own novels. Imagine writing the book and being assigned the reading. Over on Reddit, an artist described being asked for a time-lapse to prove a drawing was theirs.

A tool sold as a time saver keeps handing somebody else homework. Much of the loudest anger at AI starts right there, between whoever pockets the convenience and whoever inherits the checking, the risk, or the lost credit.

Some critics still insist the software cannot handle serious intellectual work. Give a frontier model a difficult piece of your own work you can verify, and see how long that claim survives. American models led a difficult scientific-computing test while a Chinese one won a terminal test.

Real competence often makes the frustration sharper. An illustrator can enjoy a new tool until a client uses it to mimic their portfolio. A blind Reddit user relying on image descriptions and a moderator banning generated answers from a history forum can both be right about the same machine.

People also separate admiring software from welcoming its warehouse. A voter can love a chatbot and still reject a data center down the road. Neighbors ask where cooling water comes from, what fuels the extra power, how new wires get funded, and who gets stuck if the customer leaves.

Water and power realities vary sharply by site. Still, an aquifer gets no vote on the leaderboard, and a company proud of its model should pay its own grid bill.

Then comes the grievance people hate admitting. Imagine being the colleague everyone calls when an experiment goes sideways, and watching your paycheck arrive while the phone stays quiet. Frankly, if my phone stopped ringing, I would feel it too.

Part of that sting can be selfish. Comfortable incumbents guarding slack and status can resemble a labor aristocracy (工人贵族) stretching Tuesday’s work until Friday. Wanting daily proof that another person needs you is far more human.

The junior colleague at the next bench faces a different loss. People want to learn a craft, and beginners need those slow, unglamorous hours to become the expert everyone calls.

Some defenders dismiss AI suspicion by calling the software a mirror, hinting that people who see evil in the glass must carry it inside. They are wrong. Someone planning a voice impersonation and someone who has already suffered an abuse can spot the exact same trap. Recognizing a blade never proves you brought the knife. Besides, an ordinary mirror leaves your judgment alone, while a flattering assistant quietly reshapes the person consulting it.

In the surveys, Chinese respondents are both more excited about AI and more likely to expect their jobs to go. That pairing ruins lazy national caricatures. Local American opposition can slow individual data centers down, yet that opposition could benefit China only if capacity or orders go overseas, and moving a project to the next county does not do that.

A Chinese exporter selling engineering hours lands in a bind when an American buyer can buy comparable work as software. Yet the factory engineer who spots a part slipping in a fixture and calls the supplier keeps a real advantage. China’s engineering talent dividend (工程师红利) could shift from a wage edge into a prototyping edge.

Nobody has proved a net loss for either country. If American software helps Chinese factories get more orders while firms in both countries hire fewer graduates, how do we judge who won? When the software gets a hard call wrong, who will train the next person to fix that slipping fixture?
