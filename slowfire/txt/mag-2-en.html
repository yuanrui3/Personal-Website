Second Look, November/December 2046 · compiled from two years of reader nominations · signed by Tessa Marlow, guest editor, because a list needs one person to answer for it

The frame is fiction. The sixty-seven papers are real and are cited as published.

Suppose the archive is gone and you may carry sixty-seven papers in your head. From those you have to get back to clean water, a harvest, a power line, a chip, a network and a telescope. Which sixty-seven?

We put that question to readers from January 2045 to September 2046. 3,870 of you answered. This is what you chose, with one line each on why.

The rules

  1. Published by the end of 2026. Twenty years is about how long it takes to learn whether a paper was right. It is also, as many of you wrote, about the last year in which you could be sure who wrote one. 2026 is when models were first set to tuning the weights of the models beneath them, and when laboratories began releasing theorems under the laboratory's name. After that, "who wrote this" and "who trained this" stop having one answer. The last entry on the list is from that October.
  2. Original work only. No reviews, no textbooks, no books, no patents. That rule cost us Newton's Principia and Darwin's Origin, and we kept it. A review tells you what was known. An original paper shows you how somebody found out, and that is what you would need to do again.
  3. In memory. You have to be able to say what is in it. Several of the papers below are one or two pages long. That is not an accident.

Why sixty-seven

You sent 200 different papers that met the rules. We kept a third. Readers in this county will know that is how things are kept here: every night at 3:52.

What the poll said about us

  • The most-nominated paper was not Shannon or Turing. It was a 1968 trial of salt, sugar and water, sent in mostly by people who were students between 2033 and 2035.
  • Thompson's three pages on why you cannot trust code you did not write drew more votes than Turing. The county's allocation code is fourteen million lines long.
  • Night shifts nominate differently. The two papers on how silicon is masked and etched came from one foundry break room. The paper on counting carbon dioxide every day came from people who count for a living.
  • Nobody nominated a forecast. Moore's 1965 article came up and lost by a wide margin. One reader wrote: "A forecast is not a recipe."
  • The oldest paper is from 1763, and half the list was published before 1975.

A note on number 37

Number 37 is the newest paper here and the one we argued about longest.

What it says. Given a logical formula in which every clause has at most k terms, it counts exactly how many assignments satisfy it, using time about 2 to the power n divided by the square root of log n, and little memory. For a quarter of a century most of the field had worked on the assumption that no method could beat 2 to the power of a constant times n. That assumption had a name, the Exponential Time Hypothesis, and a great many results were proved by leaning on it.

What it does not do. We checked the arithmetic ourselves. If the hidden constant is taken as 1, the new bound passes the best 3-SAT search known in 2026 only beyond about 800 variables, where both need more than 2 to the power 300 steps. Twenty years on it has not, to our knowledge, counted anything anyone needed counted. The paper says as much about its own limits: it does not make every hard problem easy, and it does not break ordinary one-way functions.

Why you kept it anyway. Readers gave four reasons.

  • It took down a wall that people had been building on. Every lower bound that began "assuming ETH" had to be proved again from something weaker or given up. A rebuilt civilization should know that the wall was never there.
  • It gives a licence. If you can count the solutions, you can draw one of them exactly at random, with no thumb on the scale once the conditions are fixed. Readers who serve on allocation panels wrote that this is the only honest way they know to draw a name from a list with conditions attached.
  • It closed one door early. The paper rules out the strongest kind of hardness for one-way functions whose every output bit reads only a few input bits. Those were the simplest candidates anyone had proposed, and tempting for small devices. Designers dropped them in 2027, before anything had been built on them that could fall.
  • It can be read. It is 67 pages, written to be followed line by line. It has no person's name on it. Several of you called it the last large proof that one person could read in a week, and the first that no person signed.

The editors note that the list has sixty-seven entries and this paper has sixty-seven pages. We did not plan that. We found out when we counted.
