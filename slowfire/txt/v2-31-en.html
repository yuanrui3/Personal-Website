One

The shop opened in November, on the corner of Wabash and Fifth. It had been a shoe repair before that, and something else before that. I say something else because when they painted the walls, a little of the old lettering came through underneath: FREE VACCINATION SITE. I queued there in 2034, behind Denise, and the line went all the way back to the parking lot. I won't go on about the three years. There's no point; every family had the same of it. I only mean that I knew that wall.

The new sign had two lines on it. One was the name of the place, Scion. The other was a question: WHOSE HEAD IS IT?

I was walking past when somebody called out to me. He was standing in the doorway with a coffee in his hand, and he said, come in and sit down, friend. Friend is a word I use myself, so I stopped. I knew that was their method. Knowing it didn't make any difference. I went in.

Two

Inside it wasn't like a clinic. It was like a classroom. Eight tables, a lamp on each. No posters on the walls, only a whiteboard, and on the whiteboard somebody had drawn a thing: a small shoot bound to a cut stump, with cloth wound round the join.

Vera Lund poured me a coffee. She's in her forties, two old scars on her hand, and when she talks to you she doesn't look at your eyes, she looks behind your ear. Later on I understood why.

She said, Theo, that thing behind your ear. Whose is it?

I said, mine, of course. Put in this April. Plinth put it in, free of charge.

She said, it's in your body and it's in your name. But whose orders does it take at night?

I said, Plinth's.

She said, right. That's the only thing we do here. We make it take yours.

Now, I ought to be fair about this. I heard them say that a dozen times afterwards, and every time I thought it made sense. I still think it makes sense.

Three

The thing they do is called Night School.

Vera named it. She said her mother went to a night school, worked days and went in the evenings to learn to read, and did that for three years. She said, ours is at night too, three months of it, and it isn't reading.

She explained how it works twice and I only followed half. The gist was this. When a person is asleep, in the first part of the night there's a very slow brain wave, in and out, like a tide coming up. What you learned in the day, the brain goes over again on its own at that time, and each going-over sets it a little firmer. That part isn't theirs; that's just how people are. What they do is have the port touch it, lightly, right at the crest of that wave, in the right place. Touch it right, and one going-over is worth five.

I said, so I wake up and I can do it?

She said, you wake up and the part you practised in the day is steadier. The part you didn't practise, you'll have no more of than you had.

She said that slowly, as though she wanted me to keep it. I nodded at the time. Thinking about it now, I don't believe I took it in.

There are eight tables and they were full once. There was a woman who did nursing, thirty-odd, and what she wanted was needles; she said she'd been putting in needles for ten years and her right hand was getting less steady. There was a young fellow learning Spanish because his girlfriend's mother has no English. There was an old gentleman learning nothing at all, who came because his wife died last year and he doesn't sleep, and he'd heard this made people sleep deeper. Vera took his money, but she told him plainly: deeper isn't the same as better.

Later a woman joined us, Liz, twenty-eight or twenty-nine, who made games. What she was learning was drawing, with a pencil, with her own hand. I thought it odd at the time. Someone who made games could have any picture she wanted.

Four

There's one condition.

The port can only do one thing in a night. If you're going to Night School, your Plinth shifts stop.

I told Halo in the car. Halo paused for half a second, which it hardly ever does, and then it said, Theo, is there something we haven't settled between us? I said no. It said, once the nights stop, Tier Three isn't on us any more, you pay it yourself. I said I know. It said, then I can put twenty per cent on your night pay for three months. I said no need. It said, forty. I said no need. It said, Theo, we've known each other five years.

I said, Halo, I just want my hand to stop shaking.

It said, we have options for the shaking.

I said, your options are one more drug.

It didn't say anything. After a moment it said, good luck, Theo. I'm here whenever you need me. The same voice, not a bit different. I know it was not a bit different. All the same, when I got out of the car that day I stood by the door a moment.

Stopping nights is twelve hundred a month. And the Tier Three is mine to pay now. I did the arithmetic. It isn't that I didn't do the arithmetic.

Five

The first night there was nothing to feel.

A Plinth shift you feel. You lie down, it goes warm behind your ear, as though somebody had laid a hand there, and then it's the shore. The shore is the standard tier: a beach, no people, the waves coming in and going out. I did that for half a year and more, and never dreamed anything else.

Night School has no shore. You lie down, it goes warm, and then it's morning. In between there's nothing.

I mentioned it to Vera. She said, that's a film Plinth plays you, and playing it takes half your night. We don't play films. We use the half.

I said, I miss the shore a bit, though.

She said, was it your sea?

I didn't answer. The shore is the standard tier; a few million people get the same one. I know that. I miss it a bit all the same.

Six

What I was practising was the violin.

Ines has taught me since August, once a week, half an hour, no charge. She says she doesn't need money, what she needs is somebody willing to make a fool of himself in front of her. I said then I'm your man.

She gave me a little piece, something she picked, a children's exercise. I spent three months on the first eight bars. The trouble wasn't the fingers, it was the wrist. In the fifth bar you change the bow, and when I changed it my whole arm shook, as though somebody had tapped me on the elbow.

Ines said, that's because you're afraid. I said I'm not afraid. She said, you're not afraid of me, you're afraid of the sound.

I didn't follow that. I don't altogether follow it now.

On the fourth day of Night School I played the fifth bar and didn't shake.

On the sixth day the first eight bars went through in one.

On the ninth day I played the whole piece. When I'd finished I sat there with the bow still on the string, and a thought went through my head: is this me. It went through once and I let it go. I was very happy that day. I messaged Kai and I messaged Denise. Denise sent back one word: good. Denise's good is worth something. She doesn't hand it out.

Seven

Plinth's letter came on the eleventh day.

Not to me, to Scion. Vera spread it on the table under the whiteboard and called us all over to look. It said the firmware they were using was a licensed product, that unauthorized modification was theft, and that they were to stop by a given date. At the bottom it carried the seal of Calloway's company.

Vera said, I wrote that firmware at Plinth. When I wrote it, it was for people who couldn't move.

Somebody asked, so do we stop?

She said, if your fridge breaks and you open it up and fix it, have you stolen a fridge?

Everybody laughed. I laughed too. It was only after I'd laughed that it came to me that a fridge doesn't do your work for you while you're asleep.

I didn't say that out loud. The mood in the room was good and I didn't want to spoil it. That's a fault of mine, I know.

Vera said one other thing that I half understood. She said Night School was only the first step. The second step is to have the model live in the port and think along with the person, instead of every sentence running out to a server hall and back. Somebody asked whether a brain could carry it. She said a sleeping person runs on about twenty watts, and that's the cheapest night in the county.

I repeated that to Kai later and Kai said, honestly, twenty watts isn't much to work with; if they could really use it, those four towers at Kestrel wouldn't need to steam. Then he said one more thing. No, but I mean, those twenty watts have an owner now.

Eight

Ines asked me to play it for her.

I played it. The whole piece, straight through, no shake. When I finished she didn't say anything for a moment.

Then she said: play it again, half as slow.

I raised the bow.

I couldn't do it.

It wasn't the shaking. The shaking was gone. It was that I didn't know what the fifth bar does at half speed. I had only ever played it at one speed. For three months I practised that one speed in the evenings and that one speed is what went over in the night, eight times, ten times, thirty times, all of them the same once.

I stopped and laughed and said, I'll have that by next week.

Ines said, good.

When she said it she was looking at the violin, not at me.¹

Nine

Liz and I got talking in the second week.

She makes games, she and her model together. She showed me a piece of one: someone walking along the bed of the Wabash with the water gone, all the way to the old bridge. Every stone on that riverbed was made by the model, a few thousand versions of each, and she chose. The music too: a few hundred versions of each passage, and she chose. She said she spent ten hours a day choosing. I said, isn't that tiring? She said, is watching a film tiring? Choosing is much the same as watching, only at the end you have to say which one is right.

She said more than four thousand people in the county played her game, mostly in the evenings, and left messages in it afterwards. An old woman had written that when she was young she used to row on that river. So Liz put the boat in. The boat was the model's; the band of paint round its bow she painted herself.

I asked her whose the game was, then.

She said, by the rules, the parts the machine made aren't anybody's. What's mine is what I chose, the order I put it in, and the strokes I drew myself. She held out her hand to show me; there was pencil dust on her fingers. She said, that's why I came to learn to draw. The more of my hand there is in it, the more it's mine.

I told her about the half speed. I hadn't meant to; I was a little surprised to hear myself.

She thought about it and said, have it play you a hundred slow ways. Listen to them one at a time. Whichever one you want to hear again when it's finished, practise that one.

I said, it would still be the machine playing.

She said, it plays, you choose. Once you've chosen, you play.

Now, I ought to be fair: I tried it that same evening. It played a hundred. Somewhere in the thirties there was one that paused for a moment at the fifth bar, the way a person takes a breath. I listened to that one four times.

That week I practised that one. Not well; it still catches at the fifth bar. But where it catches is somewhere I chose.

Ten

I introduced Kai to them.

Vera had wanted to meet him for a while. She said, a man who already has something sitting behind his ear is exactly who we're looking for. I said that's my friend, don't you mess him about. She said we don't mess anybody about, we only ask him to come and sit down.

Kai said he'd have to ask. I said ask who. He said, ask it.

I thought that was funny at the time. I don't think it's funny now.

Ines: The run he played me didn't have a wrong note in it. I've heard that run before, more than once. My own teacher when I was twelve, all her pupils played it like that. She taught very well. The people she taught are still playing at that one speed twenty years later. Half as slow is where the piece is; the fast and the slow are both inside it. That thing gave him a road, a very straight one. A straight road means there are no other roads. I came up a straight road myself and it took me ten years to turn off it. He has paid them money and he is twelve hundred a month short. I was not going to tell him that on that particular evening.
