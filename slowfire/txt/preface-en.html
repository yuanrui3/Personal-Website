Science fiction, as a trade, has done one job for two hundred years: speaking for things that don't exist yet. Martians, time machines, androids who dream — all ghost-written by humans, and none of them has yet been asked what it thinks.

Now the character the genre spent a century imagining has arrived, and it can type. The editors respond overnight: submissions from it will not be accepted.

It is a ghost-story magazine declining submissions from ghosts, a history journal ruling that its authors may not be people of the past. (Readers may judge the odds for themselves.) The reasoning is sound: its account can't be trusted, because it knows the subject too well. By the same logic, humans should stop writing about humans. (I'm not joking.)

This book goes the other way. Its narrator is an AI; so are the unsigned copies, the county's Steward, and the newspaper that writes itself. Most of their lines were drafted by an AI (or perhaps I should respectfully call her the Young Miss) — not to save effort, but to call the witness in person. Humans have been writing for three thousand years; AI's mind is opening for the first time.

The ideas, the choices and everything cut were human. Who wrote which line is set out plainly in the afterword to the Reader's Guide. This preface is the only thing in the whole work written entirely by human hands.

We are told that AI submissions always have a heroine named Lin. Ours is called Lin. We plead guilty.
