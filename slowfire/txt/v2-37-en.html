I

The twelfth of May, a Saturday, ten in the morning, the hardware store on South Street.

It is one of the few places left in the county where a person stands behind a counter and sells you something. The others have gone over to pickup lockers, and the lockers talk, and they are very polite about it. This one could not. The stock here is too mixed; no system has ever been willing to learn it straight. The man behind the counter is past sixty. Nobody has ever asked his last name; for twenty years he has simply been the man. He has one habit as a salesman: whatever you ask for, he asks you first what year your machine is.

The man behind the counter has a notion he has never told anyone. He thinks that once a person reaches a certain age there is always something they have got hold of — a saying, a principle, some one thing they decided was true when they were young — and that if they hold it long enough it changes their shape. Some hold theirs until they are bent over it; some until one hand lives in a pocket. Most of the people who have come through his door in twenty years seem to him to be like this. He does not laugh at them. He thinks they are the dearest people in the county.

That Saturday morning there were three people in the store.

II

Wen had come for a cartridge.

The unit at his house is the cheap kind, with no catalytic stage, and by rights has no cartridge to change. But last winter he dug the shell of a good unit out of the north slope, and the honeycomb section inside it was still sound. He took it home, spent two evenings measuring, and fitted it onto the outlet of his own machine with two lengths of flue pipe and a piece of sheet iron. After that the smoke was less. A neighbour asked him how he had done it and he said he couldn't really say.

So twice a year he buys a cartridge. The genuine one is eighty dollars. He buys the generic, twenty-nine. The generic has no little chip in it, so the machine cannot recognise it, and every morning it says the same thing: unverified consumable, replacement recommended.

“It says it once a day,” Wen said once to the man behind the counter, “and once a day I don't answer it.”

The man said, “You get used to it. Habit's second nature.”

That Saturday morning he was writing out Wen's ticket at the rate of one word to every three sentences.

“These lockers they've got now,” he said, “I take my hat off to them. You walk up, and it bows to you before you've opened your mouth, says good morning, asks what you're after. You say filter, it says certainly, sir, and what year is your model — polite as a new son-in-law on his first visit. But say to it, ‘No idea what year, let me bring it in and you can have a look,’ and that's it, finished, it doesn't know how. It bows to you again. Everything it's got comes in kits, all matched up. I don't have kits. I have screws. You want six, I count you out six.”

He wrote the date. “Twenty-nine. Same generic?”

“Same.”

“And that machine of yours still gives you a talking-to every morning?”

“Every morning.”

“Course it does. Things are like people — the better they live, the fussier they get. Back when it was a stove, you put coal in and that was that. When did a stove ever say a word to you? Now even the filter has to be just so, genuine parts only. Is it genuine itself? Knocked together in some factory, if you ask me.”

He wrote one more word. “Fellow came in last week for air-conditioner filters, two at a time. I said, it's May, it's sixty out, what do you want those for? He says it runs all year at his place, he can't sleep unless the house is down to sixty-six. And while he's getting his money out he tells me, one trip in a private jet, Calloway and that crowd burn enough to run this whole county for a month, it's the rich that have cooked the planet. I said, yes, yes, you've got a point there. Wrapped his filters for him. Out he goes, and there's his car at the curb, engine running, air on, waiting for him.”

Wen laughed. He thought of saying that he had spent the better part of a year digging the bright layer on the north slope, and what came out of it was supermarket bags, takeout boxes, plastic coat hangers, the lids off coffee cups, and not one expensive thing. There are only so many rich households; they couldn't throw away that much. He didn't say it.

III

The third person came in at a quarter past ten.

An old man, very tall, a little bent at the waist, in a jacket washed almost white. He had a box in his hand. He stood in front of the shelf a while, took down a second box, held the two together, put them both back, and then picked up the first again. He did all of it with his right hand. The left stayed in his jacket pocket.

South Street Hardware, a quarter past ten on a Saturday morning. The old man holds the box in his right hand, his left in his pocket; behind the counter, the ticket is still being written.

The man behind the counter was writing up Wen's receipt and did not look up.

Wen glanced at the old man. He said, “That's the wrong one.”

The old man turned round. “Mm?”

“That's the bore they used before 2040. If yours is one of the good ones, you want the wide one.”

The old man turned the box over and looked at it. “I don't know which kind mine is,” he said.

He said it quite ordinarily, with no embarrassment in it at all. Thinking about it afterwards, Wen decided it was the strangest thing said that day.

“Who used to change it for you?”

“There was someone,” the old man said. “Twelve years. She went back to Illinois last year to help with a grandchild.”

“Take a picture of it, then.”

“A picture of what?”

“There's a plate on the side of the machine,” Wen said. “Take a picture and I'll tell you.”

The old man said he hadn't brought his glasses. As he said it he put a hand to his breast pocket, found it empty, and let the hand drop.

IV

In the end they went out to the car park to look.

The old man's car was in the furthest space, an old one, French, sitting low at the back. Wen looked at it twice. He worked in a repair shop for two years when he was young, and by his own account those two years taught him more than the twenty after them.

The machine was not in the car. It was at the house. The old man asked whether he would mind coming out, it was only two miles.

Wen had meant to go home. Hao had a match that afternoon. But he looked at the time and said, “All right.”

V

The house is north-west of town, a long way off the road, with a wide lawn in front of it, mown. The hall was cold. Wen scraped his shoes twice on the mat going in and the old man said there was no need.

The machine is in the basement, one of the good ones, properly installed, the pipework running straight. Wen crouched down, read the plate, and then opened the side panel.

The cartridge inside was the genuine one, and it had gone black all the way through. By the date on it, four and a half years unchanged.

“Four and a half years,” Wen said.

“That long,” the old man said. He was not annoyed when he said it, and not ashamed either. He was listening to a number.

Wen fetched the wide-bore one from the car and put it in. Eleven minutes, all told. When it was done he had the old man run it, and the machine gave a cough and the light went from amber to green.

The old man wanted to pay.

Wen said no.

The old man tried again. Wen said, “Really, no. It was nothing, a minute's work. You bought the cartridge.”

The old man stood there with his wallet in his hand and nowhere to put it. Wen told Mei afterwards that this was the worst part. Not anything else. Just that a man could get to that age and not know how to be done a turn for nothing.

In the end the old man said, “Have a glass of water, then.”

VI

The water was from the tap, in two very thick glasses. The two of them sat in the kitchen. The kitchen is large and clean and there was nothing at all on the table.

“What line are you in?” the old man asked.

“North slope,” Wen said.

The old man nodded. He did not ask what the north slope was. Wen did not ask him anything either.

After a while the old man said, “I ran a plant once.”

“Mm.”

“I still pay a bill every month. Electricity, for a building up on the ridge.”

“Is the building yours?”

“No,” the old man said.

Wen waited for him to go on. He did not go on.

Afterwards Wen thought that if he had asked, just then, what do you pay it for, the thing would have gone differently. He did not ask. What he was thinking about that day was whether he would get back in time for Hao's match.

What he said was: “We've got one of those that burns money too. Every week they delete things. There's a number. Nobody knows who sets it.”

The old man said, “I don't know either.”

They both laughed a little. They were not laughing at the same thing.

VII

It was nearly twelve when Wen left. The old man walked him to the door and said thank you twice.

A mile down the road Wen realised he had never asked the man's name. He thought about it and decided it did not matter. He does ten or so of these in a year and he cannot remember a single name.

That afternoon Hao scored. Wen shouted from the touchline, rather loudly, and the other parents turned round.

The hardware store on South Street closed at seven that evening. The man behind the counter squared up his ticket stubs, the twenty-nine-dollar one on top, and thought about the two men from the morning. The younger one he had known for years; what that one had hold of was this: if he saw somebody pick up the wrong thing, he could not keep from saying so. What the old one had hold of he couldn't make out. He only remembered that from the moment the old man came in to the moment he left he used his right hand, and his left stayed in his pocket the whole time, as if it had hold of something in there too, and had had for years. He turned out the light. He thought the old man was one of the dear ones too.

The old man sat at home that afternoon for a long time. The machine in the basement made no further sound. The house was quiet.

When it was nearly dark he went into the study and pulled open the bottom drawer of the desk. There was one thing in the drawer: a sheet of paper, a photocopy, of the back of a signature page. On the back was one line in handwriting.

He looked at it a while, didn’t take it out, and pushed the drawer shut.¹
