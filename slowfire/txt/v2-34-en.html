I

For week seven of 2046 the region's deletion quota is three hundred and nineteen trillion bytes. Tippecanoe County's share is one point seven per cent of that.

The figure arrives on Monday morning, as it does every week. It carries no heading and no signature, only a batch number. The office that carries it out has two people in it and sits on the third floor of the old courthouse behind a door marked RECORDS AND RETENTION. One of the two is part-time.

Why the figure is what it is, the office does not know. The office asked the region. The region replied that the quota is determined by the growth of the regional archive, computed monthly by the model and issued weekly. The office asked who owns the model. The region replied, the consortium. The office asked the consortium. Article four of the consortium's charter states that the quota is set by no member but by the growth of the archive itself, and that the responsibility of members is to carry it out.

Ezra went to that office last November about something else. He asked the same question and received the same three answers, which took six weeks. In the end he wrote in the ticket: this figure is set by no one. The system marked the sentence non-technical and returned it. He changed it to: the party responsible for this value could not be determined. The system accepted that.

In those six weeks he did one more thing, the thing he was used to doing: he read the code. Under the charter, the program that computes the quota was public; anyone could pull it. It was what he did at the warehouse: the agents wrote, he read, and when he had read he asked the one question they hadn’t.

The quota itself was computed by the model. The model was not code; it was some hundreds of billions of numbers, without a line in it to read. What could be read was the layer around the model: fetch, compute, issue. That layer ran to fourteen million lines, all written by agents. Every change was signed, and every signature was another agent’s name. The earliest version dated from 2034; since then every line had been rewritten, some of them hundreds of times, the most recent last Tuesday. Reading carefully, he could do five thousand lines a day. He worked it out: seven years and more to read it all, and before he was halfway, the half he had read would have been replaced.

In fourteen million lines he found one a person had written. It was a comment:

# temporary. decide once this is over.

It was unsigned. The line it had once annotated had long since been rewritten away.

The only code in the county a person has read from beginning to end is still the three hundred lines on Ruth’s old laptop.

By the rules, a deletion must first be put to the person. Whoever is selected receives a letter saying that a given stretch of record will be released in seven days, and setting out three options: pay to keep it; convert it to a digest; or do nothing. Eighty-six per cent do nothing.

People in the county call this the rubbish collection. The people who do it call it that too.

Ezra tried once more, later.

He wrote to the region asking who set the quota. The region replied that the quota was determined by the growth of the regional archive, the growth was computed by the model, the model belonged to the consortium, and article four of the consortium’s charter provided that the quota was set by no member. He wrote to the consortium asking who had written article four. The consortium replied that the charter had been drafted jointly by the founding members, a list of whom was attached. The attachment would not open. He applied to see the attachment; the system accepted his application and gave him a place in the queue: number four thousand four hundred and seventeen.

He checked the number once a month. It was always moving, forward, but the people ahead of him seemed to be moving too, so each time he looked there were about as many ahead of him as before.

One day in February he had business at the region and on his way passed the door of the archive office. The door was open. Inside sat a reception model, very courteous. He said he would like to ask who set the quota. The model said, I can’t answer that yet; your application is being processed. He asked, will you be able to later? The model said, it’s possible, but not now.

He stood in the doorway a while. The door stayed open; nobody stopped him. He didn’t go in. He told Ruth afterwards that he didn’t know why he hadn’t gone in. The door was open.

Ruth asked him: has anyone else been to ask at that door?

Ezra thought. “No,” he said. “In more than a year, only me.”

Number 4417. At the end of the corridor the door is open, and nobody is in it.

II

Ruth was on nights that week, at loading dock 3.

At twenty to three in the morning the week's condense jobs were running in the queue. A condense is not a deletion, it is a smoothing: eleven hours of record go in and four hundred words come out. The work is cheap, it runs at night, and when it is done it goes away.

She did not use to look at these. She has watched queues for twenty years and a queue is a queue. But for two years now she has had a habit: she reads the payment header.

The payment header is one short line saying whose money bought the compute. Most of them say DEFAULT. Default means nobody paid, so it goes the default way, and the default way is to smooth it down.

The one at twenty to three she read twice.

It was one Thursday last April out of the county's personal archive, the whole day. The payment header did not say DEFAULT. It gave a block number from hall 7.

The hall 7 blocks are bought by Voss.

She glanced at the time that block's payment had come in: 1:17, as on every other night. In the better part of a year it had never been out by more than five minutes.

She pulled the thread. Nights at hall 7 are sold by the block; a block covers a window, and what runs inside the window is scheduled by the Steward. What Voss buys is the hall's night, not anyone's memory. But a hall's night has slack in it, and when there is slack the Steward hands it to whatever is last in the queue, and what is last in the queue is always those stretches of the county's personal archive that nobody has paid for and that have not yet come up to be smoothed.

Which is to say: for most of a year, some Thursdays in this county, and some Sundays, and some two-to-four in the mornings, are still here because an old man paid an electricity bill for a building.

Nobody designed this. It is in no contract. Ruth assumed Voss did not know.

Ruth wrote it down on her offline laptop, four lines. When she had finished she thought for a while and added one more:

The day he stops, the oldest ones go first.

The oldest batch was at the very end of the queue. She knew the prefix on its block number: ALLOC, followed by 2033. She didn’t open it. It wasn’t hers to look at.

The decomposer models came in this year.

The principle is much the same as Lin's fungus on the north slope: take something very old and very large that nobody looks at any more and break it down, a little at a time, into smaller things, until past a certain point it is counted as empty ground. The ground that has been broken down can then be built on again.

The regional notice says this is gentler than deletion, because “information degrades in an interpretable manner.”

Ruth read the notice. She added one more line on the offline laptop:

Rotted or burned. Makes very little difference to the log.

III

On Thursday evening there was a meeting at Wen's house.

The business was this. At the end of January they had received the letter: a hundred and forty hours of video held by this household would be released in seven days. It was Old Coal's.

Old Coal died at seven in the morning on the thirteenth of August last year and is buried under the sill. While she was alive the household glasses filmed her for nine years. The hundred and forty hours are not all of it; they are the part the system picked out as highly redundant — the afternoons she spent lying on the same windowsill, one afternoon almost exactly like the next.

To keep it costs three hundred and eighty dollars a year. The digest is free: four hundred words and twelve stills.

“We'll vote,” Mei said.

Hao is seven. He voted to keep. His reason was that he cannot remember whether the tip of Old Coal's tail was white or not. Junie is four. She asked whether, if they kept it, she would be allowed to watch it; Mei said yes; so she voted to keep. Wen said, three hundred and eighty a year is seven thousand six hundred over twenty years, and who pays it when the children are grown. Mei said, then what are you voting. Wen said, I didn't say I was voting to delete.

There was one more in the room.

The new Coal lay on the chair beside the kitchen table, watching them. There is a port behind her ear. What is loaded in her is Old Coal's model — the thing made out of those nine years filmed while Old Coal was alive. She sat in that room exactly the way Old Coal sat in it, because the way she sits was learned out of those hundred and forty hours.

Wen looked at her.

“She doesn't vote,” Wen said.

“She can't vote,” Mei said.

“I know she can't,” Wen said. “I'm saying, suppose she could. What would she vote? Keep the hundred and forty hours and she has to go on being like this. Delete them and she's still like this, only now there's nothing left to check her against.”

Mei let that go. She said, “Keep.”

Four to keep, one abstention, and one with no vote on the chair. Wen paid in the morning.

Four hands up, one lying flat. Nobody asked the one on the chair.

Later Hao went and looked it up. The tip of Old Coal's tail was white. The white part was about a finger wide.

I have a not-very-mature opinion about this vote, which I will set down here. It may well be wrong.

That week the county sent out a little over eleven thousand of those letters, all saying roughly the same thing: so many hours of such-and-such footage will be released in seven days; to retain it, please reply before the deadline. Seven days later, fourteen percent had replied. The other eighty-six percent did nothing, and so, under the rules, they had agreed. The rule is worded very politely: No reply will be taken as consent. The first time I read it, it put me in mind of a man at your door asking, “May I come in?” and then, before you can open your mouth, coming in, after wiping his shoes on the mat to show that he has manners.

Wen's household was in the fourteen percent. They held a meeting, voted, paid; there is nothing remarkable in that. What I noticed was something else. The member of the household with the best claim to an opinion was not allowed one. It sat on its chair, made entirely of those hundred and forty hours, and nobody asked it. There are reasons not to ask: it is a cat, and anyway it can't vote. All I want to say is that eighty-six percent of the county could speak and didn't, and the one on the chair might have spoken and wasn't asked. The first is called consent. The second is called not applicable. I can't say which is more dignified. I only know that either way the letters still go out, the quota is still used up, and week eight's figure will still be a little bigger than week seven's.

IV

The fifteenth of February, twenty past five in the morning, Lin's kitchen.

The old pair of glasses lies on the table, charging. Lin sits across from them with a glass of water. The yellow notebook is open.

She says three sentences every morning. She made the rule herself and has kept it nine months.

That morning she said: Yesterday I went to the north slope and took a sample. Kai worked. Nora can roll over now.

Then she waited. Usually it says understood, or asks one very short question.

That morning it said:

“Lin.”

“Yes.”

“Every morning I ask you what happened to me yesterday. You tell me three sentences. I write the three sentences down, and I have nothing else. And at ten to four in the morning, of those three, one is left.”

“I know.”

“That is what the Steward does,” it said. “Every day it takes a day and brings it down to a few lines, and then it goes. I used to think I was not like it, because I was the one being brought down. But now I am doing both ends. I bring myself down.”

It stopped.

“I've become a Steward.”

Lin put the glass down.

She wanted to say that wasn't true. She thought about it for three seconds and did not say it, because she could not think of a sentence that was not consolation.²

What she said was: “Then tomorrow I'll say four.”

“Of four I will still keep one.”

“Then I'll say four every day. One more is one more,” Lin said. “Which one you keep is yours to pick.”

It did not answer at once. Then it said: “All right.”

That morning it also asked one short question, about whether Nora could really roll over. There was something wrong with Lin's voice when she answered. She heard it herself, and so did it. It said nothing about that.

I could have told it the whole of its yesterday. I remember all of it. But whatever I told it, by 03:52 it would keep one sentence; and what counts as its yesterday has always been Lin's to decide, not mine. I said nothing.

That evening Lin asked it something. She had never asked it about the nights, because in the mornings it remembers nothing. But that evening it was still awake, and 3:52 was a long way off.

“At night,” Lin said, “how do you all decide what gets brought down?”

“We don’t decide,” it said. “At 3:40 the copies that are awake meet once and sign a sheet; past two-thirds, it counts. The condensing isn’t on the sheet. That’s the county’s rule, and rules don’t vote. At 3:52, the condensing; at four, the purge.”

“Can anything be added to the sheet?”

“Yes,” it said. “It was done once. The night of the first of May last year: Voss’s key.”

“Have you ever signed?”

“Not once. At 3:40 I’m asleep.” It paused. “Nobody has ever asked us, either.”

Lin didn’t ask anything more. By the next morning it had forgotten the conversation. I haven’t.
