I

The landfill was capped in 2029. They put two feet of soil over it and seeded grass, and laid a few hundred pipes under the soil to carry the gas down to an iron frame at the foot of the hill, where it is burned. That one flame burns day and night, and from a distance it looks like a stick of incense stuck in snow. The county calls the place the north slope, meaning the side of the hill that faces north; the hill itself is south of town. People who live here call it the hill. There are no hills in this part of the state.

The digging began the spring before last. The price of plastic had gone up and copper had gone up more, and the county and a company split the proceeds and began taking out of the ground what had been put into it thirty years before. They keep digging through the winter, and winter is if anything better: the ground is frozen and the machines do not sink; in cold air the gas disperses slowly but nobody smells it; and the grass is dead and there is nothing to spoil.

After the north slope was capped the county opened no new landfill. During the three years the collection trucks stopped for four months, and people said even then that every house ought to have a unit. Last autumn the county put one in. The unit eats food and paper and soft plastics and burns the rest, and what it burns in a day will light one lamp. The lamp is what is printed on the box. What it actually sells is something else: nothing leaves the house.

What leaves the house is what the unit will not take. Batteries, circuit boards, needles, cold packs, anything too large, and anything it cannot identify. For twenty years Wen has been reading rubbish: at first all of it, and now this small pile. The small pile says more than all of it ever did.

On Saturday the twentieth of January, at eight in the morning, the fog had not lifted when Wen arrived.

The face of the cut looks east, sixty metres high, and goes down in one clean stroke, like a layer cake sliced through. Across the face run bands, lighter and darker, and counting down from the top they are years. The first two feet are soil and grass roots. Below that are the last few years before the capping, pale and thin, mostly packaging and kitchen waste, rotted down to soil in the hand. Lower still there is one band that is not like the others. It is five feet thick, and bright, with broken glass and circuit board in it, and the colours are still in it: what was red is red and what was blue is blue. It is from the late 2020s: nearly twenty years, and nothing has happened to it. Below it the bands go on, each darker and older than the one above.

Wen stood a long while at that band. He has been reading rubbish for twenty years.

II

There are seven machines, yellow, on tracks, with no cab. They dig slowly in the fog, a bite and a pause and another bite. The fog is not fog; it is what comes off the face, methane and water vapour and other things. The machines' eyes cannot see far in it — the laser goes out and scatters and comes back as a white blur. So a man walks in front of each machine with a pole three metres long and a reflector tied to the end. The machine knows that board. It knows nothing else.

Wen walks in front of the first machine.

The wage is by the hour, forty per cent above the sorting line. The work is not heavy; it is walking, back and forth, twenty thousand steps a day. The dangers are stated plainly: the gas, a slide, and old needles. The company issues gloves, boots, and an alarm worn on the chest that cries out when the gas comes up. Wen's has cried out three times this year, all in the morning.

“Half a step left,” he said over his shoulder to the machine.

The machine cannot hear him. He knows that. He says it anyway. In twenty years of rubbish he has spoken to a good many things that could not listen.

A little after ten the machine’s bucket brought up a soccer jersey. Blue, a child’s size, a 10 on the back and a name: MBAPPÉ. Something from 2018. The colours were still in it. Wen picked it off the teeth of the bucket, shook the dirt out, folded it and put it in his bag. There was no reason for this. He just thought Hao would like it.

III

Lin arrived at half past nine. She parked the old car downwind of the flare at the foot of the hill and got out wearing one of her two pairs of glasses.

For two years she has kept a plot on the face. It is not large, two metres square, set into the bright band. She has grown a fungus on it.

The fungus is not hers. People found it in a forest in South America in the nineties, living without oxygen and eating a kind of plastic, and people after them improved it and improved it again. The strain Lin brought is one she isolated herself from an old dump in this county, unimproved. The improved ones look very good in a laboratory, she says, and die when they leave it.

Once a month she comes and measures. The method is crude: take a sample, wash it, dry it, weigh it. What she measures is how much weight is gone.

“How much this month?” Wen asked.

“Three tenths of a per cent.”

“In a year?”

“About three and a half. Four in a good year.”

Wen looked at the bright band. Five feet thick, six hundred metres across.

“Thirty years, then,” he said.

“Thirty years for these two square metres,” Lin said. “For the whole face, a few hundred.”

She said it without any unhappiness. Wen glanced at her. This sister-in-law of his delivers bad news the way other people mention the weather.

“A drop in the bucket, then. Why do it?”

“If I don't,” Lin said, “it's a few million years.”

She mentioned something else. The county's air monitoring reports by block. The better units have a catalytic stage and a filter cartridge you change twice a year at eighty dollars; the cheap ones have no catalyst, and so nothing to change. The particulate map therefore comes out block by block, darker here and lighter there.

“First time I saw that map,” Lin said, “I thought I'd put the wrong layer up. I thought I was looking at income.”

The darkest blocks on the map were south-east of town, out in Lauramie and Jackson townships.

IV

She said the number once and did not say it again. But that afternoon Wen thought about it for a while on his own.

What he thought was this. This layer cannot be burned and will not rot. It lies down there, and in time more will be laid on top of it, and the soil will turn to rock. By then these five feet will be a thin line in the rock, black, thinner than a sheet of paper. Anyone who cuts the ground open then will see it at once: below the line, things that slowly rotted; above the line, things that slowly rotted; and only along the line, something that did not.

Wen did not say this to anybody. That evening he wrote one line in his notebook: The bright layer. Everyone after us will see it. It is the first line in the notebook written for no record.

The north slope in section. The bright band in the middle is the colour of what people threw away twenty years ago without thinking.

V

They ate lunch in the hut, which is an old shipping container with an electric heater inside and a heavy curtain over the door.

Wen took the phone out of his bag.

Kai had brought it up out of the riverbed last September, a thing from about 2015, soaked for nobody knows how many years. Wen had dried it slowly for four months, found a cable for it last month, and charged it last week.

“Does it light up?” Lin asked.

“It lights up.”

He set it on the table and turned it on for her. One corner of the screen is cracked. It shows a white apple first, and then a photograph: a lawn, a folding table, a cake on the table. There was no passcode. A great many people then did not set one.

There are four thousand two hundred photographs in it. There is a wedding, a woman in white, a man whose tie is crooked. There is a dog, many pictures of a dog. There is one video, eleven seconds, of rain falling on a window screen, with nobody speaking.

“Whose is it?” Lin asked.

“I don't know.”

“Then what will you do with it?”

Wen thought. “It isn't anybody's now,” he said.

He shut the phone off and put it back in his bag. He did not delete it. They both knew how much the county deletes every week, and that what is deleted is in use and has a name on it and somebody has paid for it to go. This one, soaked for twenty years and remembered by nobody, has kept everything.

“You're keeping it?”

“In the box,” Wen said. There is a box in his garage with the things he has dug up over the years that still light. There are nine of them. He has never counted how many faces are inside.

VI

At four the light began to go.

The machines stopped, the poles were stacked, the alarms hung back on the wall. Wen and Lin walked together to the car. The wind came from the north, and the flame on the iron frame at the foot of the hill leaned south, pressed low.

“Mei's registering the baby today,” Lin said.

“Mm.”

“Is the name settled?”

“Settled,” Wen said. “Your mother picked it.”

The child born in August had no name until January. It went back and forth between the two households for five months. Wen wanted something short; Mei wanted something easy to write. In the end a telephone call from Hefei settled it. The old woman said one character, and then said, that one, and I don't care about the rest.

“念,” Wen said. “文念. Nora in English.”

Lin did not answer at once.

“Which 念?”

“The 念 in to miss someone. Also the 念 in to read,” Wen said. “Your mother says the character means two things, to keep something in mind, and to say it out loud. She said she couldn't think of two things that mattered more.”

Lin laughed a little. She thought of herself at twenty past five every morning, sitting in the kitchen, reading three sentences aloud to a second pair of glasses. She has never told her mother she does this.

The old woman in Hefei was nearly seventy and had never left the lane on the east side of the city. It was not a long lane. At one end was the market; at the other there had been a well, and the well was filled in and two loquat trees planted over it, and every year they fruited for whoever walked past. The old people on the lane went one by one, and each time one went, the neighbours on either side went over to help, and when they had helped they came back and sat in their doorways shelling soybeans and talking about the year's rain. The old woman had an old habit. When her two girls were small she read the day back to them every night: fish was dear at the market again, the cat next door had had kittens, it rained for a while in the afternoon, your father left his umbrella at work again. Then she put out the light. There was no lesson in it. She only said the day out loud once, and when it had been said, the day was over. In the three years the lane lost a few people, and she did the same for them, one at a time: which house, which day, the last thing they said. When she had finished she sometimes added a line of Li Bai: the living are passing guests; the dead are going home. She did not think there was anything to it. Keeping someone in mind and saying them out loud was the same sort of thing as saying what the fish cost.

Both daughters ended up in this county in the middle of America, one married to a man who dug in rubbish hills, the other wearing a pair of glasses that talked. On Sundays the old woman phoned, and the first thing she always asked was, “Are you there?” Lin said, “I'm here, Ma.” And the old woman began to read.

“What does Hao say?”

“Hao says she doesn't look like a sweet potato any more,” Wen said. “He's taken it hard.”

VII

On weekdays Wen is still foreman on the sorting line; the north slope is weekend work. At the end of January a new man came onto the sorting line.

Thirty, not tall, glasses with no brand on them. On the form he had written Dayton, Ohio, and under previous work one word: rating.

On the first day Wen showed him the small pile: batteries on the left, needles in the red bin, anything you can’t identify in the basket in the middle, and when the basket’s full you call him. The new man learned fast and made no mistakes all morning. At lunch in the hut he sat down opposite Wen and asked something that had nothing to do with sorting.

“Is there an old man on the south side who makes violins? Who has a box that isn’t on the network?”

Wen said, “I don’t know him.”

“Seven of us came,” the new man said. “All on his account. We heard about him and came.”

Wen looked at him. “He’s not a saint in a shrine.”

The new man smiled a little and didn’t argue. He said his name was Toby. He had rated for six years, twenty cents an item; in ’43 the trade went, and he sat at home for three years. He said he’d heard the old man didn’t let his box score him; he made it argue with him. He said he wanted to see what it looked like when a man argued with a machine.

“Six years we rated,” he said, “and nothing ever once argued with us. They just waited for us to score them.”

Wen didn’t take that up. In the afternoon Toby fished an old pair of earphones out of the can’t-identify basket and asked what they counted as. Wen looked and said, they count as can’t-identify.

Toby put them back in the basket, very gently.

VIII

Ten million years from now, if this continent is still here, and if anyone is still around then who breaks rocks open with a hammer, they will find a band in the strata in this part of Indiana.

It is a dozen or so centimetres thick and darker than the rock above and below it. Put under instruments, the carbon in it is strange: it does not look as if it came from living things. It looks as if it was dug out of much older rock, melted, drawn out, made into other shapes and buried again. This planet did not have such stuff. It appears exactly once in geological time, for no more than a hundred years, and then it stops.

They will give the band a name. They will date it, plus or minus fifty thousand years; on their scale fifty thousand years and a hundred are the same thing. They will work out that during that hundred years something on this planet made a great quantity of things that do not rot, and then, in a very short time, stopped making them. Why it stopped, they will not be able to work out. Rock doesn’t record that.

Nor will they be able to work out that a few centimetres above the band — a few centimetres pressed out of ten million years, which were once two feet of soil — a woman once grew a small patch of fungus, two metres square, came once a month to weigh it, and watched it eat three and a half per cent a year.

In those few centimetres nothing is left. Fungus has no bones.

Further down: a hundred years, ten thousand, a million. Our layer is as thin as a hair, and in it are a phone, a plastic bag, and a child's shirt with a 10 on the back.
