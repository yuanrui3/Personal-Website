Wednesday 18:20 · Ruth's kitchen

Ruth Okafor's laptop was bought in 2031, which made it older than her grandson. Last Friday she took out its wireless card and put it in a cookie tin.

A computer that isn't connected to anything is practically an antique these days. That was the point. No updates, no assistant, nothing that would try to understand anything on her behalf. For nineteen years she had run the night shift at Kestrel Ridge, and every request the Steward made in Lafayette passed through her halls. She couldn't see what was inside a request. She could see the envelope: when it was sent, where from, where to, whose seal was on it. All week, before she clocked out, she copied a few more envelopes onto a thumb drive and took them home.

She wrote a program, three hundred lines. The first line was a comment:

# no model. just counting.

It didn't guess. It didn't reason. It didn't write a nice summary. It did one thing: it put the envelopes in time order and picked out the ones that had no business being next to each other. Ruth figured whatever was clever enough to do all this wouldn't waste its effort fooling a program that could only count.

19:30 · The old Methodist church

Ines's spring recital was set for the following Saturday, so all week her nine students came to the church every evening to rehearse.

Rehearsal wasn't over when Wen got there. He stood at the back and watched Hao clamp a quarter-size violin under his chin and saw away at a tune Wen couldn't identify. Hao played as if the violin owed him money.

Someone else was sitting in the back row. Very tall, tall enough to stand out even sitting down, with broad shoulders and the soft, even glow on his skin you see in ads. On his left wrist was a thin green band with a number: 37. Behind his right ear was a small flesh-colored patch, still a little pink at the edges, as if something minor had just been done there. Wen knew the size of that patch. It matched the button-sized dimple pressed into the foam of the black port-kit boxes that came down his sorting line.

"Theo," Ines called from the front, "would you help me with the music stands?"

The tall man stood up.

Wen didn't think anything at the time. Later he decided it had felt like standing at the cut face with a trowel and hitting something hard: you don't know what it is yet, only that there's something under there.

As soon as rehearsal ended, Hao dragged him over by the hand. "Ba, this is the giant!"

Theo crouched down, and even crouching he was taller than Hao. He winked at Hao, then held out his hand to Wen. "Theo Ross. Your son's the hardest-working kid in this class."

Wen shook it. The hand was steady. Not a tremor.

"Ross," Wen said. "With an R."

"That's right." Theo grinned. "Why, do I owe you money?"

"No."

There was a lot Wen wanted to say. That last Thursday at nine, Theo had stood at a window waiting for a box that never came. That the box was in Wen's refrigerator, in the vegetable drawer behind Mei's yogurt, its strip still blue. That his medicine had never been spoiled; somebody had said it was.

But behind Theo, Ines was standing with an armful of music stands, watching the two of them. Watching Theo. Wen had seen that look before, on Mei's face, years ago.

Wen said nothing. All the way home Hao talked about the giant. Wen pedaled and, in his head, finished the line in his notebook. Theo Ross. He could cross out the question mark.

Ruth didn't mail it. Mail goes by drone now, and the drones belong to the Steward. She drove her old pickup out to Voss's place, charged at noon the day before when power was so cheap it was practically free, and at three in the morning she pulled up outside the iron gate and rang.
