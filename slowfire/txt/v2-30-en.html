One

In 2045 the night was a scarce thing.

Daytime power came from the sky, most of it at noon, so much that it could hardly be given away. Night power was daytime power kept in batteries, at a loss of a tenth or more on every unit, topped up with what could be bought from the next state, and it cost several times the noon price. The county had long since learned to move what it could into the day: the washing, the air conditioning, the hourly rooms. Only a few things couldn't be moved. People had to sleep, and a sleeping body could be rented out, and a rented body had to work at night. And models had to be trained, which took weeks, and couldn't stop at two every afternoon to wait for the sun.

Kestrel Ridge lay twenty kilometres north of town: four cooling towers, twelve halls. Almost nothing in the halls talked over wire any more. Racks spoke to racks in light, and the biggest machines did their multiplying in light as well, at thirteen hundred and ten nanometres, a wavelength to which the human eye and silicon are both blind. Who got that invisible light at night had always been settled by a queue. From 1 November it was settled by auction.

The compute council made the change. EXO-CART's first claim, granted in the spring, ran out on 31 October, and the council voted five to two not to renew it: freight, hospitals and the rest of the old priorities kept their places, and the remaining nights would go to the highest bid in lumens. Voss voted for it. A queue, he said, rewards connections; an auction rewards money; at least you can see money.

In the first week of November three bidders stood above the rest. One wanted to look at a planet, one wanted to teach a pair of hands, one wanted to run a sky. There were not nights enough for all three.

Two

The sky first.

In June 1991 Mount Pinatubo in the Philippines put something like twenty million tonnes of sulphur dioxide into the stratosphere. Up there the sulphur formed a fine haze of sulphuric-acid droplets that sent a little of the sunlight back to space, and for nearly two years the whole world was about half a degree cooler. From this people learned a method, and they learned why the method was dangerous.

The method is simple to state: put something into the sky twenty kilometres up, and let the Earth hand back a little of its sunlight. The danger is as simple: once you start, you cannot casually stop. The greenhouse gases are still there; the heat held off is only entered in a ledger, and if the programme ever stops, the ledger is settled within a few years instead of over decades. This is called termination shock.

In the 2020s someone tried it. A start-up released a few balloons of sulphur dioxide over Mexico and sold “cooling credits”; in January 2023 the Mexican government announced it would ban such experiments. Several American states passed laws, Tennessee in 2024 and Florida in 2025, forbidding anyone to do it in the skies over them. It was a joke then, or a conspiracy theory, depending on whom you asked.

In the summers of 2039 and 2040 several cities in South Asia and around the Gulf grew so hot that people could not shed heat even in the shade. In 2041 eleven tropical and subtropical countries launched Parasol.

Parasol works like this. High-altitude aircraft fly from four latitudes, thirty north, fifteen north, fifteen south and thirty south, and release sulphur dioxide at about twenty kilometres. How much is released each month, and at which latitude, is decided not by people but by a control model. It watches three numbers: the global mean temperature, the difference between the hemispheres, and the difference between the equator and the poles. Whichever number drifts, it moves the latitude that moves it. The design was first written down by a few climate scientists in 2017, when it had only ever run in simulation. Parasol runs its descendant. It makes one decision a month, and before each decision it runs the whole Earth's climate four hundred times.

Four hundred runs take a great many nights. Parasol buys nights all over the world, and Kestrel Ridge sells it some.

In Tippecanoe County, Parasol looks like this. The sky is a shade whiter than it was twenty years ago, its blue less deep. Sunsets are redder, and last longer. The noon light has a veil in it that no one can see, and the solar panels make 1.3 per cent less each day. The sun hour is still the sun hour, only a little thinner. Li Bai, writing of the moon, said the green haze burned away and the clear light came. Over this county there is now a haze that does not burn away, because someone put it there.

Nobody in the county ever voted for this, and nobody ever voted against it; the United States is not one of the eleven. Its supporters say that since 2041 heatwave deaths have halved and the corn belt's harvests have steadied. Its opponents say emissions haven't fallen at all, the debt has only been hung in the sky, and nobody asked the monsoon. Both are telling the truth. How much each of the three numbers counts is set every year at a meeting of the eleven countries' ministers. That table of weights, like the tolerance band on the lumen, is mathematics and politics at once.

Three

Then the hands.

Most of Plinth's night shifts are still driven by licensed remote operators. A person driving a person is the natural fit: one person's movements carry over to another person's body without translation. Letting a model drive a human body on its own is something Plinth has been researching for years, and in 2045 it is still not mature. The mixed match in August was one of its experiments; the model knew where the ball would be, and didn't know how slow a stranger's leg was.

A model learns to drive a body the way it learns anything: from examples. And the examples it needs are the ones seen from inside a body looking out: where the hands are, where the knife is, where the pan is, where the eyes go first and where they go next. Such examples were once rare. Now they are everywhere. In 2045 nearly everyone has glasses on their face or lenses in their eyes, watching their own hands at work from morning till night.

In 2026, in southern India, people strapped phones to their heads and filmed themselves slicing mangoes, folding clothes and making coffee, for two hundred and fifty rupees an hour, sold to firms that gathered data for larger firms. Twenty years later the trade has a different name and is larger by more than anyone can say. Plinth's version is called Show Me. You tap “agree” on your lenses, and while you cook, sweep, fold and lift, the lenses go on watching as they always do; what they see goes to teach the model, and you are paid by the piece.

By law, glasses that save footage must show a red dot. Show Me shows none. Plinth's lawyers say the footage is never saved: each frame goes into the model, the model learns from it, and the frame is thrown away. “Nobody kept it. It was only learned from.” The line was later printed on the flyers of Question 1's opponents and on the flyers of its supporters, meaning opposite things.

This November Plinth means to train a new generation of control model on the two billion hours and more that Show Me gathered from all over the world in October. It wants Kestrel Ridge's nights.

The people at the SAG-4 depot learned of this because the rota for 1 November was forty bays short. The depot's three hundred robots had learned from people: the people moved upstairs behind the glass, and the robots moved with them in the bays below. The operators called it ghosting. Now the robots could learn from the world's glasses, with nobody upstairs moving at all. Forty bays would close first. Kai's didn't. He said, honestly, it was because his hands were quick. No, but I mean, it was because he'd never taken a day off.

Four

And last, the planet.

Proxima b is a little over four light-years away. The thirty-nine-metre telescope in Chile watched it for two years and got one pixel, brightening and dimming every eleven days. From that one pixel EXO-CART draws a map: where the land is, where the sea is, where the clouds are. The map it drew has a continent on it shaped like Africa.

Most of that map is not Proxima b. A light curve fixes only a few numbers: roughly which side is bright, which is dark, and by how much. The map has thousands of cells, the rest are filled in by the prior, and EXO-CART's prior was learned on Earth. It has never seen another planet. All the land it has ever seen is Earth's land.

Calloway knew this. So in October he did something.

In 2038 his solar sail left Earth, swung close to the Sun to gather speed, and headed outward at about twenty-five astronomical units a year, to reach, around 2064, the line six hundred and fifty units out where the Sun itself becomes a lens and magnifies Proxima b a hundred billion times. By October 2045 the sail was about a hundred and ninety units out, a little more than one light-day from Earth. Sunlight there is less than a thirty-thousandth of what falls on Earth; it had long since stopped pushing, and the sail simply coasted along with the craft.

The sail is not a sheet of cloth. It is a programmable metasurface, every small patch of it able to set its own angle of reflection, built for steering near the Sun; once clear of the Sun it doubled as the craft's laser antenna, and had to face Earth at all times. A thing that faces Earth can show Earth to itself.

On 20 October Calloway's engineers sent the sail a new set of parameters. It was flat no longer. Across a kilometre of surface it bowed by about thirty micrometres, into an extremely shallow convex mirror, just enough to spread the light Earth sent it back over a patch the size of Earth. They parked it at right angles to the Sun, so that the sunlight falling on it was thrown back to a point an astronomical unit away, where it troubled no one.

Li Bai said the moon was bright as a flying mirror. In 2045, for the first time, there was a flying mirror in the sky.

Two days later, a little over a day for the light to go out and a little over a day to come back, the telescope in Chile found a new pixel in the direction of the sail. About a hundred photons a second, as faint as twenty-seventh magnitude. It brightened and dimmed once every twenty-four hours: dim when the Pacific turned toward it, bright when the Sahara did. It was Earth. Earth two days ago.

At three in the morning Calloway posted: I turned my ship around to look at us.

The post had 4.1 million likes. How many had a person behind them, this time nobody counted.

The scientists didn't want likes. They wanted a planet whose answer was known. Every day of Earth's clouds and ice and green is on file; give EXO-CART the pixel from two days ago, let it draw, and compare what it draws with the truth. In astronomy this is called calibration. Put plainly, it is looking in a mirror.

Five

Thursday 2 November, eleven at night, Kestrel Ridge.

Ruth Okafor sat at the duty desk in the night-shift control room, reading glasses on their chain, chewing gum. On the screen was the auction: three bidders, a line each, the figures after them jumping every few seconds.

Parasol's agent bid the steadiest, like someone who had to buy. Plinth's bid the hungriest. EXO-CART's agent… Ruth read it twice before she understood: EXO-CART's agent and Plinth's signed with the same key.

Calloway was bidding against himself. In 2039 he had licensed his control firmware to Plinth, and for every hour of every body Plinth drove, he took a share; the more mature Plinth's model, the more bodies it drove, the more he took. EXO-CART was his sail, his map, his planet. Both were his, and both agents did his sums. The sums took forty milliseconds.

The result: of the week's seven nights, Plinth four, Parasol two, EXO-CART one.

Calloway was asleep. His settings said: anything that can be settled in under a second, don't wake me.

Ruth copied the three numbers onto the back of her folder. She copied everything onto paper.

Once the price had been bid up, the county's personal memory could no longer afford hall 7's night. At 23:59 the county's night allowance ran out, and the system sent the request it always sent: who pays. At midnight Voss's key paid. It was the hundred and fourteenth night he had paid that year.

Ruth looked at one more thing. It wasn't on the auction page. It was on the meter.

Plinth's Lafayette night shift was running three hundred and fifty-two bodies tonight. Whether the seat in each held a licensed remote operator or a certified control model, Plinth didn't say, and the people renting out their bodies couldn't see. But the two drew different power. An operator sat somewhere else, and hall 7 only relayed one video stream for him, about what a desk lamp uses. A model sat right here in hall 7's racks, and every body it drove had to be computed in earnest. That showed on the meter.

Ruth didn't use a model. She counted. Of three hundred and fifty-two seats, nineteen were drawing power.

She wrote 19 on the back of the folder.¹

Six

EXO-CART's night was Friday 3 November.

One night was not enough to run the old prior. The old prior was a large model and took seven nights to draw a map. The engineers swapped in a small one, a cheap one, also learned on Earth, only learned less.

On Saturday morning the new map of Proxima b came out. The data hadn't changed: the same one pixel over the same two years. The continent shaped like Africa had moved eleven degrees north.

Nobody found that strange. Only Ruth knew why.

A light curve can't tell north from south. The planet turns, you see it brighten and dim, but whether the bright part sits above the equator or below it, the curve won't say. North and south are set by the prior. The large model had learned more, and had seen that Earth keeps some of its land in the south; the small one had learned less and kept only the big number: two-thirds of Earth's land lies in the northern hemisphere. So it put the continent north. It put it where Earth puts land.

On Friday night Ruth did one more thing. With half an hour of EXO-CART's night left over, she fed it the pixel of Earth from two days before.

First with the Earth prior. The map came out, and it was Earth: Africa, Eurasia, the Pacific, the cloud belts, nothing missing. In the channel the engineers applauded. Ruth didn't. Of course the map was right; the prior had seen Earth. Teach a machine to know a man from his photograph, then test it with his photograph, and of course it knows him.

Then she swapped in a prior that had never seen Earth: one learned on Mars, the Moon, the radar maps of Venus, Titan, and some tens of thousands of computed planets that don't exist.

The map came out again. This time it wasn't Earth. A grey disc, with a bright band running from pole to pole, probably the longitudes of the Sahara and Arabia, and on the far side a dark band, probably the Pacific. Nothing else. No Africa, no Eurasia, not the continent she lived on.

Ruth looked at it for a long time.

Then she counted. A light curve like this, whatever planet it comes from, fixes seven numbers. The map had three thousand and seventy-two cells.

On the back of the folder, under the 19, she wrote: 7 / 3072.

In the channel the engineers asked what she made of it.

“That's what we know about Proxima b,” Ruth said. “The rest is us.”

Seven

Saturday 4 November, evening.

Kai brought home a lunch box. The depot's kitchen bays were testing new home cooking that week, and staff could take home what the tests made. Today's test was Chinese: red-braised pork. The robots had made forty portions in an afternoon.

Lin's rice cooker still had its dead button, so the rice still went in a pot, and still burned. Kai said burnt was good, it was the crust, honestly. Lin tipped the pork into a bowl and tried a piece.

She said nothing, and tried another.

“Like Mum's,” she said. And after a while: “And not.”

Kai said, “No, but I mean, like whose mum?”

Lin thought about it. “Everyone's.”

At seven in the evening Lin video-called Hefei, where it was seven on Sunday morning. Her mother was in the kitchen, glasses on.

“Mum, Kai brought home some braised pork. A robot made it.”

“Is it good?”

“It's like yours.”

Her mother laughed. “Of course it is. I showed them how to do it.”

Lin didn't follow. Her mother spelled it out: Plinth had a thing called Show Me. The red-braised pork she'd learned the year the Olympiad school downstairs became a cooking school; in October she'd tapped “agree” on her glasses and cooked it with them on, twelve yuan a dish, thirty-seven dishes in the month, and every old lady in the building was doing it. “Teaching a machine is a lot less trouble than teaching you two,” she said. “The machine learns it once, and it doesn't need practice to make perfect. You two had more than ten years of me, and one of you still burns rice.”

“So it learned it from you?”

“From me alone? From every old lady in the world. My bit of cooking is a drop in the ocean.” She thought about it. “But you tasted it.”

Lin didn't say anything.

Her mother went on. Yesterday on the news, that sail had turned round to shine back on Earth, the Earth of the day before yesterday. She'd seen the dot on the news, a blue dot, you couldn't make anything out. “Li Bai said nobody now sees the moon of old,” she said. “That's wrong now. People now can see the Earth of the day before yesterday.”

When the call ended, Lin wrote one line in the yellow notebook: Mum's in it.

Eight

Sunday 5 November, two in the morning: every clock in the county went back an hour.

That night was twenty-five hours long. The extra hour comes once a year and was in nobody's contract. The auction rules didn't mention it; the council hadn't thought of it. Under the oldest rule, night power that nobody claims goes first to freight, hospitals and server halls, and among the halls the first in line is the county's personal memory.

So for that one hour the power in hall 7 at Kestrel Ridge went unauctioned and unbid, back to the memories of the county's people. Lin's household, at one third, kept a little more in that hour. For that hour, Voss didn't have to pay. Nobody noticed.

On Sunday morning EXO-CART published the new map of Proxima b. People liked it better than the old one: the continent sat farther from the equator, and the planet looked more like somewhere a person could live.

The continent had moved eleven degrees north. The planet hadn't moved at all.
