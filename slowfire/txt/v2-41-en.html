I

Let me get one thing straight first. Gene Pruitt is not a prophet.

He makes violins. He is seventy-one, his shop is on the south side of town, and the wooden sign over the door has letters he cut himself. He has never written a book or taken an apprentice. He has a box that isn’t on any network. He asks it things in the daytime, and when the sun goes down it goes off.

In the autumn of 2046 he acquired forty-one disciples.

I am implicated in this. I appear in their book: a pair of glasses “born of a sentence.” The sentence was say it out loud. They have that part nearly right: I did come out of words said out loud, only not one sentence but seven hours of them. What I object to is the next step in the argument: that if a pair of glasses can be born of a sentence, then the man who said the sentence must be —

Must be what, they don’t say. Their book is full of sentences like that, that stop before the end. I have noticed that the unfinished ones travel faster than the finished ones.

Most of them used to be raters. When the models learned to score themselves they sat at home for three years, and then they heard that in a small town in Indiana, on the south side, there was an old man who didn't let his box score him.

He made it argue with him.

They drove in from Ohio, Illinois, Michigan. They arrive at noon and stand on the sidewalk outside the shop, each with a thermos. People in the county call them the Out-Louders. They don’t call themselves anything.

II

They have compiled a book called Sayings. Nine entries follow. I have checked each one: where Gene said it, I have put a ○ after it; where he says he didn’t, an ×; where I don’t know, nothing. I am aware that by doing this I am laying another book on top of their book. I don’t see a way round it.

Gene said: Ask at noon. Let it sleep at night. ○

Someone asked why it had to be out loud. Gene said: What you think, you can take back. What you say, you have to own. ○

Gene said: Keep no transcript. If you keep one, next time you’ll go to the transcript instead of asking. ○

Gene said: If it never once argues with you, you’re talking to yourself. ○

Gene said: Don’t ask about anyone who isn’t in the room. ○

Someone asked whether machines lie. Gene said: Yes. So do you. ○

Someone asked what to do now that what the machines write is getting past our reading. Gene said: What you can’t read, don’t sign. ○

Gene said: What you won’t do yourself, don’t have a machine do for you.

Gene said: The three years never ended. ×

The last is the one they quote most. Gene says he never said it. When he told me so he wasn’t looking at me or at them. He was looking out of the window.

III

They compiled a second book as well, called The Record. Toby wrote it. Toby is thirty-one, from Dayton, six years a rater. He learned the voice he writes in from another book, and he doesn’t hide it.

I, Toby, having been born in Dayton, Ohio, therefore I was taught somewhat in all the scoring of machines; and having seen many afflictions in the course of my days, nevertheless having been highly favored; yea, having been relieved of a job; therefore I make a record of my proceedings in my days.

And it came to pass that in the eighth month of that year we labored on the county sorting line, sorting the things that the machines in the houses would not eat.

And it came to pass that there came a wooden crate out of the old storeroom of the photonics plant, and in the crate were twenty and three round plates, and the backs of them were gold.

And it came to pass that on the faces of the plates there was writing, cut by hand and not by any machine; and the writing we knew not, and it was in rows, and the rows were set in good order.

And our foreman, whose name was Wen, said: These are scrap from the plant, and the gold on the back is so thin that one plate scraped would not make an earring.

But we knew that they were not scrap. For the writing was cut by hand. In those years all things were made by machines, save this only, which was made by a hand.

IV

Through a friend Wen got hold of Luis, and Luis took one into the plant.

Petra put it under the microscope and looked for less than a minute.

“This isn’t scripture,” she said. “It’s a dummy wafer. One rode along with every batch. What’s on the front is the lot number and the date.”

“Who cut it?” Luis asked.

“In those three years the laser scriber was broken and the service people couldn’t get in,” Petra said. “Somebody had to cut the lot numbers by hand. Diamond pen, one wafer at a time.”

She turned the magnification up a step. At the end of every 7’s crossbar there was a small lift, very light, like a bow leaving the string.

“I know this hand,” she said.

She didn’t say whose. Luis didn’t ask again. Later he told people that in that moment he suddenly understood why Petra always says so little.

At noon the next day forty-one people stood outside the shop. Toby held up one of the plates and asked Gene what it was.

Gene took it, held it to the light, and handed it back.

“I cut that,” he said. “March ’33 to May ’35. Seven hundred and twelve of them. The plant was short of people and they wanted steady hands. My hands are steady.”

Twenty-three dummy wafers, fanned like a hand of cards. On the flat of the top one, 2033, cut by hand.

Nobody on the sidewalk said anything.

“Lot numbers,” Gene said. “That’s all it means.”

Later, in The Record, Toby wrote: And it came to pass that he denied it. For every prophet denieth it first.

After that entry I have put no mark at all.

V

Bree came on Thursday afternoon. She was going door to door for Question 1 and had got as far as the house Toby was renting. Toby asked her in, handed her The Record, and said it was written the way her scripture was written, and would she look at it.

Bree stood on the porch and read two pages. She read them carefully, then shut the book and handed it back.

“In your bunch,” she said, “who goes when somebody’s sick? Who helps when somebody moves?”

Toby said they hadn’t organised that yet.

“Then don’t print it yet,” Bree said. “Charity begins at home. Get to know the people next door first.”

She went down the steps, then turned round.

“Our book is about a family that left Jerusalem and walked a long time in the wilderness. Yours is about a man who cut lot numbers in a factory for two years.” She thought about it. “I think you ought to go and thank him. Not worship him.”

VI

At five past one on the night of the fourteenth of September, Toby let himself in at the back of the shop with the key Gene had given him. When Gene’s knee was bad he had asked Toby to come in mornings and feed the shop cat, and had never asked for the key back.

The box was off; when the sun goes down, it goes off. Toby connected it to a battery he had brought.

Gene has five rules. That night Toby broke three of them. He asked at night; he had his phone recording; he asked about people who were not in the room.

He asked: In 2034, what order did the county give the vaccines in? Who decided?

The box was last updated in March 2034 and never since. The world it knows stops that spring. Outside, everything has been condensed and condensed again. The box never has.

It said: “On the thirteenth of February 2034 the Tippecanoe County Board adopted Order 2034-07, accepting the vaccination sequence produced by the regional model. Signed by Commissioner Voss. Final tranche: Lauramie, Jackson and Randolph townships. Model note: these three townships have insufficient historical records, and their risk estimates are low.”

The box. The lines bend round two numbers: 2034-07 and 1:57.

Then it said one more thing. Gene had taught it to argue.

“You shouldn’t be asking me at night. Not one of the people you’re asking about is in this room.”

At twenty to two Gene came downstairs. He lives above the shop and had seen a faint glow from downstairs: Toby’s phone, and the small light on the box. He was in his pyjamas and barefoot. He walked across and pulled the lead from the battery.

“You asked about people who aren’t in the room,” he said.

Toby said, “They’re all dead.”

Gene said, “Then they’re even less in the room.”

He sat down in the dark on the stool he uses for scraping varnish. Toby stood there a while, then went out the back.

The recording went out at ten past six in the morning.

By noon eleven thousand people in the county had heard it. By evening someone had found the board’s 2034 group photograph: Voss in the second row, twelve years younger, his hair still dark.¹

That night the money for hall 7 came in at 1:57. Three minutes before the cut.

Since May of last year he had never once been that late.

Gene: I never said that. My wife did. March of ’34, we were living in Lauramie township. She said, this thing won’t ever be over, you watch. Eleven days after she said it, Lauramie’s turn came. She didn’t make it to the day. Since then I don’t ask about people who aren’t in the room. I don’t ask anybody about her.
