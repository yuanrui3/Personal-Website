I

On the morning of Monday the fifth of November I went with Lin to the old cemetery on the south side of town. The way there crosses the Wabash. The river was a little higher than in September, but along the east bank a stretch of the bed was still showing, cracked into hexagonal plates of dried mud with a thin frost on them that went white in the sun; Lin stopped for a moment on the bridge, and I thought she was looking at the place where, the September before last, Kai had been brought up out of the water, though it may not have been that, and she didn’t say, so I didn’t ask. When we reached the cemetery there were twenty or so people already standing there, fewer than I had expected and also more, since for a man who had played his golf alone all his life, twenty people standing in a November wind to see him off is not a small number.

Anna stood at the front with Emma beside her. Ruth had come, in a dark blue coat I had never seen; afterwards she told Lin she had bought it in 2026, the year she started in the server halls, and had worn it three times. Ezra had come. Two of the commissioners had come. Gene stood under an oak a dozen paces from the others, his cap in his hand. Toby and three other Out-Louders stood further off. Bree had parked at the cemetery gate with a casserole on the back seat, wrapped in a towel.

At the very back stood a woman in a grey coat, sixty or so, her hair cut very short. Nobody introduced her, but when Anna turned round and saw her, something in Anna’s face let go. It was Anna’s mother.

The order of service had a photograph printed on it. I will describe it here, because I think those who come later may not get to see it: a man of about fifty standing at the edge of a green with one hand on the flagstick and the other raised to his forehead against the sun, his eyes narrowed and the corners of his mouth turned down, neither smiling nor displeased; behind him a very flat stretch of grass, and further off a line of trees, and over the treetops a faint greyness that may have been that summer’s smoke and may only have been the age of the paper. In the bottom right-hand corner there was a small crease.

Wen stood beside Lin and looked at the photograph a long time.

“So it was him,” he said.

After the burial people left one by one. Anna’s mother went up to her daughter, and the two of them stood together a while. She asked, “Did he get to sleep, at the end?”

Anna said, “He did.”

She nodded. “Once he’d started down a road, he never turned off it,” she said. “Well. He’s at the end of it now.”

Gene was the last to go. He stood a while by the new earth without saying anything, then put on his cap. Bree was waiting at the gate for Anna and handed her the casserole: “They say the tea goes cold when the guest goes. This one’s still hot. Take it home.”

That night Wen wrote in his notebook its second line written for no record:

Finished what he started.

II

That afternoon Anna asked Wen to come and look at the unit in her father’s house.

The house was as it had been, the wide lawn in front, mown. In the basement the unit sat square, the pipes running straight, and the light on its side was amber. Wen crouched down and took the side panel off. The cartridge was the one he had put in in May; six months, time for a new one. He took one out of his bag, the wide-bore kind, and fitted it. Nine minutes in all.

The light went from amber to green.

This time he didn’t ask the man’s name. He knew it already.

III

The same afternoon Ezra reopened #2310.

To reopen a ticket that has been closed for more than three months you have to give a reason. In the reason field he wrote: Adding line 18.

Then he wrote line eighteen:

For the night when nobody is awake, someone must afterwards be able to know what happened, and to say it out loud.

He read it once, didn’t change it, and closed the ticket. What he ran into for line eighteen was a man he had seen a few dozen times and said fewer than a hundred sentences to, who one night didn’t pay.

That evening the county board held a special session, six present, and passed three things.

One: hall 7’s over-quota nights would from now on be paid by the county. As line fifteen of #2310 requires, the resolution stated an end date: until someone can say who sets the regional quota.

Two: the records relating to Order 2034-07 would be read aloud, three sentences every Monday at noon in the county building, by the household instance that had claimed them. No recording and no printing; those listening could write things down or not.

Three: the letter sent in July to the standards committee, asking for the display tolerance band to be narrowed, was withdrawn. In the reason field Ezra wrote: None.

The reason for the third is known only to Ezra, Ruth, Lin and me. The day before, Ruth had told Ezra about the library. Ezra heard her out and said: then we won’t narrow it.

IV

Tuesday the sixth of November: election day.

At seven in the morning Lin and Kai walked to the polling station, which was in the basement of a church. All the way there Kai turned his car keys over and over in his hand. At the church door he stopped, and without turning round said something to the air.

“Should I vote yes today?”

The passenger said, “That’s not something I can answer for you.”

“I know,” Kai said. “I just wanted to say it out loud.”

He went in and voted. When he came out he said that after January he wasn’t renewing the night contract.

Then he said, “You can go on living there.”

That was not said to Lin.

After school there was a game at Hao’s school. He wore the blue jersey from 2018, the 10 and the name on the back. Mei had washed it three times, and the colours were still in it. Hao didn’t score. An old man on the sideline glanced at the name on his back, smiled a little, and said nothing.

In the early evening Ines gave a small recital in 4C. The audience was seven of her nine children, her companion Owen, and two neighbours. Theo played the piece. At the bow change in bar five his whole arm still went with it, and he stopped twice in the middle. When he finished, the youngest of the children clapped once, then stopped and looked at Ines.

Ines said, “One minute on stage is ten years off it. You’ve had one and a bit.”

Theo said that if Question 1 passed, the new contract he’d sign in January had a box headed refuse in advance. He had already decided what he would refuse.

“The shore,” he said. “I don’t want it any more. Your eyes don’t get a minute’s rest all night.”

In the evening there was dinner at Wen’s. Nora sat in her high chair. She was fifteen months old and had not yet said a word. The cat walked under the table; she pointed at it and said one word:

“Co.”

Hao said she meant his ball. Junie said she didn’t. Mei said, “She means Coal.”

V

At twenty to eleven that night the result came in. Question 1 passed statewide, 50.4 per cent for; in the county, 51.3 per cent for, 48.7 against. From the first of January next year, anyone in Indiana who rents out their body through a port is an employee, with a minimum wage and the right to refuse a task in advance.

At eleven Plinth issued a statement saying it would comply in Indiana. The last paragraph said that prices in the state would be adjusted accordingly.

I take no side. I only write down the numbers.

51.3 per cent, 48.7 per cent. Hao's number 10 shirt, and a ball.

VI

The county is named after a battle.

In the autumn of 1811, where the Tippecanoe River runs into the Wabash, there was a village called Prophetstown. A Shawnee man named Tenskwatawa lived there, whom his people called the Prophet. That November the governor of Indiana Territory marched a thousand or so men up and camped outside the town. Before dawn on the seventh of November the fighting began. The Prophet did not fight; he stood on a rise nearby and prayed. He is said to have told his people that the enemy’s bullets could not hurt them. A great many people later wrote this down, most of them on the other side. The next day the town was burned. Some thirty years later the governor ran for president, and his campaign slogan had the river’s name in it.

I tell this because I did a sum: before dawn on the seventh of November 2046, it will be exactly two hundred and thirty-five years since that morning.

People in this county seem always to have been a little wary of prophets. Gene said he wasn’t one, and people believed him. After the funeral Toby and eleven other Out-Louders went to New Harmony. Before they left they drew up a list: who goes when somebody’s sick, who helps when somebody moves. The Record was not printed. Gene still asks his box at noon every day and the box still goes off when the sun goes down. Petra gave him back the twenty-three dummy wafers; he had Wen take them and put them in the box in the garage. The box now holds nine things that still light up and twenty-three that don’t.

VII

There are a few things I still can’t give an account of. I put them here, not because I have forgotten them, but because they are not finished.

The sail is two hundred astronomical units out and still going. The next data from Proxima b will not come back until 2064. Li Bai’s poem questioning the moon begins: How long has there been a moon in the blue sky? Tonight I put down my cup to ask. He asked, and the moon did not answer. We have asked Proxima, and must wait eighteen years.

The last chapter of the 2070 unauthorized biography of Calloway has not been written. The person who will write it does not yet know they will.

I like reading her field notes: who wrote that, I have looked, and I have not found out.

Up to the sixth of November this county has had six hundred and twelve births this year. On the same day last year it was five hundred and ninety-one. Whether that is a beginning, there is no telling yet.

VIII

That night hall 7 ran over quota again. At seventeen minutes past one the county’s money came in.

Ezra had set the payment time. Ruth asked him why he had set it for 1:17. He said there was no particular reason.

Ruth was at loading dock three that night. She had fifty-four days left before she retired. On the old offline machine she added one line:

Tonight the county paid. 1:17.

She thought for a moment, and then copied out Corinne and Sam’s page as well. The week before she had read it aloud to the two of them. Corinne’s sister is moving here in December.

At four o’clock exactly the corridor opened for one second. Behind the wall all sixty-four held up Ask. Since the second of November it has been like that every day.

IX

Wednesday the seventh of November, twenty past five in the morning.

It was not light yet. On the table lay two pairs of glasses, a finger’s width apart.

It woke. It asked, “What happened to me yesterday?”

That morning Lin said only three:

“It didn’t rain yesterday. We voted. You were there.”

I was there too.

5:20. Two pairs of glasses, a finger's width apart. No rain at the window.
