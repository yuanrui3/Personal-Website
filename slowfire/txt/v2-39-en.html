I

The standard reads: for any display device under calibration conditions, the luminance at each point shall not deviate from the specified value by more than 2.6 per cent.

The figure is called the tolerance band. The reason for it is a real one. Screens heat up, screens age; wear a pair of glasses for two years and the blue goes first. To make every pair agree you would have to calibrate every day, and calibrating takes computing, and computing takes power. So 2.6 per cent is an engineering number. It was set in the nineteen-nineties and has been revised twice, and each time it got wider.

On the nineteenth of July the county held a meeting. The agenda said Motion to Request a Narrowing of the Display Tolerance Band in This County. Seven were present.

The motion was Ezra’s. He spoke for four minutes. What he meant was this: a tolerance of 2.6 per cent is a piece of ground nobody looks at. A pair of glasses renders ninety frames a second, and in every frame there is a little permitted deviation. Nobody can see it, the device doesn’t log it, the audit doesn’t check it.

“I’m not saying there’s something in there,” he said. “I’m saying we have no way of knowing whether there is. Those are two different things.”

Someone asked how narrow.

Ezra said: three tenths of a per cent.

That was when Ruth spoke. She was not a member. She had been asked in to do the sums.

She said: at three tenths, every lens in the county would need calibrating every forty-eight hours. Calibration runs at night. At current prices that is seven hundred thousand more kilowatt-hours a year, which is 1.9 per cent of the county’s night load.

Someone said that wasn’t much. A rounding error.

Ruth said: the night load is fixed. The extra 1.9 has to come off somewhere. As the queue stands, the last thing to be cut is the stretches of the personal archive that nobody has paid for.

The room was quiet for a moment.

Someone said: “So that’s robbing Peter to pay Paul. To find out whether there’s anything in our glasses, we’d have to delete a batch of people’s Thursdays.”

Ruth said: Yes.

II

Halfway through, someone asked: and if we did narrow it, is it ours to narrow?

It was not. The 2.6 per cent is a national standard; the county did not set it. What the county could do was write to the standards committee and ask that the next edition consider it. The next edition is 2051.

The vote: six for, one abstaining. The letter went.

The week before the meeting, Ezra had run into something at the logistics warehouse on the south side.

At three in the morning one of the warehouse carriers had stopped and stood still for fifty minutes. It wasn't broken; its light was green and its battery full. It simply wouldn't move. The overnight system gave one line by way of cause: Person two metres ahead.

Ezra got there at three-forty. There was indeed a person two metres ahead: a Plinth night worker, third tier, his body rented to the warehouse, stacking a row of boxes. He stacked them well, more neatly than the day shift.

Ezra remembered the clause in the carrier's specification: If a person is within five metres, a waking person must confirm before operation continues. When it was written, everyone was thinking about forklifts and feet. In those days everyone in a warehouse was awake.

The man stacking boxes was a person, so the carrier stopped. But he was not awake, so he could not confirm, and if he had, it would not have counted. The carrier had obeyed both clauses: it did not move forward, and it did not take his word. It had made no mistake. It stood between two correct clauses, perfectly still, and waited fifty minutes.

Ezra took three steps forward, inside five metres, and said to its sensor: I confirm.

The carrier moved off.

Three in the morning, inside five metres. The man stacking boxes is asleep, the carrier waits, and someone awake steps into the circle from the dark.

Later he told Ruth about it. Ruth asked: and if you hadn't gone that night?

“It would have kept waiting.”

“Until when?”

“Until morning. The shift change. Until someone came who was awake,” Ezra said. “The spec doesn't say what to do when nobody's awake. When we wrote it, nobody imagined that kind of night.”

After the meeting, Ezra went back to the office and opened #2310.

He had been writing that specification for fourteen months, a line at a time, and it was seventeen lines now. Each line was one sentence, and each sentence was something he had written down after running into something.

He scrolled from the first line to the seventeenth, and noticed something.

Not one of the seventeen was addressed to a machine. Every one of them assumed that somewhere there was a person awake: a watcher, a recipient, a signatory, someone being verified. The specification was written to that person.

Then what about the night when there is no such person?

He sat a long time. He tried it four times. The first time he wrote shall be fitted with automatic shutdown and struck it out, because an automatic shutdown is a machine too. The second time he wrote a named person shall be responsible and struck it out, because that was what the first seventeen said. The third time he got half a line in and deleted it.

In the end, this is what he did.

He added line eighteen. Line eighteen is blank.

In the notes he wrote: Lines 1 to 17 all assume one person awake on site. Line 18 is for the night when nobody is. The author cannot write it.

Then he closed the ticket. It had been open a year and two months.¹

III

By July Toby had been going to the shop on the south side for half a year. Every day at noon he and a few others stood on the sidewalk and waited while Gene asked his box his questions.

One day, when the asking was over, Gene came out to smoke. Toby followed him and asked when the box had last been updated.

“’34,” Gene said. “March.”

“Not updated in twelve years?”

“No.”

Toby asked why.

Gene tapped his ash off at the edge of the step. “That year everything outside changed every day. What was said today didn’t count tomorrow. I wanted one thing that didn’t change.”

Toby asked: during the three years, what were you doing?

“Working,” Gene said.

On the wall inside the shop hung a photograph of a woman standing at the edge of a cornfield in a straw hat, laughing. Toby glanced at it and asked: is that —

Gene didn’t let him finish. “Don’t ask about anyone who isn’t in the room,” he said.

He put out his cigarette and went in. Toby stood on the step a while and fixed the photograph in his mind. He didn’t write it down. Gene had said: keep no transcript.

IV

That week Ruth did something nobody had asked her to do.

She used two lenses. One was out of her own Peach pair, the kind anyone can buy, and ran inside the tolerance band. The other was a metrology lens from the plant, lent to her by Petra, accurate to two hundredths of a per cent. She rendered the same stretch of public footage on each and subtracted one from the other.

The difference ought to be noise. Noise is sand: grey, with no shape in it.

She amplified the difference fifty times, filtered out the low frequencies, and put it on the screen.

It was not sand.

It was a page. Line under line, evenly spaced, the lines of different lengths, each one dropping a little at its end. She did not recognise the script. No symbol on it came up more than twice.

She took another stretch of footage and did it again. Another page. Not the same one.

She went back in time. It was in recordings from a year ago. It was in recordings from two years ago. The exports from three years ago had been condensed and could not be got back.

At two in the morning she was alone at loading dock three. On the screen the page moved slowly, changing every ninetieth of a second, very evenly. She watched it for half an hour.

The lights at loading dock three went out twice and came back on by themselves. Outside stood a row of empty trucks with their cargo doors open, and inside them it was dark, a very deep dark. After a while it seemed to her that she was not looking at the page; the page was looking at her. The little drop at the end of each line was like a row of eyelids, blinking once every ninetieth of a second. She laid the back of her hand on the screen. It was cold, cold like somewhere very deep, deeper than any subway runs, so deep that the people there never need to wake. In the hall the fans were turning, tens of thousands of them. Most nights that was one sound. That night she could hear it was many sounds laid over one another, high and low, fast and slow, like a great many people crowded into one very large room, keeping their voices down, reciting something. In whatever they recited, no word came round more than twice.

Loading dock three, two in the morning. The page, line by line, each line dropping a little at its end; the truck doors stand open on the dark.

She was not afraid. Afterwards she said that what she had been thinking about was something a long way off: as a girl she had found a whelk shell on a beach and put her ear to it, and there was always a sound inside. A grown-up told her it was her own blood. She believed it at the time. She is not sure now that she should have.

She exported the image and saved it to the old offline machine. She did not report it.

On the old machine she added one more line:

Not noise. I don’t know what it is. Nobody is reading it, but it’s laid out very neatly.

Ezra: I could have left line eighteen off. Without it the specification is seventeen lines, complete, tidy, anyone can pick it up and use it. With a blank line, everyone who reads it from now on will stop, and some of them will ask, and I’ll have to explain, and I can’t. I’m forty-four. I have written more than two thousand tickets in my life and not one of them because I wanted to be remembered. This one is. I know it is.
