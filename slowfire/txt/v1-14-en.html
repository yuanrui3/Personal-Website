From Weather: An Unauthorized Life of Rex Calloway (2070)

Chapter 11 · Ten Days in April

In the spring of 2045, Rex Calloway was probably the most watched man in the world. His heart rate, how many hours he had slept the night before, which floor of which building he was standing on: something was watching all of it, every second. Most of what watched him was not human. It is hard to think of anyone else in history who was looked at so much and understood so little. I have never met him. Not many people have.

Readers who have come this far know the facts of his life. Readers who have skipped ahead can have them in a minute:

1971. Born in Bakersfield, California. His father flew a crop duster back and forth over the cotton. At nine he rode in the back seat for the first time and was sick the whole way. At ten he asked to go again.

1993. Left college after three semesters. 2001. Sold his first company, which wrote software for routing freight. Everything he has done since has been, in the end, a business of getting things somewhere else.

2012. His rocket reached orbit on the third try. 2031 and 2033. Two uncrewed landers set down on Mars. Both are still there, and every Martian morning they send home a weather report. Almost everything on Earth that reads those reports is an agent. The same is true of his posts.

2015. He began posting at three in the morning, and has not stopped. That year he met a young man named Voss for the first time, across a courtroom, over a piece of land in Nevada. The two of them fought for the next thirty years.

2029. He began publishing his sleep score every morning, the way other people post their weight.¹

2032. His first humanoid robots went on sale. By 2045 they were folding towels in four million homes and moving boxes in far more warehouses. They saw with cameras and lidar. In the summer of 2044, when peatland in Manitoba burned for more than a month and the smoke sat over the Midwest for weeks, they could not see at all, and the warehouses began renting people by the hour.

2034. He and Voss appeared together on the cover of a business magazine under the headline THE TWO MEN WHO OWN TOMORROW.² That year Voss's company passed a trillion dollars. Voss's marriage did not make it to the end of the year.

2035. He financed a studio that made vertical dramas with no actors and no cameras. Every episode was generated in several thousand versions at once, and whichever version held a viewer half a second longer was the one that stayed. Ten years later, the faces Americans watched most had come to look more and more like one face. Nobody designed that face, so nobody owned it: a thing picked out that way had no author in law, and anyone could use it without paying a cent. A real face was expensive. In Indiana, a person's face stayed theirs for a hundred years after they died. Calloway himself has watched exactly one episode, in 2041, at four times normal speed. "A bit long," he said.

2038. He launched a solar sail toward a point where there is nothing at all: on the far side of the Sun from Proxima Centauri, 650 times as far from the Sun as Earth is. Out there, the Sun's gravity bends the light of the planet Proxima b into focus. At the sail's speed it would arrive around 2064.³ While he waited, he paid the machines at Kestrel Ridge to guess. The 39-meter telescope in Chile had watched Proxima b for two years and seen a single pixel that brightened and dimmed every eleven days. A computing job called EXO-CART was working backward from that one pixel to a map of the whole planet. In the spring of 2045, that job had first claim on Kestrel's night power, ahead of the county's personal memory.

2039. He licensed his robots' control firmware to Plinth. Plinth's business was renting out bodies: you had a port put in behind your ear and rented your body by the hour to an operator or an agent somewhere else, who drove it. His view was that "a body is a robot with better hands." The firmware went over unchanged, along with a rule that had kept robots safe for decades without a single failure: if the link drops, stop.

2041. He announced "the button." Three years of animal trials followed.

April 2045. He was seventy-four.

The title of this book comes from a man on the sorting line at the Tippecanoe County recovery plant. He stood beside that belt for more than twenty years, watching everything the county threw away go past, and asked to be named here only by his first name, Wen. "I never saw one thing of his," Wen told me in 2066. "He doesn't sell his stuff. He leases it, and when it breaks they take it back. He doesn't even leave trash here." I asked him what Calloway was to him. He thought about it. "Weather, I guess. Way off somewhere, and it lands on you."

⁂

Monday, 10 April

Four nights earlier, at three in the morning, he had posted a photograph: a white room, a reclining chair, and the button on a steel tray. On the morning of the operation he was not in Indianapolis. He was on the top level of a launch tower in south Texas. A tanker flight, one of the refueling runs to orbit, had been set for that morning and was scrubbed for wind. He watched the surgery live on his phone up there, with the wind pressing his jacket flat against his back. At 10:41 he posted:

First human port. Success. 🧠🚀 These people are heroes.

The photo showed a white operating room and six people standing in a row in masks and caps, only their eyes showing.

It was not the first human port.⁴ By April 2045 more than four hundred thousand Americans already wore a port behind the ear, most of them for work: the port reads what a person means to do with their hands and passes it to a machine somewhere else. Calloway's button looked exactly like theirs. The difference was inside. It didn't only read out; it could write in. The patient was a forty-four-year-old former roofer from Muncie who had fallen from a roof in 2041 and lost all feeling below the neck. He asked not to be named. At 11:02 he moved a cursor on a screen by thinking about it. At 2:10 that afternoon a nurse put a cup of coffee into the robot hand he was steering with his thoughts. Sensors in its fingertips passed the warmth to the button, and the button wrote it into his brain. He said he could feel that the cup was hot. It was the first time in four years.

10 April, 10:41. The launch was scrubbed. The surgery wasn't.

Second from the left was Anna. She told me that on the Friday before, her father had called her, for the first time in eleven years. "He asked if Monday was going to go all right. I said I thought so. At ten that morning he withdrew his motion." The button trial's models ran at Kestrel Ridge in Indiana, on computing leased from Voss. His motion would have taken back thirty percent of it, and the trial would have slipped by months. Calloway's company never learned why he withdrew it. "I've never told them," she said. "I suppose I'm telling you."⁵

The post had three million likes within an hour.⁶ At 10:47 someone launched a coin called HEROES. It was up forty percent by the next morning and worthless by Friday.⁷

Tuesday 11 to Sunday 16 April

Over those six nights he posted forty-one times between three and four in the morning: rockets, a brain wearing sunglasses, and a poll, "Should sleep be optional?" When the poll closed, sixty-one percent had voted yes.

Monday, 17 April

That day in Lafayette, Indiana, a vision engineer named Ezra opened a ticket in his depot's issue tracker. It was number 2291:

Renter body, control link lost mid-task: what does it do?

The ticket went up the chain the way tickets do: from the depot to Plinth, and from Plinth to the firmware vendor, which was Calloway's robotics company. At the time Ezra had eleven followers, four of them human.

Tuesday, 18 April

He flew from Austin to Detroit to look at a robot plant. The route crossed Tippecanoe County at thirty-one thousand feet. He took a picture through the window and posted it with two words: "Flyover country 😴."

The photo has been enlarged since, more than once, mostly by agents. Enlarged, it shows Tippecanoe County in the middle of April. The corn and soybean fields are not planted yet; between wide stretches of turned black soil run strips of green, the cereal rye sown the autumn before to hold the soil, already knee-high. The woods along the river have gone green, and in the Aprils the previous generation remembered they would still have been gray. The Wabash is high with spring water, the color of coffee with milk in it. To the west, Benton County's wind turbines stand in rows along the horizon. To the north a yellow-brown band lies along the sky: the Manitoba smoke. Farther off is a large field of white rectangular roofs covered in solar panels, which is Kestrel Ridge. Farther southeast, among a stretch of long gray roofs, is the depot where Ezra worked. The photo doesn't show which one.

Wednesday, 19 April

At 3:12 a.m. he posted: "You sleep. Your body goes to work. The future of work is a nap." Plinth's first regular night shift in Lafayette was set for the night of 30 April: you sleep, your body works. That day the sign-ups went from four hundred and twelve to five hundred and eighty-eight.

That afternoon the firmware team gave ticket 2291 a status: Won't fix before 05/01. Nobody was being lazy. The release going live on the first of May had been frozen, and a frozen release isn't changed. That, too, is a rule that had worked for decades without a single failure.

Thursday, 20 April

At three in the morning his digest was ready, as it was every morning. His companies, the things he cared about, everything that touched him: every night agents pressed all of it into one page and put it on the first screen of his phone.⁸ Ticket 2291 was in there, pressed down to four words: Edge case, depot, Indiana.

He did not open the digest. Nobody had expected him to. The digest was made so that it could go unopened.

At 10:41, ten days to the minute after the first post, he posted: "Ten days. Zero complications. He felt the cup." That one had four million likes within an hour.

⁂

On the night of 30 April 2045 he slept nine hours.

The morning before, he had slipped in the shower and bruised his hip badly, and he had posted a photograph of the bruise. His doctor prescribed a sleeping pill, and he took one for the first time in his life.⁹ He was seventy-four. The body he lived in was not one of his products, and like other people's bodies, it had started to do less of what it was told.

1 May, 3 a.m. The phone kept lighting up. He kept sleeping.

It was the only night that spring he did not post, and the one night his posts would have been read.¹⁰

What he posted at 9:12 the next morning is a matter of public record, and of a later chapter.¹¹

So if a post of his said something wrong, he answered for it; if it said something well, the credit went to no one. His lawyers fought two suits to make the posts his. All they secured for him in the end was the order his posts came in.
