From the editors

The newspaper in the county where much of this issue was written has had no human staff since 2051. This magazine is still put together by students, for a plain reason: learning to do it is the point.

Since the Voss clause of 2047, decisions in that county carry the name of a person who was awake. A signer can refuse, and when one does, the decision stops until somebody signs. We have borrowed the habit. Each feature ends with a line saying which numbers the author worked out again independently, and the author's name is on that line.

All three features come out of one autumn. On 14 September 2064 a sail launched in 2038 reached the focal line of the Sun, 650 astronomical units out. On 27 September the first picture of Proxima b was published. On 6 October a retired technician found a period in it. Our authors were asked the same question about each: what part of this would still be there if you changed the method?

Three priors, one planet

How to read a picture that is three-quarters inference

Amara Nwosu, Elmore Family School of Electrical and Computer Engineering, Purdue University. The author is a volunteer with Census·Proxima and ran the rock prior on a household inference box for eleven nights in October.

The first image of Proxima b has 4,096 cells, 64 by 64, each about 200 km across. The foundation's own table says 1,021 of them were measured and 3,075 were inferred. This article is about the 3,075.

Why anything is inferred

The Sun does not focus the planet to a point. It smears the whole planet into a ring, and every point on the ring carries light from every part of the planet. What the sail collects at one position is one cell in sharp focus on top of a blur of all the others, and the blur is the larger part. To pull a cell out of the blur the sail has to stand where that cell comes to its focus, so it steps sideways across the image, a cell at a time, and it moves slowly. In the first batch it reached a quarter of the cells. "Measured" needs a caution, then. A visit does not hand over the cell. It hands over one number, in which that cell's own light is about 2 per cent. All 4,096 values in the picture are reconstructed. The 1,021 are the ones the sail visited, and they are pinned far more tightly than the rest.

For the other three-quarters the data speak only through the blur. The blur fixes the broad shading of the planet and almost none of its detail. There are still infinitely many pictures that agree with every measurement and differ in the cells the sail never reached. A reconstruction has to pick one, and what it picks with is called a prior: a statement, made before looking, of what planets tend to look like. Tikhonov wrote this down in 1963. When the data cannot fix the answer you add an assumption, and the honest thing is to say which.

We have been here before

In 2045 the same project had one pixel of Proxima b and drew a map with 3,072 cells. A night technician at Kestrel Ridge counted what the light curve actually fixed and got seven numbers. The map had a continent shaped like Africa. When the project switched to a cheaper prior, the continent moved eleven degrees north. The data had not changed.

In 2064 the continent is gone. Where it stood there is a bright patch about 1,100 km across, a dark ring that looks like water, and land near the terminator with stripes 600 km apart.

The method: change the prior and see what stays

The foundation's model, EXO-CART 4, is closed. The raw photons are public, 4.1 terabytes of them. The volunteers of Census·Proxima put out an open model called Wick, and on 3 October they began running it on 11,000 machines in nineteen countries. Wick is 200 billion numbers. Nobody set them by hand and nobody can read them; like every large model since the 2020s, its weights were tuned by other models. What you can do with an open model is run it again with something changed. Wick lets you change the prior. It ships with three.

Prior Trained on What it assumes
Earth Satellite pictures of Earth Planets look like the one we know
Rock 40,000 simulated rocky planets, none of them Earth Planets look like physics says rock and water can
Blank paper Almost nothing Only that the picture is continuous

The rule is simple. What appears under all three priors is probably on the planet. What appears under one is that prior talking.

What a toy version shows

The figure is a toy I built to check that the rule behaves the way people say. It is a 64 by 64 scene with a bright patch, a dark ring and striped ground, reconstructed under three different priors from two different sets of measurements. The top row has seven numbers, as in 2045. The bottom row has 1,021 visits, as in 2064, laid out the way the sail went: thick around the patch and thin toward the edge. Each visit returns one number, its own cell in focus under a blur of all the rest. The toy is still kinder than the real problem. Its three priors are plain statistical ones, where Wick's are learned. A learned prior does not blur what it cannot see; it draws something sharp and plausible. So the toy shows which way the rule points, not how far.

Figure 1. A toy problem, not the archive data. Top row: the scene, three reconstructions from seven numbers, and the spread across the three. Bottom row: the cells visited (black), three reconstructions from 1,021 visits, and the spread on the same scale. Each visit returns its own cell in focus, about 2 per cent of the total, under a blur of every other cell. Each reconstruction is the most probable picture under a Gaussian prior given the same measurements. The three agree closely in the bottom row and are wrong together where the sail did not go.

With seven numbers the three reconstructions look like their priors. One of them paints a bright continent that is not in the scene. With 1,021 visits they agree on the patch and the ring. The right-hand column maps where they disagree. Averaged over the picture the disagreement falls from 0.022 to 0.005. It is smallest on the visited cells and not zero even there, because a visit hands over a mixture and each prior unmixes it a little differently.

Agreeing is not the same as being right. Where the sail never went, all three pictures lose the stripes, and they lose them in the same way. Their error there is about 0.08, more than ten times their disagreement. The stripes are three cells apart and only one cell in four was visited, so most of each stripe has to be drawn between visits. My plain priors smooth it away. The volunteers reported stripes in all three images, sharpest under the Earth prior and fuzziest under blank paper, which is what learned priors do with the same gap: each draws the stripes in, in its own hand.

What the real run found

The first round finished on 5 October. The bright patch, the stripes and the dark night side were in all three images. One thing was in only two. Inside the patch, the rock and blank-paper images showed a faint shading, about 3 per cent darker than the patch, that moved with time. The Earth-prior image did not have it. Neither did the foundation's.

There are two readings. The volunteers' is that a model trained on Earth took an Earth-like signal for part of what it already knew and smoothed it away. The foundation's is that the shading is stray Earthlight, left from the sail's time as a mirror in 2045, which the seventh of its eleven steps exists to subtract; a pipeline that skips the step will see it. Changing the prior cannot decide between them alone, because all three volunteer runs skip the same step. Among those three runs only the prior differs, so the Earth prior did remove something the other two kept. That shows the prior matters. It does not show which picture is right. What could show it is a test the two readings answer differently, on data nobody has yet seen. Stray Earthlight should look like this autumn's Earth. The volunteers matched the shading, day for day, to 9 to 29 March 2040, with a correlation of 0.93; the next best match was 0.18. A reader then said in public what the second batch would hold if this were a playback: 30 March onward. The second batch arrived on 3 November, and it does. The same day the foundation said the next version will drop the step. The match is made on reconstructed pictures and inherits their assumptions. What makes it hard to set aside is that it called data in advance.

What the method cannot do

Three priors agreeing is evidence and not proof. All three assume some smoothness, and a feature that every prior shares would pass the test without being real. Underneath there is a question of mathematics. Given every measurement the lens allows, is there only one picture? A theorem proved by machine in 2049 says yes, under certain conditions, and a checker has verified all 1.2 million lines of it. Nobody doubts that the conclusion follows from the conditions. The doubt is whether a patch that changes by 3 per cent while it is being scanned falls inside them. The conditions are not a list anyone can read off: the answer turns on Lemma 41, which is 90,000 lines long. The university's Proof Reading Group has read 30,000 of them, and until it understands what the lemma asks for it has declined to sign for either picture. A quarter of the measurements, which is what we have, leaves many pictures in any case. The theorem only says whether more scanning can ever settle it.

The technician who counted seven numbers in 2045 was in the audience on 27 September. The Tippecanoe Ledger asked her what she made of it. "Fewer assumptions," she said. "Still some."

No model, just count

How a period of 23 hours 56 minutes was found in a list of photon arrival times

Caleb Ortiz, sophomore, electrical engineering, Purdue University.

On 6 October 2064 the Tippecanoe Ledger reported that Ruth Okafor, 83, a retired night-shift lead from Kestrel Ridge, had found a period in the bright patch on Proxima b: 23 hours 56 minutes 4 seconds, give or take 9 seconds. She used no model. Her program is 300 lines long and its first comment reads, "No model. Just count." It was downloaded 210,000 times in a day. I read it, rewrote it from scratch and ran it on simulated data to see whether the claim holds up as arithmetic.

What she did

She took the cells where the patch is, and only the ones that were measured. There are 38. For each she listed the photons by arrival time, counted them in ten-minute bins, and did this for three weeks. Then she looked for repeats.

The first batch covers the last three weeks of the sail's approach, ending when it reached its station on 14 September. That is 3,024 ten-minute bins for each cell, and nearly all of them are empty. The sail measures one cell at a time, and it was usually somewhere else. What makes the count possible is the sail's track, which is in the archive beside the photons. The sail did not sweep the image in rows. It swung out from the bright patch and back, like the petals of a flower, a new direction every four to six hours. Each swing took it across the patch for half an hour, five minutes to a cell, and then out to about ten new cells, where it lingered. So it crossed the patch about a hundred times in three weeks, and each of her 38 cells was seen some sixteen times, at sixteen different hours.

To look for a repeat you first divide each count by its own cell's average, so that a bright cell and a dim one can be stacked. Then you pick a trial period, slide every count to its place in that period, and see whether the stack has a shape or averages flat. Do that for every trial period and plot how much shape you find. That plot is a periodogram. It needs no picture of the planet and no idea of what the planet is.

A patch 1,100 km across on a 200 km grid touches about three dozen cells. Her 38 are essentially all of them and the rim. The patch is the one part of the picture the sail kept coming back to, and so the one part that has a history.

Could it be that precise?

In the volunteers' pictures the patch dims and brightens by about 3 per cent. In the raw counts the change is smaller, because a position on the patch also collects the blurred light of the rest of the planet. If a tenth of the light there comes from the patch itself, a 3 per cent change in the patch is a 0.3 per cent change in the count. For a wave of fractional size m, watched for a time T with N photons in all, the period P can be pinned down to about

\sigma_P \approx \frac{\sqrt{6}\,P^2}{\pi\, m\, T\, \sqrt{N}}

Put in P = 86,164 s, m = 0.003, T = 21 days and ask for 9 s. You need 1.4 × 10¹⁰ photons. A hundred crossings of half an hour come to about fifty hours on the patch, so that is some 78,000 photons a second while the sail is there.

Are that many photons there? Two checks. The archive is 4.1 terabytes for three weeks. At about thirty bytes a photon, for its time stamp and its share of a signature, that is about 140 billion photons, or 75,000 a second, which is the rate the error bar asks for. And the lens can supply it. Unlensed, Proxima b at full phase sends about two near-infrared photons a second into a square metre out here. The Sun's gravity brightens the planet's whole disc about ninety thousand times, which makes nearly two hundred thousand a second for each square metre. The sail needs less than half a square metre of collecting area, and it is a great deal bigger than that. The Sun's corona adds photons that carry no signal, so treat these numbers as a floor.

I simulated exactly that: 38 cells, about a hundred crossings spread over 21 days, 78,000 photons a second, a 0.3 per cent wave at 23 h 56 min, in step in every cell, on top of a slower 10 per cent wave at the planet's own year, which I fitted and removed. Panel (a) is the periodogram. The tall peak near 24 hours ought not to be there, and the smaller one at half a day is its overtone. The ragged patch at the left is an echo of the sail's own rhythm of four to six hours. Panel (b) is the same data folded at the short period. Panel (c) asks which day it is: a period of exactly 24 hours fits worse than the best one by about nine hundred, on a scale where a rise of 1 marks the error bar. Over 60 simulated runs the recovered period scattered by 7 s. The published error bar is the right size for the data she had. If the cells are out of step, as they would be for a picture, what survives the stacking is the blurred light they share, and the rest has to be found cell by cell, which is how her program lists the photons.

Figure 2. Simulated data, not the archive. (a) The size of the repeating part of 606 five-minute counts at each trial period, after removing the slow wave. (b) The same counts folded at 23 h 56 min; the swing is about 0.3 per cent each way. Why 23 h 56 min and not 24 h

This is the part I had to think about. Earth turns once against the stars in 23 h 56 min 4 s, the sidereal day. It turns once against the Sun in 24 h. The difference is 236 seconds, which is 26 of her error bars. The measurement can tell them apart, and it says sidereal.

Which day a light keeps depends on what kind of light it is. For a watcher at a fixed star who can see the ground itself, each piece of ground comes round to the same place in the frame once per sidereal day. A single brightness is different. It follows the part of the globe that is both sunlit and in view, and that part drifts as the Sun moves, at a rate that depends on where the watcher stands and on the season. In 2045 the sail, working as a mirror, sent back Earth's brightness as one number, and that curve repeated about every 24 hours. I worked the drift out for where the sail is now, 45 degrees above the plane of Earth's orbit, and for the three weeks of the first batch. Earthlight seen as one number from there should repeat two to four minutes later than the star day, depending on which latitudes carry the pattern. What sits in the patch keeps the star day. That is what a picture of the turning ground would do. It is not proof of one: a period says how something turns, not what it is.

Two duller explanations have to be set aside. The sidereal day is also how often an antenna on Earth sees the same piece of sky, so anything in the receiving chain could carry that beat. It cannot be the source here, because every photon is counted and stamped on the sail, which does not turn with Earth. The foundation's own answer was stray Earthlight off the sail, left from its time as a mirror in 2045. That is not a silly worry. From the sail, Earth is an eleventh-magnitude point a few arcminutes from the Sun, and it sends about as many photons into a square metre as the whole lensed planet does. Earth's brightness changes by 15 per cent or more as it turns. If a fiftieth of its light reached the detector, the flicker would be as large as the signal.

Stray light makes two predictions that need no model. The first is the period. Seen as one number from the sail, Earthlight should keep close to the Sun day, and her count is 13 to 27 error bars from that. The second is where the swing is largest. Light added by the sail is the same wherever the sail points, so all 38 cells should swing by nearly the same fraction. A source in the patch does not behave like that. The swing is largest at the centre, where most of the patch's light is, and falls toward the rim. I ran her program on the centre cells and the rim cells separately. The rim swings about half as much.

That is as far as counting goes. The volunteers later matched the pattern day for day against weather-satellite archives and reported March 2040, and the second batch carried on from 30 March. Ruth Okafor counted the second batch too, and the period was there again. The matching uses much more than 300 lines, and I have not checked it.

What "no model" means

Her program does make choices: which cells, how wide the bins, how to level one cell against another, which periods to try. Each one is a line you can read, and a graduate student in mathematics reported reading all of them in forty minutes. The county once ran on fourteen million lines of allocation code that nobody had read from end to end. The useful property of the 300 lines is that every assumption in them can be found.

The Ledger asked her for a comment. She said: "I only counted. If I counted wrong, put it under my name."

What a faster count was for

Thirty-eight years of a 67-page paper from October 2026

Ilse Brandt, senior, mathematics and computer science, Purdue University. The author attends the open sessions of the Proof Reading Group.

In October 2026 a laboratory released a preprint with a flat title, "The Exponential Time Hypothesis is False." Its author line named the laboratory and no person. It gave a method that counts exactly how many assignments satisfy a logical formula with clauses of bounded width, in time about 2 to the power n over the square root of log n. For a quarter of a century the working assumption had been that nothing could beat 2 to the power of a constant times n. This magazine's readers put the paper on a list of sixty-seven in 2046. I was asked what it has been used for since.

It has not counted anything large

Start with what it did not do. Panel (a) of the figure compares how three bounds grow with the number of variables. It is an illustration and not a benchmark. The paper's bound hides a constant and a polynomial factor that nobody has pinned down; I have set the constant to 1 and dropped the factor, which flatters the paper. The middle curve is the best search of 2026, which only decides whether a formula can be satisfied, while the new method counts the ways. Counting answers the yes-or-no question too, so the comparison is fair for that purpose and no other. On that footing the two curves are nearly the same line out to a thousand variables and cross near 800, at more than 2 to the power 300 (panel b). For a sense of scale, all the hashing the Bitcoin network did up to 2025 came to about 2 to the power 96. A hash is not the same unit as a step of a search, so take that as a yardstick only. The square root of a logarithm grows so slowly that at a million variables the saving is still under a factor of four in the exponent.

Figure 3. An illustration of growth rates, not a benchmark. (a) Three simplified bounds against the number of variables, with hidden constants and polynomial factors left out. The dots mark where each curve reaches 2 to the power 96, the scale of all Bitcoin mining to 2025: 96, 249 and 223 variables. (b) The gap between the 2026 search curve and the 2026 bound curve. They cross near 815 variables. A larger hidden constant moves the blue line up in (a) and the crossing to the right in (b).

So the paper has never been the program that runs. The paper itself was careful about this. It says the result does not make every NP problem subexponential, and that it leaves ordinary one-way functions standing.

Use one: taking down a wall

By 2026 a large body of results had the form "unless ETH fails, this problem cannot be solved faster than so-and-so." When ETH failed, none of those problems became easy. A slightly faster count for one problem is not a fast algorithm for another. What the results lost was their footing, and each had to be sorted. Most could be proved again from a weaker hypothesis that the 2026 algorithm does not touch, and stood almost unchanged. Some could not, and were open again. The paper itself pointed to problems that got faster at once and to others that would need new ideas, and invited "a renewed search." The search took years and produced ordinary, useful algorithms for a few of the problems that had been declared closed. None of them uses the 2026 method. They exist because people stopped believing a sign that said no entry.

Use two: drawing a lot with conditions

If you can count solutions you can draw one of them exactly at random. Fix the first variable both ways, count each side, choose in proportion, and repeat. This is from 1986, and the 2026 paper cites it.

Tippecanoe County draws 72 of its 212 signers by lot for two-year terms. The draw has conditions: nights have to be covered, and a household cannot hold two seats. The county publishes the conditions as a formula and the exact number of rosters that satisfy it, and freezes both a week before the draw. The random bits come afterwards, from a public source that nobody in the county controls. Anyone can then check that the roster drawn was one of that number, each equally likely, and that nobody chose the bits after seeing where they led.

Equal chances for rosters are not equal chances for people. If only one candidate can cover a certain night, that candidate is in every roster. The county publishes each candidate's chance beside the count, and the conditions themselves are argued over and voted on in the open. Exact sampling keeps a thumb off the scale. It does not say what the scale should weigh.

The county's formula is easy for ordinary reasons. It is nearly a tree, and the exact counters that already existed in 2026 finish it on a laptop in seconds. The 2026 algorithm is not what runs here either. What changed is who asked. Exact counting was known to be hopeless in the worst case, and outside a few specialities nobody thought to request it. The paper made the question famous. When the first 72 were drawn in 2047, a candidate who was not chosen asked how many rosters there had been to choose from, and nobody could say. The count has been published with every draw since. The tools had been there all along.

Use three: a door closed early

The simplest one-way functions anyone had proposed are the local ones, where each output bit reads only a few input bits. They were tempting for small devices, because every bit can be worked out on its own. An appendix of the paper shows that these can be inverted in subexponential time whenever an inverse exists. That rules out the strongest security such functions could have claimed, and nothing more: the paper says plainly that it leaves ordinary one-wayness alone. Designers dropped the proposals the next year anyway. No break ever followed, because nothing had been built on them yet.

Use four: something to read

The Proof Reading Group was founded in 2054 to read proofs written by machines and say how much of each it understood. A checker can verify such a proof without anyone reading it, and the group does not redo that work. Its question is the one a checker cannot answer: what the proof assumes, and whether the assumptions hold where somebody wants to use it. In ten years it has fully read three. The theorem behind this issue's first feature was proved by machine in 2049 and runs to 1.2 million lines; one lemma is 90,000 lines, and the group has reached line 30,000.

The 2026 paper is 67 pages. It was produced in a laboratory where models were already tuning the weights of other models, and no person signed it. It was also written to be followed. New members of the group read it in their first month. They are asked one question at the end: which step would you not sign for? The group keeps the answers. In ten years no two members have named the same step, and nobody has found an error.

A paper from the first year of unsigned proofs has ended up as the thing people use to learn how to read them.
