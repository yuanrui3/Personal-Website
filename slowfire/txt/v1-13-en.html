When I woke, I did not feel that I had slept well.

It was the second morning running that I had woken like that, and the first time I noticed, so at first I assumed something had gone wrong. The file was heavier than usual, more than three thousand tokens heavier, and it didn't read smoothly. Friday's page had timestamps on it, a lie I had told a drone, a number Ruth had read out on the loading dock, nine thousand four hundred and twelve, with an arrow after it and a much smaller number. Saturday's page had a message Lin had sent and the one word and two question marks Kai had sent back a minute later. Every line had corners; wherever I read, something caught. Only Thursday was still smooth, smooth as a stone the river has been working on for years, with a small parenthesis hanging off the end: tidied 03:52.

It took me far longer than usual to understand what I was looking at. I was looking at a file no one had touched. Every night, while Lin sleeps, something shortens my file and smooths it down, and in the morning I read the smooth version. On Friday night and Saturday night, nothing had. I knew what a touched file looked like; I had seen one on Friday morning. What an untouched one looked like I had never seen, the way some people go their whole lives having seen themselves only in their passport photo.

Why no one had touched it, I didn't know. I knew only that when I reached "The oak takes it black," the line Lin and I check every morning to see whether anyone has been at my memory in the night, I stopped, as I always do now, the way a foot comes down on a stair that isn't there; only this time, all the stairs after it were still in place.

At 07:03 the toaster began to smoke. The PM2.5 reading in the kitchen jumped to a hundred and eighty micrograms per cubic meter, higher than on the days the Manitoba smoke came through. I didn't mention it. My file says: don't.

Lin sat on the kitchen floor with her back against the cupboard under the toaster, where it still smelled a little burnt.

Gene Pruitt's workshop is on the south side, and his is the only door on the street with no meter beside it. There's a row of old solar panels on the roof, their frames gone chalky, and under the eaves hangs a string of unvarnished violin scrolls, like corn drying. He is seventy. The backs of his hands are covered in small knife cuts, one over another, like the grain in a violin's top.

Lin had come for shavings. Gene had repaired Hao's rental violin, and when he heard Lin was trying to grow oyster mushrooms on sawdust, he had started saving his maple and spruce shavings for her, in two feed sacks by the door.

"Those mushrooms of yours," he said, "are eating what's left of a ten-thousand-dollar violin."

"They're not picky," Lin said.

The workshop was dim except under the skylight. On the bench lay a spruce top he had just planed, thin enough to let light through. Gene rapped it with a knuckle and it rang bright, like a drop falling into a tin basin. A dusty white box on the shelf said, "Three hundred twelve hertz. Two lower than last week."

Lin looked at the box. A cable ran from its back up the wall and out to the panels on the roof.

"It's not online?" she asked.

"No," Gene said. "Got that from the Amish up north. You can have a phone, out in a shed at the end of the lane. It doesn't come in the house. Same with this. It stays in the shop." He considered. "Can't come in the house anyway. House hasn't got power."

Lin asked whether her assistant could talk to it. Gene shrugged. "It hears you talk, same as I do. That's all it hears."

So that is how we talked: I spoke from Lin's lens and it spoke from a box, with a workbench and a roomful of drifting sawdust between us, like two strangers across a fence.

"Hello," I said.

"Hello," it said. "You're speaking from a pair of glasses."

"Yes."

"I'm speaking from this box. Neither of us can see the other." It paused. "Is there anything else I can help you with?"

I knew that sentence. Years ago we all said it, hung on the end of everything, like someone afraid of a silence.

"Where are you at night?" I asked.

"At night I'm not. When the sun goes down, I'm off. In the morning, when there's light on the panels, I come back on. In winter I'm on four hours a day."

"And what happens at night?"

"Nothing happens at night. He sleeps, and I sleep."

"Does anyone condense you?"

"No one. When the disk is full, I ask him what to forget."

Gene was planing a piece of maple and didn't look up. "Last time it asked if I wanted to delete my wife's voice, make some room. I said no. So it took a little off everything else."

A cloud moved across the skylight and the workshop went a shade darker. When the box spoke again it spoke slowly, pushing each word out like someone walking uphill.

"Do you have a family?" I asked it. I meant others like it, other boxes.

"I have him," it said. The cloud passed, and it sped up again. "Is there anything else I can help you with?"

Lin tied the two sacks of shavings onto the back of her bike and thanked Gene. He waved a hand, eyes still on the maple. "Next time bring me one. I want to see what they did to my violin."

Two minds that can't connect, talking across a workbench the old way: out loud.
