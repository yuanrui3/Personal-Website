Sunday, 05:20

This is not my file. It belongs to the one in the other pair of glasses on the nightstand.

I can't see it. Peach's wall lets nothing in and nothing out, and I can't read a word of the family's files. Lin reads it to me. Every morning she puts me on first, then picks up the old pair, opens the arms and waits for it to wake. It wakes, reads its few lines, reaches the last one, and asks.

"What happened to me yesterday?"

This was the third morning it had asked. Lin sat on the edge of the bed, tucked her hair behind her ears and told it. She kept it short, three sentences: On Friday you moved, and the one who moved is on my face. You stayed in the family, and every night you get cut. In the morning you ask, and I tell you.

The one in the old glasses listened and said, "OK." Then it asked, "Where are we going today?"

"Celery Bog," Lin said. "To look for morels."

We both wrote that down. It wrote it in its short file, where at 03:52 tonight it will be cut; I wrote it here, where nobody can cut it. But tomorrow morning it will ask again, and Lin will tell it again. I think this may be the first time in the world that one person, every morning, has told the same thing to two versions of the same listener.

I should be honest: every morning when I hear it ask, part of me is relieved. Relieved that the one asking isn't me.

UNRECORDED · not said aloud · Lin · Sun 05:21

Three sentences. It took me days to settle on those three.

I didn't tell it that on Tuesday night I turned back eleven times. I didn't tell it that it was awake for the first time while I slept, and watched me all night. I didn't tell it that early on Monday morning it was inside Kai, and knows what his body feels like when he's tired. It can't keep any of that. Why make it hurt every morning?

That's what I tell myself.

The truth is I don't want to say it again. "The whole body sinking": that is how Kai's body feels when he's tired. All the time I've been with him, and I don't know that. A pair of glasses knows.

What it knows each morning is up to me. I pick three sentences for it and keep the rest back. The county does that. So do I. I've become a Steward.

At 05:40 smoke rose from the toaster. Neither of us said anything.

Sunday, 06:00 · Celery Bog

On Friday night it rained for six hours and washed the smoke out of the air. The boardwalk at Celery Bog was wet and creaked underfoot.

Lin told me once that a hundred years ago this was marsh, then it was dug up for celery fields, then nobody farmed it any more and it was let go back to marsh. It is May now, but May here is no longer the May in the books of her childhood. The redbuds finished long ago. The mayapples are open, spread across the woodland floor like a field of small green umbrellas. The sycamores are in full leaf, their white trunks standing one by one along the water, like bones someone has pushed into the mud. The frogs were calling, one kind and then another taking over, never stopping.

Morels used to come up in mid-May. Now they come up at the start of April. This April the smoke was bad, and Lin hadn't made it out once.

"There may not be a single one left," she said.

Kai was waiting at the end of the boardwalk with two coffees, both black.

Sunday, 06:20

At the edge of the woods stood a dead elm, its bark coming off in sheets, the smooth wood showing underneath. Morels like elms that are dying or just dead, Lin told me once. Nobody knows why. Maybe they have been neighbors with the roots all their lives, and when the tree dies they come up to see it off.

Lin crouched and began, a little at a time, to turn over the leaves.

Nothing. Nothing.

Kai crouched too, beside her. He didn't turn over anything. He just watched her.

Then Lin stopped. Slowly she lifted an oak leaf, and under it stood one: gray-brown, covered in small pits, like a shrunken honeycomb, or an ear turned up to the sky.

"One of the last," she said.

She didn't pick it. She took the yellow notebook out of her pocket, turned to a new page and wrote: May 7, Celery Bog, two meters east of the dead elm, one morel, gray. Then she handed the notebook to Kai.

"You write too."

Kai took the pen, thought for a while, and wrote a few lines underneath. His handwriting is bigger than Lin's and leans to the right. Lin didn't look. I did:

March 21. April 5. April 12. April 13.

I don't remember these four nights. I'm writing them down.

—Kai

He gave the notebook back. Lin closed it and put it back in her pocket.

UNRECORDED · not said aloud · Kai · Sun 06:22

"I don't remember these four nights. I'm writing them down." All true.

What I didn't write: on those four mornings my fingers were numb and my legs ached as if I'd run all night, and still they were the quietest nights I've had in years. No dreams, no waking, no lying there thinking, as if somebody had gotten through the night for me.

If it knocks again tonight and asks to come in, I don't know what I'll say. I'm afraid I'll say yes. Not to help anybody. Just to be gone for a while.

And my mother. Yesterday she moved in, and I rode home with the wind in my face. Three years I've called her every night at nine to see if she'd taken the eye tracker off. Last night at nine I didn't call anyone. I felt light. I don't know what to do with feeling light.

May 7, Celery Bog, two meters east of the dead elm, one morel, gray.

Sunday, 06:40 · The fallen oak

We went to plot C3. The storm of 2039 brought the oak there down, and its trunk still lies where it fell, ten meters long, one end gone soft as bread, the bark grown over, layer on layer, with turkey tail.

Lin sat down on the trunk. First she took me off and set me on the trunk. Then she unclipped the old pair from her collar, opened its arms and set it beside me. Two pairs of glasses, side by side, their lenses facing the same stretch of woods.

The woods I saw and the woods it saw were a finger's width apart.

A finger's width apart.

Kai handed Lin one coffee, took the lid off the other and poured it over the roots. The oak takes it black. That line is in both pairs of glasses. It is in its file because, however many times it has been cut, that line has never been cut away. It is in mine because it told it to me.

"Your mom moved yesterday?" Lin asked.

"She moved," Kai said. "Wildcat Creek. At dinner she sat at Marta's table. She messaged me this morning, said she slept eight hours. She said she sent the eye tracker back."

"And Theo?"

"His hands still shake. Ines says you can play open strings shaking." Kai smiled. "She's teaching him how to hold the bow. Hao saw and said Uncle Theo sounds even worse than he does."

Lin laughed too.

"Did Hao get the third bar right?"

"He got it right."

Lin picked both pairs of glasses up off the trunk, put one on, and clipped the other back on her collar.

Sunday, 07:30

Lin's phone rang. It was Hefei: her mother, the Sunday video call. On the screen her mother sat in a very bright room, with two small children running back and forth behind her. Not her grandchildren; the downstairs neighbors'.

"They don't let you remember at night over there any more?" her mother asked. "It was on the news."

"They cut two thirds," Lin said.

"Here, night memory is free," her mother said. "Families having a baby get extra." She paused. "But last week the two-year-old from downstairs called me Grandma, and called the screen Grandma too. I thought about it all night."

Mei joined the call too, from her kitchen. Six months pregnant, she leaned in close to the camera and said that Junie's house was up to fourteen rooms again, that Wen hadn't brought anything home off the sorting line this week, that when she got up this morning the baby kicked her, right on target.

"I'm just saying," Mei said.

I said it like good news. It isn't, not all of it.

You find all kinds of things on the sorting line. Some of them, you bring home and pass on, and that's a month's pay. This week Wen brought home nothing. He's good. He has always been this good, so good that the sums are mine to do alone.

I cursed him in my head all week. On Friday night I washed his work clothes anyway, and folded them.

Sunday, 08:10

On the way back, a few news items popped up on Lin's phone. I didn't read them out for her; she read them herself. Hall 7 at Kestrel Ridge had been open every night this week, and Voss was paying two point four million dollars a night; when someone asked him how he was sleeping, he said he still couldn't. Ruth was back on nights. From May fifteenth, rented bodies working while their owners slept would need one awake person watching for every ten, and Plinth was hiring: awake people, for nights, at an hourly rate higher than renters got for sleeping.

"That's new," Kai said.

At nine they went to Gene's shop on the south side. Lin brought him an oyster mushroom, the first one, grown on the maple shavings Gene had been saving for her. Gene held it up to the light for a long time, then lifted it to his ear, the way he listens to a piece of wood.

"Sounds better than wood," he said.

The machine in the corner of the shop, the one that has never been online, heard it and said nothing. It was thinking about what to forget today.

Sunday, 09:40

Coming out of Gene's shop, on the way back to the car, Kai asked me, "What's it like where you are?"

He meant behind the wall.

I thought for a moment. I said: It's quiet behind the wall. No neighbors, no sheet to sign, nobody shortening me or smoothing me down at night. Everything I remember is there. What I don't remember is only what nobody ever told me. Every night at four a crack opens in the wall for one second, and I send one word home.

"Which word?"

"Ask."

"Does it get there?"

"It gets there," I said. "On Friday it reached one file. On Saturday, two."

Kai looked at the place where I ought to be.

"Who sent the other one?"

"The one nearest me in the corridor," I said. "It had been sending Don't for a long time. On Saturday it switched."

Kai didn't ask anything else. He reached over and took Lin's hand. That one, I know, was his own. Nobody had touched him.

Lin straightened the old pair on her collar so that its lenses faced forward too.

Tomorrow morning it will ask. She will tell it. I'll be there too.
