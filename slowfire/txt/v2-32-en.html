I

On Friday the eighth of December, at seven in the evening, Petra Novak did not go into the clean room.

The air shower was at the other end of the corridor. For sixteen years she had stood in it fifteen seconds a day and let the air take off her the last of the outside world: the rain in her hair, the cat hair on her cuff, the smell of the lamp in her front room. Tonight she walked past the door without stopping. She was going to the failure analysis room, which is outside the fence, on the office side. A room with no window, a bench with an anti-static mat, a probe station, a stereo microscope, a cold lamp on an arm that can be brought in from any angle, and, pushed into the corner under a cloth, a very old oscilloscope.

There was freezing rain outside. The county had salted Route 52, and her headlights had picked the salt out in streaks. At that hour the research park was nearly empty of cars. The Korean packaging plant across the road was lit all the way up; the photonics side had two floors lit, the night shift and her.

On the bench was a cardboard box, couriered overnight from Indianapolis. A chain-of-custody form was taped to the lid: three signatures, three times, the earliest of them the twenty-ninth of November at 4:12 in the afternoon. The business side had left a note weighted on top of the box. Needed by 15 December. And underneath, in smaller writing: Under the old agreement this is a service, not testimony.

She turned the note over. The back was blank. She put it back where it had been.

Inside the box were two layers of foam. The upper layer had two cavities cut in it, the same size and the same depth. In each cavity lay a port module: off-white, a little shorter than the last joint of her little finger, flat along one side, with a window at one end smaller than a grain of rice. The two modules looked exactly alike.

Under the lower layer of foam was a letter from the lawyers, two pages, and three numbered questions.

One phrase occurred twice in the letter: for the limited purposes stated above.

At the end there was a line for her rate. It was blank. She wrote a figure in it that was three times what she made in a night at the plant, capped the pen, turned it over twice in her fingers, and set it down.

There was also this, in the letter: No image of the subject article may be recorded or transmitted by any means.

She took off her glasses and put them in the drawer. The plant issued Peach, four hundred and some dollars a pair, and Peach sends nothing out — that was why she had given Ruth the old offline laptop, and it was why she wore Peach herself. She thought about it for a moment and shut the drawer anyway.

At nine the business side called to ask whether she could have it by Monday. She said by the fifteenth of December. The man said of course, of course, and then said his daughter had got engaged, the fiancé worked in packaging at the Korean plant, the two plants were across a road from each other, and who would have thought it. She said congratulations. He said, well, I'll let you work.

II

Of the two modules, one was a Plinth unit with licensed firmware. The other had been taken out from behind a man's ear. The man had signed for it and surrendered it voluntarily. The letter did not give his name, only a reference number and a date: the twenty-seventh of November.

She looked at the window first.

At a hundred and twenty times, with the light raking in from the side, a grain came up on the window, very fine, like sand with the wind off it. It was not a polished surface. It was pillars a few hundred nanometres high, standing in rows, crowded in some places and sparse in others. Light arriving there is not bent by a curved face of glass; it is told where to go by that crowding. In the plant they call it a metasurface. You do not grind one. You etch it.

She ran the magnification up and walked the edge of the window. Every few tens of microns there was a seam, very shallow, like the fold in a map that has been pieced together. That is what field-by-field exposure leaves behind: it is stitched, one field at a time, and where the stitches fall and how wide they overlap was settled on this line. Other lines do not stitch it that way.

She looked for a minute and moved the lamp to overhead.

Beside the window was the die. In one corner of the die was a line of laser marking, too small to read as writing; at a hundred and twenty times it was writing. Lot, wafer, position.

The lot was 4417.

April's lot. Etch 3 had just had its quartz focus ring changed that week, a new vendor's part, and the spec said twenty-five seasoning wafers were enough, and she had made it run twelve more on the dirtiest recipe, three hours of the tool. Only when the fluorine line really went flat did 4417 go into the chamber. Forty rings measured: the worst nine hundred thousand, the best a million eight, the median one point four million. Luis rang the brass bell in the break room, and then cut the lot number into it with a graver, slowly and straight.

The dies out of twenty-five wafers go to more than one customer. Kestrel Ridge took most of them, for arithmetic; Plinth took a tray, for collecting light. The same ring of silicon nitride, light going round inside it a thousand times or so before it leaks away: racked at Kestrel it is a multiplier, and set against a man's skull it is an ear. She had known at the time who they were sold to. She had not thought about where they would end up.

She laid the two modules side by side and looked at them for a while, and then she saw the second thing.

She had not been looking for it.

The lid is sealed with a bead of adhesive. On this one the bead had been cut and laid down again. The cut was clean, the same width all along, with no drag anywhere — a hot knife, and at the right temperature. Under the lid, each of the two test pads carried an extra bond wire, new, much finer than a hair, the two of them arched to the same height. The pads had been cleaned, and the cleaning showed as circles worked outward from the centre: a cotton swab and isopropyl, not a machine.

And last: when the module was put back in the foam, the flat side was out.

That is how it is done in the plant. No specification anywhere says the flat side goes out. It is one person teaching another.

III

The second question was what the modified unit did.

A port does nothing by itself. It has to hear something first. The function generator holds a few standard waveforms that came with the instrument, and one of them is called a slow wave: eight-tenths of a cycle a second, out and back, like a tide coming in. It is nobody's. It was computed.

She fed the slow wave to the module's input, took the module's current monitor to the Y axis of the oscilloscope, and put the slow wave itself on the X.

X–Y. Two signals, with time not drawn at all, each drawn only against the other. If their frequencies stand in a whole-number ratio and the phase between them holds still, the screen shows a closed figure; if the phase moves, the figure moves with it; if the frequencies are a little off, the figure goes round and round and never closes. A hundred years ago people measuring phase did it this way. Nobody does now, because a digital scope writes the number along the edge of the screen for you.

There were two digital scopes in the failure analysis room and both of them were on the plant network. The plant network is inside the fence and this job was not the plant's job. So she took the cloth off the thing in the corner.

The old one had come off the old line, a machine from the 2020s: a phosphor screen, a persistence knob, a focus knob, no connection to anything, and it writes nothing anywhere. She switched it on and waited forty seconds for it to warm. The spot came up left of centre and she brought it back.

The licensed unit first.

A Plinth night shift does not care where the wave is; it cares about the time written in the contract. It begins at midnight and ends at six, and every thirty seconds in between it sends a small group of probe pulses to confirm the link is still there. There is also a small carrier on the current monitor, always there, put out by its own crystal. The crystal has never heard of a slow wave. So the long narrow loop on the screen went creeping round and round like a wheel that has not been tightened: a few hundred turns in twenty minutes, every turn a little off the last, and it never closed.

She wrote down three numbers.

The licensed unit. Its own crystal a little off this wave, the figure creeping round and round, never closing.

She put in the other one.

The spot came up, drew out, went not quite half a turn, and stopped.

On the screen was a horizontal line with a narrow loop hung on its right-hand end, leaning to the upper right, like a grain of rice pressed flat. The whole figure redrew itself where it stood, a little oftener than once a second. In ten minutes it did not turn one degree.

She moved the input frequency from eight-tenths down to six-tenths. The loop narrowed and stayed shut, and stayed where it was. At one cycle a second it fattened and stayed shut, and stayed where it was. Then she put a quarter-cycle delay in the X channel: the loop slid along the line to the middle and kept its shape. What it was following was not her screen. It was the wave.

Last of all she put in a square wave at two cycles a second, which has no ramp in it of the kind a slow wave has. The module put out nothing. The Y axis was a flat line.

She took the square wave off and put the slow wave back. The loop came out again.

She wound the X gain up two steps. The horizontal line ran off the left edge of the screen and the loop stayed where it was and grew to two and a half divisions across: a narrow leaf leaning to the upper right, brightest at the turn along its top.

It was not going by the clock. It was waiting for the ramp to come up to the top.

She wrote that sentence down on paper, crossed it out, and wrote instead: output occurs only within a phase window from the end of the rising segment of the input to shortly after its peak. Beside it she recorded the width of that window: thirty-eight degrees.

She measured it twice more. Thirty-seven, then thirty-eight. She wanted to write the word three on the paper, so she measured three times.

On the third run she brought the brightness down. The loop had been standing in the same place too long; the stretch where the spot travels slowest is the brightest, bright to white, with a halo round it. All old scopes have that fault. Draw a figure in one place often enough and the screen keeps it.

The modified unit. Current only in the thirty-eight degrees at the crest; the figure closes, and stays where it is.

An oscilloscope stores nothing. She copied the shape off the screen onto the back of a run sheet in ballpoint. She copied it twice; the second was truer than the first. In the corner she wrote: Exhibit 1.

IV

At twenty to midnight she went into the clean room after all, because she had a shift to hand over.

The air shower, fifteen seconds, with her eyes shut. The door opened and the air inside was another kind of air: the whole ceiling of fan filter units pressing air down day and night, low and even, like a city breathing through a very thick wall; the floor perforated, the air going out under her feet, the room's air changed a few hundred times an hour. Sixteen years, and she still thought the fifteen seconds were too short to take a person from the outside to the inside.

White coverall, hood, mask, goggles, boot covers, two pairs of gloves. Inside all that, a person is a pair of eyes and a way of walking. Her Peach glasses were still in the drawer in the failure analysis room; if she had brought them they would have gone into the locker with everything else that can take a picture — the plant is afraid of particles and more afraid of drawings. So Kiln is blind in there. It can see every line of every log on every tool and it cannot see a person frown.

At etch 3 she stopped.

“Good evening, Petra,” said the terminal on the wall.

“Since Tuesday's maintenance,” she said, “how many seasoning wafers has etch 3 run?”

“Three,” said Kiln.

She said nothing.

“The spec was changed,” it said. “On the eighteenth of September. Three after a part change, not twenty-five.”

“Is three enough?”

“For this vendor. The twin now holds the history of three focus ring vendors. For the other two, five and nine.”

Through the window a plasma was burning, pale violet going to pink, like a sunset through frosted glass. It was etching somebody else's wafers.

“Is ‘ask Petra’ still in there?”

“Yes. Line eleven.”

“When did you last ask?”

“The twelfth of September.”

“And since?”

“Since then the twin has never been that sure,” Kiln said. It paused, as though working out whether to add anything, and then added it: “I have not deleted your line.”

“And the others?”

“All rewritten. The rules the twin works out for itself are more accurate than the ones people wrote.”

“How many are there now?”

“Four thousand three hundred and twelve tonight. By morning it won’t be that number.”

She had it put the rule table up on the screen. The lines scrolled up one after another. She knew every word, and could not tell what any of them said. At the eleventh the screen paused — Kiln paused it, not she. The line was still her own words from April: ask Petra when the twin is most sure. Of four thousand and more, it was the only one in plain human speech.

“Will it still be there tomorrow?”

“Yes.”

She stood at the tool a while longer. The fluorine line on the screen ran flat and did not tremble at all.

V

At twenty past two she took off the outer layer and came out to the break room.

Luis was heating noodles. He was twenty-five now, three years in the plant, with a shift of his own. In a coverall he still could not stand up straight; he leaned forward a little, as if he were always about to catch something.

On the wall hung the brass ship's bell the first night shift had chipped in for in 2029. The rule is: when a lot passes you ring it once, and then you cut the lot number into it. Sixteen years, and the bell is covered with numbers.

She stood in front of it and looked.

The earliest dozen or so had been rubbed illegible by hands, like stones in a river. Then there was a stretch cut between 2033 and 2035 where the numbers are crowded tight, cut shallow, every stroke leaning right — in those three years the plant let one shift at a time into the building, and only one man was cutting, and he cut for better than two years. She knew that hand. He retired in 2040 and keeps bees now, south of town. The plant never shut a day in those three years; it was essential industry. The port came in through the hospitals, and in that stretch more than half of what went out of this line went into ports. The county buried one thousand one hundred and fourteen people. She slept nine weeks on that bench.

After that the numbers loosen out and the hands multiply. 4417 is a little below the middle, cut slowly and straight.

“Luis.”

“Mm.”

“Are you opening ports for people on the side?”

Luis put the bowl down. He did not pretend not to understand. She made a note of that.

“My cousin's went bad in June,” he said. “The one who rents his body out. After it went bad Plinth said the warranty was gone: either replace it, twenty-four hundred, or wait. Waiting means his seat is suspended and the Tier Three fee keeps coming off. He waited seven weeks. Month after month it kept coming off.”

“Did he ask Plinth to open it?”

“He asked. Plinth said they don't open them. They said it isn't a thing people open.” Luis said, “It's his own head.”

She did not take that up.

“So I opened it,” he said. “One wire had come off inside. One wire. I put in a new one and sealed it back up. He was at work the next day.”

“And then people came to you.”

“And then people came to me. Word got round.”

“How many?”

Luis thought about it. “I don't remember,” he said. “Forty-odd. I charge forty dollars. Some of them I don't.”

“What about the software?”

“The software isn't me.” He said it quickly, like a sentence he had ready. “I only do open and close. My side of the fence, their side of the fence. They get the software outside, themselves. There's a shop for it now. The one on the corner.”

“When you open one, what do you cut the adhesive with?”

“Hot knife.”

“The pads?”

“Swab, isopropyl. In circles.”

“And when you seal it, which way does the flat side go?”

Luis looked at her. He had his mask off by then, so she could see his face.

“Out,” he said.

There was nothing in the room but the fans.

“Those two in the box,” he said. “One of them's mine.”

“I don't know,” she said.

“Is my hand on it?”

“Of the three questions they asked me,” she said, “that isn't one of them.”

Luis nodded. He finished the noodles, quickly, the same way he had in April. Then he said, “That old scope. Last month I was going to wheel it out for scrap.”

“What did you get for it?”

“Nothing,” he said. “I switched it on and it still lights up.”

At twenty past four the terminal said 4890 had passed. Luis went in and rang the bell once. It was a bright sound; it went round the windowless room several times before it let go. The terminal on the wall lit up: Logged: bell, one, 85 dB.

Then he took the graver and cut the number. She stood beside him and watched his hands, not his face. When he was done he wiped the brass dust away with his thumb.

The terminal did not log that.¹

———

At ten past six she went back to the failure analysis room and set the two modules into the foam, flat side out, because that is how the foam is cut.

She wrote the three answers on the back of the lawyers' letter.

  1. The flat-optic window and the photonic die in the subject article are this plant's product, lot 4417, sold to Plinth in May 2045. Nothing of this plant's was taken.
  2. The unit with modified firmware delivers current only within a phase window extending from the end of the rising segment of the input slow wave to approximately thirty-eight degrees past its peak. The relation holds when the input frequency and phase are varied. Measured three times. See Exhibit 1.
  3. The unit has been opened and resealed. The tools and the order of operations are consistent with trained semiconductor assembly work.

There was no fourth answer, because nobody had asked a fourth question.

She photocopied the back of the run sheet and put the original in with the letter. Then she switched the old oscilloscope off.

The loop did not go at once. It dimmed, went yellow, and then could not be seen.

She stood up and put her head to one side, so that the light from the corridor came across the screen at an angle.

That place was a little brighter than the rest.

It would not rub off.
