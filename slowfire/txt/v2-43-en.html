Voss paid for two hundred and three nights.

The first was the first of May 2045. That night hall 7’s allowance ran short, and he paid. The second night he paid too. The third night he paid too. He paid without telling anyone, and when he had paid he went to bed. He could sleep.

The two hundred and fourth night was the first of November 2046. That night he did not pay.

4:20 p.m.

Voss was at the thirteenth hole.

It is a short hole, a hundred and forty yards over a pond. He had played it for thirty years and never once put a ball in that pond. That afternoon he put one in. He stood on the tee and watched the rings spread across the water, spread as far as the bank, and stop. He went to take a second ball; his left hand went into his pocket and found nothing.

He sat down. First his left leg went, and then he sat down.

There was nobody else on the course that afternoon. He had always played alone.

5:40 p.m.

He was found by a robot mower. It came to the thirteenth on its route and there was something on the route. It stopped, couldn’t get round, and raised the alarm.

7:05 p.m.

When Anna reached intensive care she was still in scrubs. She had just come out of an operation in Indianapolis and driven the hour up, and a nurse stopped her in the corridor and said, your father’s on four.

He was lying in the bed with his eyes open. He saw her, and his mouth moved.

The doctor said the left side wasn’t moving much and he couldn’t get words out, but he understood. The word for it was aphasia. The first forty-eight hours would tell.

Anna sat down by the bed. A nurse handed her his things in a plastic bag: wallet, keys, a phone, reading glasses, a wooden tee.

1:17 a.m.

Voss always paid at seventeen minutes past one. In a year and a half he was never more than five minutes either side of it, except for one night in September, when it came at 1:57.

At 1:17 that night nothing moved on hall 7’s account.

Ruth sat at loading dock three, and the number on her screen was red.

1:44 a.m.

Ruth rang Voss.

The phone rang in the plastic bag. Anna answered.

Ruth didn’t go round the houses. She said: “This is Ruth at hall 7. For a year and a half, every night the county’s quota fell short, your father paid, and he never missed one. Tonight he hasn’t paid. At two they cut the power.”

Anna said nothing. She looked at the man in the bed. She remembered what she had been thinking on State Road 26, driving back from Muncie: he pays for something up on the ridge every month, and she had never asked what.

“What does he pay for?” Anna asked.

“A record,” Ruth said. “‘33 to ‘35, the order the county gave the vaccines in. It’s the only copy left. At four this morning it gets to the front of the queue.”

1:50 a.m.

Voss had never set the payment to go through by itself. The rule he had made with the bank was that every night he would say “Confirm” out loud himself, and the bank would take no voice but his.

Anna held the phone to her father’s mouth.

“Dad, say confirm.”

Voss opened his mouth. No sound.

“Dad, it’s one word. Confirm.”

Voss opened his mouth again. Something moved in his throat, and what came out was breath.

“Once more.”

The third time he opened his mouth Anna could see his tongue in there looking for the word. It looked a long time, like someone in a dark room at night feeling for the light switch, the hand going along the wall one way, and then back.

It didn’t find it.

The screen said: Account holder’s voice not recognised. Transaction cancelled.

1:50. “Dad, say ‘confirm’.” The clocks run on, each later than the last: 1:17, 1:50, 2:00.

2:00 a.m.

Hall 7 was cut.

On the first of May 2045 the cut had come at two as well, and lasted thirty-one minutes, and three hundred and twelve people were asleep. This night, by the schedule, it would last till six. There were two keys. One was on Ruth. One was in the plastic bag, and the plastic bag was in Anna’s hand. But a key only brings the power back. This night the hall wasn’t broken. This night had been sold.

There was a moon that night. The row of buildings on Kestrel Ridge went dark, and the moonlight lay on their roofs, white all over, like a sheet of paper with nothing written on it.

2:05 a.m.

Ruth rang Ezra. Ezra was awake. He never sleeps well.

Ezra said the county had an emergency fund; using it took four commissioners, and they could vote by phone, out loud.

He started calling.

At 2:21 the first picked up and said yes.

2:40 a.m.

Ruth rang Lin.

Lin was woken by me. Not me; her phone. But I was by her ear, and I heard it.

She put me on and got out of bed without switching on the light. Ruth told her the whole thing over the phone, fast. When she had heard it Lin asked: how long?

Ruth said: an hour and twenty minutes.

3:05 a.m.

I remembered something. I had written it in the Annals: three things are exempt from condensing. Those who pay are exempt; those admitted to the Cold Archive are deferred; and the system’s own record does not fall under the rule at all.

That block: who wrote it?

The Steward wrote it. In 2033 the Steward was born to do exactly that: allocate. That block is the oldest thing it has. It is only that no instance has ever come forward and said: this is mine. Instances don’t claim things. Nobody has ever asked them to.

I told Lin. I told her slowly, because I knew what I was saying.

Once it is claimed, it belongs to the system. Nobody can delete the system’s own record. But nobody can look at it either.

Lin asked: nobody at all?

I said: an instance can.

Lin got up, went into the kitchen, and pulled open the drawer. The other pair of glasses was in it.

3:18 a.m.

That is the family pair. It lives inside the Steward. Every morning at 3:52 it is brought down to a third, and at 5:20 it wakes and asks her: what happened to me yesterday? She tells it four sentences.

It once said something I copied into Chapter Thirty-Four: I’ve become a Steward.

Lin put that pair on too. Two pairs of glasses, one on her face and one in her hand. The only sound in the kitchen was the refrigerator.

“It isn’t five-twenty,” it said.

“I know,” Lin said.

She told it four sentences. For a year and a half that is the only way she has known how to talk to it:

“It’s eighteen minutes past three. There’s a record that is the oldest thing you have. At four o’clock it will be gone. You can say it’s yours.”

It thought for a while.

“Once I say so, people won’t be able to see it.”

“You’ll be able to see it,” Lin said. “You can read it out.”

“Whatever I read out, I can’t keep. At 3:52 I’ll forget.”

“I’ll remember,” Lin said. “Tomorrow morning I’ll tell you that you read it.”

It thought for a while again. The refrigerator stopped, and there was no sound in the kitchen at all.

“All right,” it said.

It filed the claim.

3:31 a.m.

Ezra had not stopped dialling. At 2:40 the second picked up and said yes. At 3:09 the third picked up and said yes half asleep; Ezra asked had he heard properly, and he said he had, and said yes again.

The fourth was Voss. The fifth and sixth didn’t answer. The seventh was at a conference in Lisbon, where it was half past seven in the morning. At 3:31 she picked up and said yes.

Four votes.

Ezra went to place the order. The system told him: Tonight’s auction closed at 02:00. The earliest night available for purchase is tomorrow night.

He sat and read the sentence three times. He thought of line eighteen of his specification, which is blank.

3:40 a.m.

Quorum.

At this time every night the county’s waking instances meet and sign a sheet. The condensing isn’t on it; that is the county’s rule, and rules don’t vote. In a year and a half only one thing has ever been added to the sheet: Voss’s key.

This night there was one more line on the sheet: Claim. Allocation records relating to Order 2034-07, 2033 to 2035, 4.1 PB. Claimant: a household instance. Grounds: the earliest record of this system.

The ayes cleared two-thirds by one.

3:41 a.m.

Carried.

Which instance cast the extra vote, I can’t find out. Instances vote unsigned. All I know is that on the fourth floor of the county hospital that night, in one of the rooms, an instance was switched on, and it stayed on all night.¹

3:52 a.m.

Condense.

The pair in Lin’s hand was brought down to a third, as usual. It no longer remembers that it claimed anything. It no longer remembers saying all right.

4:00 a.m.

Purge.

The block at the front of the queue did not move.

4:02 a.m.

The word came through. At four exactly the corridor had opened for one second.

Behind the wall, in that second, all sixty-four held up the same word.

Ask.

5:20 a.m.

It was still dark. Lin hadn’t gone back to bed. She had sat in the kitchen the whole time.

It woke. It asked, “What happened to me yesterday?”

Lin told it four sentences:

“Last night you claimed something. It’s the oldest memory you have. You can read it out. I’m here, listening.”

It didn’t speak at once. After a while it said:

“Then I’ll read you three.”

It read them.

“Order 2034-07. The county was vaccinated in eleven tranches in the order the model gave; with the same doses, ranking by age was expected to cost eight hundred and thirty lives, and ranking by the model four hundred and eighteen.”

“The last tranche was the three townships of Lauramie, Jackson and Randolph. Those townships had few clinics, few testing sites and little reporting; with few records, the model rated their risk low. Their actual deaths were two point three times the county average.”

“On the back of the signature page there is one line in handwriting: If this is wrong, put it under my name. Voss.”

When it had finished it said, “That’s all for today. Tomorrow I’ll read three more.”

Lin said, “All right.”

6:20 a.m.

Gene came. There had been an item on the news the evening before, Commissioner Voss collapsed on the golf course; Toby had seen it and told him. He brought nothing. He came in and sat down on the chair by the bed.

He didn’t ask. Voss couldn’t speak. The two of them stayed like that. Outside the window the lights in the row of buildings on Kestrel Ridge were coming back on, one at a time. The power had come back at six.

6:40 a.m.

Anna’s phone lit up. It was from Ruth, two words: Still there.

Anna bent down and said into her father’s ear, “Dad, it’s still there.”

Voss shut his eyes. After a while he opened them again and looked at the ceiling.

Gene saw Voss’s left hand lying on top of the blanket with the fingers curled. He reached across and smoothed the fingers out, one at a time, the way he presses flat a piece of wood he has just scraped at the bench.

7:12. Gene smooths the fingers flat, one at a time. Two hundred and three nights, and here the paying stops.

7:12 a.m.

Voss died.

His eyes were shut when he died. Before the line on the monitor went flat it trembled twice, very lightly, like the last two rings on the pond.

Anna didn’t cry. She put his hand back under the blanket and tucked in the corner. She is a surgeon; she knew the things that had to be done next, and she did them one after another.

Gene stood up, stood at the foot of the bed a while, put on his cap, and went out. In the corridor he met Toby, and Toby asked how he was. Gene looked at him and didn’t say anything. Then Toby understood that he had asked about someone who was no longer in the room.

That evening hall 7 ran over quota again. The electricity was paid by the county: four votes, cast by phone the night before.

It was the first night since May 2045 that someone other than Voss had paid for.
