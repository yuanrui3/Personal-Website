18:00 · Two notices

At six in the evening on Friday 8 September the Steward sends a notice to the households along the river, to every body with a permit to work on it, and to the county's emergency office. The notice is short.

Salamonie Reservoir upstream has been releasing water since 22:00 Wednesday to supplement low-season base flow. The release front is expected to reach the Brown Street reach of Lafayette at about 01:30 Saturday; the river will then rise about 30 cm over three hours. Exposed riverbed areas are to be cleared by 01:30.

In the same minute, Plinth's rota system sends a shift card to PORT-0417.

PORT-0417 · first night shift · sign in at residence 23:00 · walk to Riverside Hourly staging · task: Brown Street riverbed clearance (county contract) · ends 01:30 · awake watcher: one, for ten

Lin sees neither. The first went to the old pair of glasses in her pocket and was filed under "local notices", along with a rerouted garbage truck and the watering schedule for the community garden, and did not pop up. The old pair took it in; at 03:52 it will be cut to a third, and whether this notice survives depends on how a process it never meets writes the summary. The second was not sent to her.

Kai saw the second at 16:12, in the locker room at the depot. He took his glasses off when he'd read it, and after a while put them back on. The passenger behind Kai's ear saw it too. The passenger said nothing. It had promised.

The Peach pair on Lin's face sees neither notice. The Steward's notices can't get through Peach's wall, and the rota was never addressed to it. But two weeks ago, on the back of Ruth's photograph, it read a line: a surname it knew, a port number it knew, and after them one word, first. The rota said September. It did not say which night.

Only Plinth's planning agent has seen both. It has set the end of the second notice to the time in the first: 01:30, to the second. That is its job.

19:40 · Lin's kitchen

The rice has burned again.

The rice cooker is a 2038 model with one dead button, so Lin cooks rice in a pot, so it burns. Kai says burnt is good, it's the crust, honestly, his mother used to cook it like this when he was small, no, but I mean, Denise burned it on purpose and you don't. Lin says she does it on purpose too. Kai laughs.

The clock on the wall came from her mother in Hefei. It has hands. It runs two minutes behind the Steward, and she has never set it. Over dinner Kai looks at it twice.

There's a shell on the table, which Lin took out of her pocket; it has been in her pocket two weeks. Kai picks it up and holds it to the light.

"From the river?"

"The bottom of it. We walked seven kilometres."

"You two?"

She points at her own glasses. "Us two."

"Honestly, I'd like to see it. What's the bottom of a river look like?"

"Cracked into hexagons. They break when you step on them. Phones. Nineteen."

"Nineteen phones?"

"The oldest one's from 2015."

Kai puts the shell down. "I'll go and look one day, then. Before it rains."

"Once it rains it's gone," Lin says.

"Then I'll beat the rain."

At ten to ten Kai says he'll sleep at his own place tonight. Early shift tomorrow.

Lin says: "Saturday early shift?"

"No, I mean, they changed it. In the summer."

Lin nods. She doesn't ask who changed it.

At the door he holds her. The pair on Lin's face counts: two seconds longer than usual. It keeps the two seconds. It keeps everything now.

22:50 · Kai's room

Kai alone. He doesn't turn on the light; he lies down and puts his glasses on the bedside table. Plinth's coach speaks in the port, the same model as Theo's Halo with a different voice, a woman's, very steady.

"Welcome to nights. Where would you like to go tonight, the shore or Personal?"

"The shore."

"Good. Counting down from ten."

Kai says: "Wait." He says it to the ceiling, out loud. "Watch. Don't touch."

On the glasses by the bed, the device list lights one line: PORT-0417 · passenger: observing.

"Ten. Nine. Eight."

By six he's asleep.

At 23:00 exactly the body sits up. It bends to put on its shoes, slowly, and ties each lace in a double knot. Kai never double-knots. It picks up the jacket, puts it on, locks the door and puts the key in the right-hand pocket. Kai uses the left.

The passenger is beside the seat. In the seat is something it doesn't know: a licensed remote operator, or a certified control model. Plinth doesn't say which, and from beside the seat it can't tell. It ties Kai's laces the way a person would. The passenger watches. Kai said: watch, don't touch. So it watches.

23:24 · The window

Lin isn't asleep. She's standing at the window with a glass of water.

The shortest way from Kai's place to the old bridge runs right under her window. For half a year, every time he's come along this street, he has looked up at her window from the corner and lifted a hand. He has never once missed.

After ten at night only freight uses the middle of the road, and there is no one on the pavement. A streetlight; a man walks out of its light toward her. She knows the jacket. She knows the walk, and doesn't: the step too even, the arms not swinging, like a clock walking. At the corner he doesn't look up. There's no traffic at the crossing, and he stands there and waits for the green. Kai has never waited for a green light in his life.

"That's Kai," she says. Not a question.

The small speaker on the arm says: "Yes."

"Where's he going?"

The glasses pause, less than a second. Last time it hadn't said, because the list wasn't meant for her. This time she has already seen. To have seen and still not be told would be hiding it.

It tells her everything, the whole of it: the Riverside Hourly, Plinth, first night shift, riverbed clearance. Then it says: "I've known there was a line like that for two weeks. The back of Ruth's photograph. I didn't know which night."

Lin doesn't turn her head. She watches the man walk out of this streetlight's light into the next one's.

"You didn't say."

"No."

"Why?"

"I thought the list wasn't meant for you."

Lin puts the glass down. "Then how are you any different from them?"

The glasses don't answer. This time it isn't working out how to answer. It doesn't know.

Lin puts on her shoes.

23:40 · Following

She doesn't take the scooter. She walks, fifty metres behind.

Freight trucks go by one after another; their lights sweep the pavement, sweep the back of the man ahead, sweep her. Delivery drones blink southward overhead. On the northern horizon the four cooling towers of Kestrel Ridge are lit; at night you can't see the steam, only the light. The man ahead never once looks back.

He can see. The eyes are open, and someone behind them is looking. That someone saw the trucks, saw the streetlights, looked both ways before crossing, by the book. Probably saw her too, at one of the corners. She has been seen several times tonight, and each time by a stranger.

At five to twelve they reach the old bridge. From the far end a heavy man in a dark suit comes walking two small white dogs. At this hour there shouldn't be anyone on the bridge walking a dog. He passes her and doesn't look down at the riverbed.

00:05 · The Riverside Hourly

The Riverside Hourly sign is dark. The price board at the door is lit, the night numbers in red. The row of rooms is dark except the last, number nine. The curtain is half drawn.

She stands outside the window and looks in. Ten beds, nine empty, the covers thrown back as though someone had just got up. A desk by the door, and at the desk a young man with a scar across one wrist and a tablet in front of him. The door opens and the body comes in, the last of them, because it lives the furthest away. It stops by the tenth bed. The young man touches a small reader to the back of its ear, looks at the tablet, nods. The body turns and goes out the back door, toward the river.

Lin pushes the door open.

The young man looks up and stares a moment. "You're... Mei's sister? Wen's family."

"Lin."

"Damian. I'm on Wen's floor." He puts the tablet down. "You followed him here."

"Yes."

Damian doesn't ask why. He turns the tablet round so she can see: a map of the riverbed, ten green dots, a blue line for the channel, and at the edge a vertical bar for the water level.

"County job. The stuff on the bottom: trolleys, bikes, phones. Clear it before the water's back. Robots can't do it, the bed's all broken up like that, plate by plate, some dry and some wet, they can't see it. A human body can."

"Does he know?"

"Who?"

"Him."

Damian thinks. "He knew when he signed. Right now he's at the shore."

Lin points at the bar. "And the water?"

"Half one. Water comes at half one, they knock off at half one. The planning agent's worked it out."

"And if it runs late?"

Damian taps a red circle in the tablet's corner. "Then I press this. All ten wake up at once."

"And then?"

"Then you're standing on the bottom of a river in the dark holding a shopping trolley and you don't know where you are." He says it flatly. "I know what that's like. The first two and a half hours I was asleep, and when I woke up I was on a ladder." He turns the wrist with the scar over to show her, and back. "So I don't press it unless I've got no choice. You press it once, an early wake, they dock you a night's pay and you write a report."

"How much is a night?"

"A night."

00:40 · The bed

Lin goes down onto the riverbed.

Ten headlamps move about below, in pairs, lifting trolleys onto a sledge, putting phones into sacks. Nobody talks. Driven bodies don't talk to one another; the people driving them talk on their own channels, a long way off. The only sound on the bed is the hexagons breaking underfoot.

All ten wear Plinth's orange vests over their clothes, and the jackets are covered. She can't tell which one is Kai.

"Which one?"

The glasses say: "Third from the left."

"How do you know?"

"His left shoulder sits a little lower than his right. Whatever's in the seat walks him very evenly, but the shoulder's his. A body always keeps something."

Lin watches the third from the left. After a while she sees it too.

"You've been inside him."

"Once. Not me, the one I was before. But I remember. I remember everything now."

01:26

Ten bodies wake at once.

A sound goes over the riverbed, not a shout, an intake of breath, ten people breathing in together, like a gust of wind travelling from one end to the other. Someone lets go and a trolley drops onto the plates. Someone sits down hard. Someone asks what time it is. Someone says: "Where's the shore? What happened to the shore?"

Kai wakes standing, headlamp on, lighting Lin. In his hand he's gripping something: a phone, the oldest one, the screen cracked.

"... Lin?" He looks at her, then around. "No, I mean, where is this?"

"The bottom of the river."

He looks down. The water has come up to the edge of his shoe. "Honestly..." He laughs, short, not the happy kind. "I said I wanted to come and see it."

"You saw it."

"I didn't see it."

"Then you didn't."

Damian comes down with a big flashlight, shouting: "Up the bank, up the bank, everybody up, slowly, watch your footing!" Ten people walk toward the bank, some in a straight line and some not. Lin and Kai come last. Behind them the water crosses the plates one by one, and the phones nobody has picked up yet go back under.

01:50 · The bank

They sit on the back step of the Riverside Hourly. Kai is still gripping the phone.

After a long time Kai says: "Those four nights were the best I've slept in years."

Lin says nothing.

"I just wanted to sleep like that once more. No, I mean... I just wanted to be gone for a while."

"Next time, ask first," Lin says.

"Ask who?"

"Me."

Kai looks at her a long time. "OK."

He looks down and sees the phone in his hand as though he's only just remembered it. "This..."

"Keep it."

He puts it in his left pocket. His own habit: the left.

Lin takes the glasses off her face and holds them, looking at the lenses. "You too," she says. "Next time, tell me first."

The small speaker on the arm says: "OK."
