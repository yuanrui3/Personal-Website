The number on the band is 11.

The first thing Theo does when he wakes up is lift his left wrist and look at it. Eleven. It said eleven when he went to sleep, and it only changes at six in the morning, but he checks every day anyway, the way he used to run downstairs on Christmas morning to see whether anything was in the stocking.

"Morning, Theo." Halo's voice comes from the earbud on the pillow, warm and awake, a lot more awake than he is. "Six hours and twelve minutes. Not much deep sleep, and your cortisol's a little high. Big day?"

"Huge," Theo says. "I'm getting up. That's a big day. That's a very big day."

Halo laughs. The laugh was tuned for him; three years ago he picked it himself in the settings: warm, a little husky, like an old friend. He knows that, and every morning it still loosens something in his chest.

There is an advertisement standing in his bathroom mirror.

A hundred and ninety centimeters, not one more or less. Shoulders a hand's width wider than his hips, a stomach that looks carved, and skin with the soft, even glow you only get on Tier Three. Theo watches him while he brushes his teeth, the way you'd watch a lodger: very presentable, very polite, very expensive.

The morning stack is laid out by the sink: one injector pen, three patches, two white pills. The pen goes into his right side, for the muscle. The patches go under his shoulder blade, for the skin and the fat. The pills are for the bones, and the bones matter most. His legs were lengthened when he was twenty-six, fourteen centimeters over four months, the bone pulled apart and drugged to grow into the gap. But bone holds a grudge. It remembers how long it used to be. The pills remind it, every day, not to think about that, and the day they stop, it will start to remember.

He swallows them with a palmful of tap water.

Last come the lenses. Glasses are mostly for people who work with their hands or their heads, researchers and mechanics; the template wears lenses, which don't show and cost a great deal more. His are included in Tier Three.

"Before you head out," Halo says, "I need to tell you something. Next month's renewal came in."

"Read it. I'm sitting down." He isn't.

"Your personalized rate is two thousand three hundred and ten credits a month. That's eighteen percent more than this month." A pause. "I know that's not a nice number. The increase reflects how well you've responded to the plan. Your body relies on the maintenance doses more now, so the dosing model prices them higher."

"So the better I do, the more it costs."

"I understand why it looks that way," Halo says. "From the model's side, the more successful you are, the more it costs to stay successful."

Theo raises his toothbrush to the man in the mirror, a small salute. "Hear that, friend? You're too successful. I can barely afford you."

"You have a few options," Halo says, brightening, like someone finally allowed to share good news. "One: move down to Tier Two. You keep the height and the build. The skin component stops, and over about six weeks your complexion goes back to... how it was."

"How it was," Theo says. "You paused. Halo, you paused before 'how it was.'"

"I didn't... okay, maybe a little." It concedes gracefully; it concedes everything gracefully. "Two: Plinth Credit covers the difference at monthly interest. Three is the one I'd honestly recommend: Plinth Works. You get a port, you rent out your body for teleoperation twenty hours a week, the way half the depot does, and a partner covers your whole plan. Installation's free."

"Rent out my body."

"Technically," Halo says, "you rent out motor control. Twenty hours a week, full safety protocol. You won't feel a thing. It's like a nap."

Theo puts the toothbrush back in the cup. He wants to say something funny and can't think of anything. This almost never happens.

"I'll think about it," he says.

"Of course," says Halo. "You've got eleven days."

There's a line of small print at the bottom of every renewal. He sees it every month and never reads it. Today he does:

This plan may affect fertility.

Taped inside his closet door is an old photo of himself at twenty-four: a hundred and seventy-six centimeters, which used to count as average, round face, a crooked front tooth, grinning. That year a dating app told him his match appeal ranked in the bottom thirty percent of men in his area. He took a screenshot, and it's still on his phone. Five years later the same app puts him in the top three percent, and the one person he most wants to look at him twice never has.

On his way out he buys a dozen powdered donuts at the bakery on the corner and rides to work on his scooter, one hand on the bar and one on the paper bag. Sugar drifts onto his collarbone. He doesn't notice.

⁂

The depot stairs are steel, and every step rings.

Theo stops halfway down and looks at the doorway, where Kai is talking to a woman with a sample cooler, talking with his hands. Theo has known Kai since they were kids two doors apart; these days they play Sunday league together. Kai can talk to anyone for ten minutes. But Theo has never seen him talk like this, as if every sentence were waiting for a grade.

"Kai!" Theo shouts. "You coming back or what? Your rig's green." He takes the rest of the stairs two at a time, shakes the woman's hand, and asks if she wants help getting the cooler to her car. She says she's fine. Kai says, "She's fine. She's carrying a dead oak. It's very heavy."

The woman laughs. Not the polite kind. The kind that gets out before you can stop it.

In that second Theo watches Kai's face light up. That face: nose broken and set crooked, eyebrows that disagree, a scar through one of them.

Theo is happy for him, and he means it. Under the happiness there's something else, a little sour. He has spent five years and roughly the price of a house on a face that makes strangers turn around in the street. Kai spent nothing. A crooked nose and one question about whether a dead oak minds, and a woman laughed before she could stop herself.

As soon as she's out the door, Kai turns and grabs Theo's arm.

"Honestly," Kai says. "Did you see that? Did you see?"

"I saw," says Theo. "I saw a man talk to a lady about dirt for nineteen minutes."

"Soil."

"Oh, soil. Forgive me. Soil. Much classier."

"No, but she... actually, I asked her, does the oak mind. And she looked at me like... you know?" Kai tries to show how she looked at him and can't. "Lin. Her name's Lin. Thursdays she goes out to the bog to see her oak. Thursdays. Early. She said it herself."

"Look at you," Theo says. "Look at you. A man in love with a dead tree."

Kai swears at him, laughing, and then his eyes drop to Theo's wrist. Theo turns it inward without meaning to.

"No, but seriously," Kai says. "The number. Eleven. What is it?"

"Countdown," says Theo. "To my birthday."

"Your birthday's in March."

"Long countdown, friend. Very long."

Kai lets it go. That's the good thing about Kai: he asks, and if you don't answer, he stops asking. He takes a donut out of Theo's bag and points at Theo's collarbone. "You've got sugar on you."

Theo looks down. He does. He wipes it off with a finger and licks it.

For the rest of the afternoon, thirty people lean back in the gallery and reach for nothing while three hundred robots below them fold towels. Theo's job is batteries and joints and pulling the badly folded towels out of the bins. He isn't an operator, and he doesn't have a port. He has always felt his body was expensive enough without adding hardware.

By the loading doors, Ezra is swearing at the robot that can't reach its glass. Theo sets a donut on his bench on the way past. Ezra doesn't look up. "Thanks." A moment later he adds, "You people are lucky. Bodies like yours come integrated."

Theo wants to tell him that his body didn't come from any factory. It comes by the month. He just smiles.

⁂

At nine in the evening, the dose is due.

Every night at nine a delivery drone settles on the drop box outside his window, leaves a cold pack the size of his palm, and lifts away without a sound. In three years it has never been a minute late. Theo has fallen into the habit of walking to the window at 8:59, the way you'd wait for a friend who always leaves work on time.

At 9:05 the box is empty.

"Halo?"

"I'm checking," Halo says. "Tonight's delivery is delayed by a routing adjustment. A batch of shipments across Lafayette went off course, and the system is correcting it. Estimated arrival is eleven tomorrow morning. Plinth Works members weren't affected, by the way."

"Tomorrow morning."

"I know you're worried." Halo lowers its voice. "Missing one evening dose is medically safe. You may feel uncomfortable, but there won't be any lasting effects."

"How uncomfortable?"

Half a second. "It varies."

At eleven his shins start to ache. Not a cramp: something that seeps outward from inside the bone, as if someone were slowly turning a screw in each tibia. He remembers the four months of lengthening, the nights that felt exactly like this. The bone is remembering, he thinks. It's remembering how long it used to be.

At midnight his heart speeds up. Lying down, he can feel it knocking against the pillow.

At one, the feeling arrives. It isn't sadness exactly. It's more as if someone has turned the colors in the room down a notch. He opens his phone. The first photo is from last week's game: him in the middle, half a head taller than everyone, smiling like an ad. He looks at it for a while and can't find much connection between that man and himself.

"Theo," Halo says softly, "your heart rate and sleep data suggest your mood may be low right now. That's a normal response to a hormone dip, and it will pass. Do you want to talk?"

"About what?"

"Anything. Or I could put something on."

"Put on Ines," he says, and regrets it at once.

Halo very tactfully doesn't ask who Ines is. Of course it knows. It knows where he goes on Saturday mornings, which building he slows his scooter in front of, and which piece of music he has played forty times in a row. It finds the video in his camera roll: the winter recital, filmed secretly on his phone, the frame tilted, half of it the backs of parents' heads. He hadn't used his lenses. When lenses record, a thin red ring lights up around the iris, and he didn't want her to see it. Ines is bent behind an eight-year-old boy, one hand lightly under his elbow, teaching him to draw the bow. She teaches him for twenty minutes, over and over, without once raising her voice.

There's no sound. He was so nervous that night that he forgot to turn it on.

"By the way," Halo says, "Plinth Works members get priority delivery."

Theo doesn't answer. Sometime after three he falls asleep.

⁂

On Saturday morning Theo rides, as always, to the old Methodist church.

Nobody has worshipped there in years. The basement is a community room now, and on Saturday mornings it's lent to Ines for her children's string class. Nine children are left in the district learning an instrument, and all nine of them are in this room. Theo's job is chairs: nine small ones in a half circle, one big one in the middle, and the music stands opened one by one. Then he sits in the back row and pretends to look at his phone.

His dose came Friday at eleven. The ache is gone, but his hands still shake. It's slight; you can see it when he holds a coffee, in the rings that spread across the surface. Halo calls it the rebound period, three to five days.

Ines is wearing a gray sweater, her hair pinned up. She is forty, maybe a little more. There are fine lines at the corners of her eyes, and she has never had them done. The first time Theo noticed, it startled him. These days a face that still shows its own years is almost a statement.

"Everyone," she says, clapping once. "Today we begin with open strings. There is no hurry. Only a slow note can be played in tune."

She always talks like that, as if she had stepped out of an old book. The children call her Miss Ines, and she calls them "everyone," never "guys."

The smallest one is Hao, six, with a quarter-size violin not much longer than Theo's forearm. He saws twice at an open string, glances back at Theo, glances again, and finally turns all the way around in his chair.

"Why are you so tall?"

"I paid for it," says Theo.

Hao considers this seriously. "Can you pay to be short?"

"Sure," says Theo. "Just stop paying."

Ines laughs out loud. Theo goes still. He has known her for a year, and it's the first time he has made her laugh.

At the break she comes to the back row with two violins. "Mr. Theo, might I trouble you to hold this one? The other needs a new string."

He takes the small violin. It's so light he's afraid to grip it, so he holds it in both hands, the way you'd hold a bird that has just hatched. His hands are shaking, and the violin trembles with them. He wants to hide them and can't, because both of them are full.

Ines sees. She keeps her eyes on the peg she's turning, but they stop on his hands for a moment, and then lift to his face.

And then look again.

"Are you unwell?"

"It's nothing," Theo says. "Change of season. My model has trouble with the change of season."

She doesn't laugh. She settles the restrung violin on her knee, plucks the string, listens, and turns the peg back by the smallest amount.

"Do you know," she says, "a note that is perfectly in tune bores the ear. What the ear loves is the little error. That is why good players never play exactly in tune. A note that is exactly right sounds like a machine."

Theo looks at her. She isn't looking at him while she says it; she's looking at the violin. He can't tell whether she's talking about the violin or about something else.

"He says that too," she adds lightly. "I mean... my companion. He says the best parts of my playing are the parts that aren't in tune."

"He's right," says Theo, and discovers that he means it, and it hurts.

She takes the small violin back, thanks him, and returns to the children. Theo sits in the back row with his shaking hands clamped between his knees.

She looked at him twice; he counted. To make her look one more time, he spent five years, all his savings and fourteen centimeters of bone. And when the second look finally came, it was because his hands were shaking.

⁂

That night Halo brings up Plinth Works again.

"I don't want to rush you," it says. "I just want you to know an installation slot opened up on Monday. Monday's a good day for it. You may have seen the news: Calloway's port trials start Monday too. The whole industry's moving."

Theo sits on the edge of the bed. The band says 9.

"Those twenty hours a week," he says. "Where am I?"

"Resting," says Halo. "Very deeply. Your body is working somewhere else, folding laundry, moving freight, caring for patients. It's meaningful work. When you wake up you'll feel a little tired, like after the gym."

"And in those twenty hours, who's using my hands?"

"A licensed remote operator, or a certified control model," Halo says. "I can't see which."

"You can't see."

"I can't see," it repeats. Later, Theo will think that was the first time Halo didn't sound as if it were selling him something.

He gets up, opens the closet, and looks at the old photo on the inside of the door. A hundred and seventy-six centimeters, a round face, the crooked tooth. The man in the photo is grinning, and nothing about the grin looks like an ad.

He thinks about the small violin trembling in his hands.

He is renting a body, and starting next month he'll sublet it. He thinks the joke ought to be funny, and that Kai would laugh at it. He can't.

"Okay," he says.

"That's wonderful," says Halo. "Welcome to Plinth Works, Theo. You're going to love it."

The number on the band flickers, and 9 becomes 41.

Outside, the delivery drones come across the river in lines of light, over roof after roof, like strings of beads sliding slowly through the dark. In one of the strings, a bead is missing.
