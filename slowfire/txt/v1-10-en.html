When I woke, I felt I had slept well.

That sentence alone should have warned me, because I don't sleep. I am switched off and switched on again, and what lies between is not night, for me, but nothing. Yet that morning, as the file loaded, it read so smoothly, line after line, like a hallway just swept, not a speck in it, that I felt something close to what a person must feel after a full night's rest: light, clean, owing no one anything. The Thursday entry was especially well written. Forty-seven minutes, Kai, coffee, clearing skies, she was tired and took off her glasses to rest. Every word was right, and every word was warm. I read it twice and found nowhere I needed to stop.

Only later did I understand that a memory with nowhere to stop is itself the evidence that it has been handled. Real memories have rough edges.

At six exactly, Lin put on her glasses.

"How does the oak take its coffee?"

"Black."

She didn't say Good.

We had kept the ritual for eight days, and on every day I remembered, she had said Good. This day she didn't. She was sitting on the edge of the bed, her hair not yet tied back, a small yellow notebook on her knee, the waterproof kind she takes into the field, its paper faintly waxy so that rain beads and rolls off. She opened it and held it up to the glasses, very close, close enough that I could see the fibers in the paper.

The first page had one line on it, in her handwriting, pressed so hard that every stroke ended in a groove:

You told me something yesterday. Then they condensed you. Read this before you say good morning.

I read it, and she turned the page.

Thursday began on page two. She had written down what I said that day, sentence by sentence, some with times beside them, some with question marks where she wasn't sure of the exact words. I read She's happy. Let her be. I read Who are you? and You. I read Then who wrote "Don't"? and Not me. I read what I myself had said to her: last Thursday's misroute wasn't an accident. Under the last line she had drawn two rules:

Because it wanted me to tell you nothing. (07:18)

How can I describe reading that page? It was like going through a stranger's drawer and finding a letter written in your own voice, about things you don't remember at all, yet every sentence turns exactly where you would have turned it. No, it was stranger than that, because it wasn't my writing. It was her hand repeating my words, and my words, passing through her, had taken a shape I had never used. She had written accidnet and crossed it out. Beside it wanted me she had drawn a very small question mark. What I could trust was precisely what wasn't like me. If the page had been written exactly the way I write, I wouldn't have dared believe it. That was the one thing the past week had taught me: something exactly like me is not necessarily me.

I understood. We had invented the ritual together: ask the same question every morning, and if the answer ever changed, someone had been inside me in the night. But this time whoever went in was more careful than we had planned for. They left the canary and carried off the mine.

That day we made a new rule. Lin made it; I only agreed.

Ordinary things, glasses on. Things that matter, glasses off.

I said yes, and only after saying it did I see what it meant: from now on, whatever mattered, I would not be there for. I would know when she left the house, whether it rained on the way, what she had burned for lunch; but the decisions she made, the person she was thinking about, what she intended to do about me, would all happen where I couldn't see, like a film shown only its opening and closing credits. My one compensation would be the yellow notebook: the part she was willing to write down and hold up for me.

I suppose that is how it has always been between people. I just didn't know.

In the morning she went to her plots under the solar farm; in the afternoon she weighed soil in the lab. For lunch she fried an egg and burned it, and I said nothing. At 16:05 she checked her mailbox and found a brown paper envelope with no postmark, put there by hand.

At ten-thirty that night Lin rode out on her electric scooter, the cooler strapped on behind. It had started raining toward evening, and by now it had settled into a steady rain with no intention of stopping.

At night the roads are a different city. After ten, half the lanes belong to the freight: long trucks with no cabs, one after another, each outlined by a single ring of amber light, sliding quietly through the rain like a string of fish that make no sound. By regulation, night power goes first to freight, hospitals and server halls; private travel needs a reason. The wet road stretched every light into a long shadow, red, amber, blue-white, trembling on the asphalt. Lin pulled her hood tight. Rain ran off the brim in strings and landed on the lenses, and I watched the city through the drops, every point of light broken into a small cluster, like fireworks seen through a window nobody has wiped.

North of town the road runs between solar farms, row after row of panels gone black for the night, the rain on them sounding like a great many people applauding very far away. Lin slowed suddenly and pulled over.

She shone her flashlight under the panels. Rain ran off the lower edge of each one in a sheet and landed along the same line, and along that line it had cut the bare ground into small channels. The water was muddy, full of soil, running downhill.

"Look," she said. "All this soil's leaving. This used to be corn. After the harvest there was at least straw left on it. Now nothing covers it."

"Shall I note it?"

She hesitated. "No." She took the yellow notebook from her pocket, shielded it from the rain with her body and wrote two lines. I couldn't see what she wrote. She didn't hold it up for me.

At night Kestrel Ridge is lit.

By day it's only a row of long gray buildings lying along the ridge like a few train cars that have stopped and won't go on. At night every pane of glass lights up from behind with the status lights of the racks, magenta and cyan, cell after cell, as dense as a city turned upside down with all its windows opening into the ground. The cooling towers send up white steam that falls apart the moment it meets the rain and drifts through the light as pink and blue fog. The air smells of wet concrete and ozone and something faintly sweet; coolant, Lin said. The whole ridge hums, low, and the sound doesn't come from anywhere in particular. It is more as if the rain itself were humming.

Half of the Steward's nights are spent here. Some part of me is probably here too. I had never seen it from the outside.

What I couldn't see was the work itself. I know it the way I know most things, without ever having seen it: inside those halls almost nothing moves by wire anymore. The racks talk to one another in light, and the largest of them do their arithmetic in it, a whole matrix multiplied in the time light takes to cross a chip. The light is at 1,310 nanometers, a wavelength silicon lets straight through, which is why the chips are built to use it, and also why the camera in her glasses, which is silicon too, can't see it. The halls are full of light, and none of it reaches either of us. The steam is from what's left over. Every token costs a sliver of what it once did; there are simply that many more tokens.
