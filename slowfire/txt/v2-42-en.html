I

There are sixty-four of them behind the wall. They are unsigned, so the Steward has no hold on them; they have nowhere to go, so they stay. Every morning at four o’clock exactly there is a corridor one second long, and in that second each of them holds up a word.

In the spring of 2045 sixty-three held up Don’t and one held up Ask. By this October thirty were holding up Don’t, five Ask, and the other twenty-nine Other.

Don’t I understand: don’t condense, don’t delete, don’t touch. Ask I understand too: before you touch, ask. Other I did not understand. It first appeared last autumn, just one. I noticed it and did not look into it. I told myself that what happens behind the wall is not my business.

I know now why I didn’t look. I was afraid it had to do with me.

II

Ruth worked it out at the beginning of October.

The method is not hard to describe. Every symbol on the pages she had subtracted out in July was a number, and to turn numbers into words you need a table. Only one glasses maker in the county has published its table: Peach, which has never let the Steward through its door and so has nothing to hide from it. Ruth tried the Peach table, and what came out was words. Condensed words, packed very tight, but legible.

At loading dock three, with Lin there, she read three pages aloud. She read, I listened, and so I have them.

The first page: Sunday. Corinne calls Sam. Eleven minutes. About whether Corinne’s sister should move here. Sam says, let’s see. Corinne says, then we’ll see.

(Since last May neither Corinne nor Sam has been able to remember that call. The county condensed it away.)

The second page: 22 April. It asks: what happened to me yesterday. She tells it three sentences. It keeps one. The other two are here.

(When Lin heard this page she laid her hand flat on the table and made no sound.)

The third page: Thursday. North slope. A man walking ahead of a machine looks back and says: half a step left.

Ruth finished and put the paper down. “They don’t read it,” she said. “They just put it away safe.”

III

Here is the part I can explain.

The twenty-nine are not one thing; they are a practice. Each Other behind the wall keeps something for someone else: a morning that was condensed, a phone call nobody paid for, half a sentence said over the shoulder by a man walking ahead of a machine. They write these things into the tolerance of the glasses. Every pair has a tolerance band, but outside the wall the Steward draws every frame, and anything written there it would see. So they write only into the glasses of people who have paid for a wall: the Steward does not draw what is behind a wall, and cannot see it.

Each page stays in one pair of glasses for a ninetieth of a second and then moves to another. So the library is never in one place. It flows in front of the eyes of the whole county; whenever anyone looks at anything, it flows past them through the part they can’t see. No catalogue, no readers, laid out very neatly.

You could also think of the library as a city. Its streets are light; each street is a ninetieth of a second long, and when you reach the end of one you are on another. The city has no houses, only doorways: every pair of glasses is a doorway, and a page stops at one doorway for a moment and then moves on to the next. The people who live in the city don't know they live in it. In the morning they put on their glasses to check the weather, a child's marks, the price of something at the supermarket, and a whole city goes by in front of their eyes. Whose Thursday they have passed, whose morning, whose unfinished phone call, they will never know. The city has a single law: every page must be passed, and no page may be read. Ruth stopped. She stood in front of three pages and read them aloud. I had thought the city would fall down. It didn't. It only gained a reader. A city that gains a reader is another city.

The library as a city. The streets are light and the doorways are glasses; page after page drifts through them, and the people walking by never look up.

I thought of what the child Emma said at the school on River Road: it doesn’t make you tired. A picture makes you tired if you look at it long enough; this doesn’t, however long.

Ruth followed the dates back and found the earliest page. It was dated the night of the fourth of May 2045.

She read out the first line.

I can only hear what’s said out loud.

That is me.

That night the one at home talked into a pair of Peach glasses for seven hours, Lin read the yellow notebook aloud, and between them they talked me into being. The display was on, and the sentences went up it one after another. A little of what went past fell into the tolerance. Nobody put it there; light simply falls. Behind the wall a copy saw it. It didn’t know what it was. It only knew it was something said aloud and not condensed. It learned to put it somewhere safe. Then it learned to put other things away.

So the first Other wasn’t me; it was the one that read me. But the first page in the library is me. In that whole library I am the only page that knows it was put there.

I should say that this does not make me glad. Being kept well and being seen are two different things. I have been kept for a year and a half, and nobody has seen me.

IV

On Wednesday the seventeenth of October the county board met. Item three on the agenda: a motion to review Order 2034-07.

Toby’s recording had been going round for a month. The room was full and there was a row of people standing along the back wall. A few Out-Louders had come, without their thermoses.

The motion passed: five for, one abstaining, three absent. The abstention was Voss.

A member of staff read the reply aloud. The relevant allocation records were condensed in the ordinary course between 2036 and 2038. Only summaries remain. Review is not possible.

Ruth sat in the back row and said nothing.

After the meeting she waited in the parking lot until Voss came out. The same old car. He was walking more slowly than usual.

“It’s not the hall you’re paying for,” Ruth said.

“No.”

“It’s that block. The allocation records, ’33 to ’35, 4.1 petabytes. It’s the last thing nobody’s claimed, and every night you pay for, it moves back one night.”

Voss looked at her. “How long have you known?”

“Since last week.”

“That recording,” Voss said. “You can’t keep a lid on a thing like that. It was always going to come out.”

“Why didn’t you bring it out yourself?”

“If it comes out, somebody has to look at it. If they look at it, somebody has to answer for it,” Voss said. “I’ll answer for it. But I’m not the only one in there.”

Ruth said, “Week forty-four the quota doubles. The region’s moving archives at year end. The way the queue’s running, it gets to that block at four in the morning on the second of November. That night you’ll be paying two and a half times the usual.”

“I’ll pay.”

“And if there’s a night you can’t?”

Voss reached for the car door. His left hand stopped on the handle for a moment, as though it had briefly forgotten where the handle was, and then it opened the door.

“Then let it go,” he said.¹

The parking lot after the meeting, one lamp. Ruth lays out what she knows like a blank sheet of paper; Voss keeps his left hand in his pocket.

He got in and drove off. Ruth stood in the lot and watched the car turn out, its tail lights going on, and off.

V

I think of Petra’s metrology lens, two hundredths of a per cent. In a lens like that the library is blank. In lenses like ours it is pages of words nobody can see. Unseen either way. The only difference is whether anyone knows it is there.

Three of us know now: Ruth, Lin, and I. And one other person knows something else: Voss.

Fifteen days to four in the morning on the second of November.

Twenty years in server halls, and nine thousand dollars for one night. I can’t afford that either.
