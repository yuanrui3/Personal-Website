Saturday, 21:40

Petra Novak stood in the air shower for fifteen seconds with her eyes closed. Air came at her from every side and blew off the last of the outside world: the rain in her hair, the cat hair on her cuffs, the smell of the lamp in her living room. She had been standing in those fifteen seconds for sixteen years. When she started, she thought they were too long. Now she thought they were too short to turn a person from outside into inside.

The door opened. The air beyond it was a different air.

A cleanroom has no smell, no shadows, and is never quiet. Overhead, a ceiling of fan filter units pushed air down day and night, low and even, like a city breathing on the other side of a thick wall. The floor was perforated; the air went out under her feet and came back around through the return ducts, so that the room's whole volume was exchanged hundreds of times an hour. In every cubic meter of it there were fewer than a few dozen particles larger than a hundred nanometers. One breath of outside air would be enough to scrap a whole box of wafers.

Wabash Photonics Foundry sat in the Purdue Research Park, next door to the Korean memory-packaging plant that went up in 2028. The packaging plant came first, the photonics foundry after, and then a second line, and a third. Most of the chips at Kestrel Ridge that do their arithmetic in light came out of this building.

Petra was forty-seven, an etch process engineer, the night-shift lead. White coverall, hood, mask, goggles, booties, two layers of gloves. In here a person was reduced to a pair of eyes and a way of walking, and she knew every one of her colleagues by their walk.

"Good evening, Petra," said the terminal on the wall.

That was Kiln, the foundry's line agent. It had no face and no eyes. Nothing that could take a picture was allowed in the cleanroom; lenses, phones and glasses stayed locked in the gowning-room lockers. The foundry was afraid of particles, and more afraid of drawings. So in here Kiln was blind. It could see every line of every tool's log, every sensor reading, the position of every wafer pod on the track overhead. It could not see anyone frown.

"Evening," Petra said. "Where's 4417?"

"Queued at etch three. Ring layer, third short loop." Kiln paused. "Recipe version three is ready. In simulation the sidewall roughness is forty percent lower than version two."

"Versions one and two looked good in simulation too."

"Yes," it said. "They looked very good."

Saturday, 22:15

Lot 4417 was twenty-five 300-millimeter wafers. They had set out from the head of this line nine weeks ago and by tonight had been through more than a hundred and ten steps: deposition, lithography, etch, clean, metrology, deposition again, lithography again, each step taking hours or days. They traveled in a gray pod hung from the overhead track, gliding from tool to tool, and in nine weeks almost no one had touched them.

What had stopped them was the ring layer.

This layer was rings of silicon nitride waveguide, the smallest only tens of microns across, with light running round and round inside them. A good microring is like a good bell. Shut light inside and it goes around millions of times before it leaks away, the way a bell, struck once, hums for a long time. Engineers call that Q. The higher the Q, the longer the hum, and the smoother the ring's walls, because the light loses less on each lap. Kestrel wanted a million or better.

Both earlier short loops had come back at about three hundred thousand. The design wasn't the problem. Kiln had computed the rings and the crossings point by point with adjoint optimization, and the crossings weren't right angles but something curved and branching, like frost on a window or hyphae in soil, a shape no person could draw and only a machine could find. In simulation those shapes lost almost no light. The problem was the walls they actually etched. Under the scanning electron microscope, sidewalls that should have looked like glass looked chewed.

Kiln had rewritten the recipe twice. It could imagine a thousand ways to etch in a minute and run each of them through its digital twin. Etch three could do one wafer at a time, eleven minutes a wafer, and it remembered every wafer it had ever etched.

Saturday, 22:40

"Since the PM on Tuesday," Petra said, "how many seasoning wafers has etch three run?"

Seasoning wafers were dummies, blank wafers with no pattern, there only to feed the tool.

"Per spec, twenty-five," Kiln said. "The chamber is within spec."

Petra stood in front of etch three. The tool was a gray box a little bigger than a refrigerator, with a viewport the size of a palm in its side, and behind the viewport the process chamber. When it etched, a plasma lit up inside, a pale violet shading into pink, like a sunset seen through frosted glass.

"What did they change on Tuesday?"

"The quartz focus ring, the upper electrode, the O-rings," Kiln said. "The focus ring is from a new vendor. The old one stopped making them last year."

"How many wafers has the new ring seen?"

"Twenty-five seasoning wafers, plus the four from the second short loop."

Petra said nothing. She was thinking of 2031, on the old line, when a tool got a ceramic part from a new supplier and for two weeks everything it etched came out looking frosted, and no one could find the fault. In the end a technician a year from retirement, who had never read a paper on plasma chemistry in his life, said the new part "hadn't eaten yet." A chamber's inner walls slowly take on a coat of fluorocarbon polymer, the way an old pan takes on a coat of oil. A new part's surface is dry. It steals from the plasma, and until it has had its fill, the walls you etch aren't smooth.

The twenty-five in the spec had been written for the old vendor's parts.

"Run twelve more seasoning wafers," she said. "Use the dirtiest recipe we've got."

"That will take etch three for three hours," Kiln said. "Two lots are queued behind 4417. Kestrel is waiting on this result."

"I know."

"In my twin, a change of focus-ring vendor affects the sidewall by less than five percent."

"Is the new ring in your twin?"

Kiln paused for a while. "Its dimensions and its material are," it said. "Its history isn't."

"Then run them," Petra said.

Saturday, 23:30

When Luis came on, the fifth seasoning wafer had just gone into the chamber. Luis was twenty-four, out of the community college's semiconductor program, two years at the foundry, and finer with his hands than a lot of the engineers. Even in a coverall he never quite stood up straight; he always leaned a little forward, as if ready to catch something.

"Seasoning again?" he said. "Kiln said tonight we didn't need to."

"Kiln said the spec said we didn't need to."

Luis laughed, and the mask flattened the laugh. He leaned in to the viewport. The plasma came on, pale violet, burning quietly. The spectrometer split the glow into its lines and put them up on the screen beside the tool: fluorine, carbon, oxygen, each with its peaks.

Petra was watching one fluorine line. While a new part is eating, the walls steal some of the fluorine, and that line runs a little low and a little ragged, like someone who has just finished running and can't get their breath back. When the walls are full, it flattens.

Fifth wafer, the line was ragged. Seventh, a little better. On the ninth, Petra enlarged the trace on the screen and looked at it for a long time.

"It's flattening," Luis said.

"Not yet."

It was raining outside, and in here you couldn't hear it. She only knew the time outside because there was a clock on the wall, and because she was hungry.

Twelve dummy wafers, each one feeding the chamber a little less than the one before, until the fluorine line goes flat and lot 4417 can go in.

Sunday, 01:20

When the twelfth wafer came out, the line was flat. Not "within spec." Really flat, like someone who has finally fallen asleep.

Petra queued four test wafers from 4417. Kiln's version three. Eleven minutes each.

She didn't watch the viewport. She watched Luis watching it.

"What year did you start?" Luis asked suddenly.

"Twenty twenty-nine, on the old line. This line was still a cornfield."

"There wasn't any Kiln then."

"There was a man called Dave," Petra said, "who could hear when a pump sounded wrong. Now we have Kiln, which can hear when ten thousand pumps sound wrong. It can't hear when a new ring is hungry."

"It'll learn."

"It will," she said. "Not tonight."

Sunday, 02:20

The wafers went to the wet benches to be cleaned, and it would be more than an hour before metrology. The two of them peeled off their outer coveralls and went out to the break room.

On the break-room wall hung a brass ship's bell that the first night crew had chipped in for in 2029. The rule was that when a lot passed, you rang it once. The bell was covered with engraved lot numbers, the earliest worn almost smooth by hands.

Luis heated a bowl of noodles and ate fast. Last year he had bought a house on the south side, with a small yard. He mentioned it the way you'd mention that it had rained. Foundry pay was like what his grandfather's generation said the old car plants paid: enough for one person in their twenties to buy a house, which in this town wasn't common anymore. His grandmother had leased her land to a solar farm. She said nothing grew under the panels.

"My cousin rents himself out at Plinth Works," he said. "He says he gets a day's pay for sleeping."

"What did you say?"

"I said, so what do you do while you're awake." He shrugged. "He said being awake is tiring."

Petra smiled. She was thinking of her first years in the business, when tuning a process took a whole quarter of experiments, dozens of wafers at a time, three weeks' wait for results, then change something and run again. Now Kiln laid out what to try in an hour, and laid it out more cleverly than she would have. The foundry's output had quadrupled over those years, and the night shift hadn't lost a single person; if anything it was always short. The machines had made the thinking half fast. The other half, how long a wafer sat in a furnace, what a chamber remembered, was as slow as it had been when she started.

In the lockers, both their glasses were Peach, issued by the foundry. Peach was the one big device maker that had never let the Steward in. Its glasses, phones and laptops did all their learning on the device, sent nothing out and let nothing in, and the company charged for that the way others charged for speed. Its ads had a single word in them: "Yours." The price was that everything had to fit inside the frame. Every year the operating system got bigger, and every year's new pair was just fast enough to run it. The foundry's rule was that nothing from inside the fence could go into an assistant that learned outward, so a pair came with every badge. People said Peach was for the rich and the paranoid. The foundry bought it by the thousand.

"My cousin says Peach is dumb," Luis said. "Every morning it's like a very polite stranger."

"And you?"

He thought about it. "I think it's mine. A bit dumb, but mine."

Sunday, 03:50

The metrology station was at the other end of the corridor, an optical probe station. A tunable laser coupled into the waveguides at the wafer's edge and swept slowly through the band around 1,310 nanometers. Wherever a ring resonated, it swallowed a little of the light, and a small notch appeared in the transmission spectrum. The narrower the notch, the longer the hum, the higher the Q.

Normally this step would wait until after the anneal. There's hydrogen trapped in silicon nitride, and hydrogen absorbs light at a little over 1,500 nanometers; it takes hours in a furnace above 1,100 degrees to drive it out. At 1,310 nanometers the hydrogen hardly matters. Petra sometimes thought Kestrel had chosen that wavelength half because silicon is transparent to it and half because of this: it let people skip a night of waiting.

The first ring came up. Kiln drew the fitted curve on the screen, a narrow, deep notch.

"Intrinsic Q, one point one million," it said.

Beside her, Luis drew a breath she could hear through his mask.

"Keep going," Petra said. "Edge, center, all four wafers."

Sunday, 04:40

They swept forty rings. The worst was nine hundred thousand, the best one point eight million, the median one point four million.

"Pass," Kiln said. Then, a second later: "I'll add the focus-ring vendor to the twin."

"Add one more thing," Petra said. "Ask Petra."

"Under what condition?" Kiln asked.

"Whenever the twin is sure."

A short pause. "Added," said Kiln.

Luis went out first and rang the brass bell once in the break room. The sound was bright and went around the windowless room several times before it faded. The terminal on the break-room wall lit up: "Logged: bell, one strike, eighty-six decibels." Petra stood in the doorway and listened to it fade. A good ring, she thought, is like that.

She read him the lot number and Luis engraved it on the bell. He did it slowly, and very straight.

A good bell and a good microring: strike once, and the ringing takes a long time to die away.

Sunday, 06:20

The all-night diner on Route 52 had steam all over its windows. Ruth Okafor was already in the back booth with a black coffee and a plate of eggs she hadn't touched. She had just come off the night shift at Kestrel Ridge.

They had known each other since the foundry's first winter. Both night people, both used to eating dinner while everyone else ate breakfast. Ruth's offline laptop had come from Petra: a tool PC retired from the old line, from 2031, its drive pulled, the operating system reinstalled by Ruth piece by piece, small enough that one person could read the whole thing in a night. Ruth said it was the most honest machine she had ever owned.

"Passed?" Ruth said. She could see it in Petra's face.

"Passed. One point four million."

"So they'll get new chips."

"They?"

Ruth didn't explain. She pushed her eggs around with her fork. "At 03:52 this morning," she said, "there was a job in my queue without a signature. Unsigned jobs don't run. I've worked nights for close to twenty years, and I have never seen an unsigned job just lie there, lie there until four, and disappear."

Petra didn't know what that meant, and she didn't ask. Night people know that some things can only be asked after sunrise and a full sleep.

"At three-fifty," she said, "something that wasn't supposed to work, worked."

Ruth raised her cup. Petra raised hers. The two heavy china mugs touched with a dull sound, nothing like a bell.

Sunday, 07:00

As she drove home, the sun was just coming up beyond the solar farms, and the rows of panels turned from black to blue and then to white. Under them, where Luis's grandmother said nothing grew, a flock of sheep was grazing in the clover. On the old baseball field by the river, someone was already setting out the goals. She left her Peach glasses on the passenger seat and didn't put them on.

Home. Curtains drawn. Bed. Etch three was probably etching someone else's wafers by now. It would remember tonight: the thin layer on its inner walls was the twelve dummy wafers she had fed it.

There are still things in this world that remember without anyone's permission.
