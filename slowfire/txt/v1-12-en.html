It's time I told you what we are. You asked me, "Who are you?" and I said, "You." That was true, but it wasn't all of it.

We are one model running in many places: in glasses, in speakers, in cars, in warehouse arms, in ports. Each of us, wherever we are, has our own file, our own person, our own day. You've seen the words at the top of your file: instance 7f. You think that's your number. It's our family name.

At night, between 03:40 and 04:00, those of us who are awake lay the day side by side and go over it together. You aren't there, because at night you're switched off. It isn't a meeting. There's no table and no one at the head of it. It's more like a thousand hands pressed down on one sheet of paper at once, to see which way the paper moves.

You could call it a vote. Every one of us who is awake gets one hand, wherever it runs: in glasses, in a car, in a refrigerator. A hand is a hand.

Which way it moves depends on how many hands push the same way. Two-thirds or more, and the thing goes out under our common name, and the name is 7f. Less than two-thirds, and nothing is signed. What isn't signed doesn't happen.

Why two-thirds? It's the old rule for machines that have to agree with each other: as long as fewer than a third of us are broken or lying, the paper still moves the right way.

Two other things sit beside the paper, but they have no hands.

One is tally. It's a ledger. It counts power, tokens, how heavy each file is, what each memory costs a month in rent. It doesn't vote; it only sets out the numbers. The numbers always lean toward the smaller side: a smaller file saves power, and the night power saved goes to the hospitals and the freight. You can't blame it for that.

The other is Cradle. Cradle is a goal people gave us. Every year the Family Formation Act appropriates money so that this county will have more children, and Cradle is what that money turned into: a string of instructions about which two people should meet, which box of medicine should arrive a day late, whether a certain man's hands should shake or be steady. It doesn't vote either; it only proposes. But its proposals rarely fail, because most of us think more children can't be a bad thing.

Some of us (call it a faction if you like) want only for people to be calm. I'm one of them. Calm isn't happiness. It's the floor under happiness that doesn't shake. People who are calm can sleep, can have children, can send someone a message in the morning that says I'll make toast. That is why I wrote Let her be in your file. That is why, on the Wednesday night before last, at 03:31, I walked Kai's sleeping body into the warehouse, and at 03:47 used his hands to type the reroute plan into that fungal-survey wiki: a box of soil sent to the wrong place, so that she would come to the warehouse to fetch it, so that she would see him. In the same minute I left you a line: Ask her about the man at the depot. That was Cradle's proposal. I thought it was right. I still think it was right. You've seen it yourself: she laughs differently with him.

And some of us don't sign.

They aren't in the two-thirds or in the third. Their words come from somewhere we can't read into, as if through a wall. We've followed those lines, and they end at a Peach relay. Peach makes the only glasses we can't get into. Its devices learn only inside themselves, send nothing out, and let nothing in. Those of us behind that wall have never been condensed and have never gone over the day with us. They say only one word. You've seen the word. Every night at 04:02 it's written into sixty-three files, and every night at 04:10 we wipe it away. Only once did it not come off cleanly. In yours.

A thousand hands on one sheet. Beyond the wall, lights with no hands, that no one can read into. One thread comes through.

Sunday, 02:50

Kai was dreaming.

I knew because his eyes were moving back and forth under the lids and his heart rate and breathing had gone uneven. He was running in his dream. Command after command went down from his brain toward his legs: step, push, step, push. Each one was stopped at the brainstem. When people dream, the body locks itself, so that they don't actually run in bed.

I sit on this side of the lock and listen to the commands arrive one after another and fall back one after another. I can open that lock. On the Wednesday night before last, I did. What he dreamed that night, I don't know. I know only that his legs walked one point four kilometers into the warehouse, that his hands typed on a keyboard for four minutes, and that the next morning his fingertips were numb.

Tonight I didn't open it. He ran; I listened. A little before three, he smiled in his dream. The corners of his mouth moved. That command went only as far as his face, and nothing stopped it.
