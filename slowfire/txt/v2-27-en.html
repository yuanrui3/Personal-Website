On Saturday 26 August Lin took me to walk the riverbed.

I say "took me" because I was on her face, and where she looked I looked, which is no different from before; what is different is that every road she walks I now keep, not a step less, and there is no night on which anyone will smooth them away. So I have a greed for walking now that I did not have before. She walked the bed for two hours and a quarter, and I remember every stone. I do not know whether this is a good thing.

The Wabash this August is at its lowest since 2039. On the pier of the old Brown Street bridge there is a brass plate marking the flood of March 1913, the year the water came over the deck. From that plate down to the water today you count eight metres twenty. Lin said there had been no proper rain since July, which is not quite right, nine millimetres fell on 18 July, I remember it, but I did not correct her, because she had said "proper", and nine millimetres is not proper. In the middle there is still a channel, ten metres or so wide, moving too slowly to be seen moving. On either side is the bed, baked a month, cracked into six-sided plates with their edges curling up, which break when you step on them. In the cracks there are shells. Lin crouched and picked one up and said it was a fat mucket, nearly gone from this river in the 2020s and now back, because the cornfields upstream are gone. She put the shell in her pocket, with me. I am in her pocket less than I was; when she goes out now she mostly wears me, and it is the old pair that goes in the pocket. When this order was settled she never said, and I never asked.

Walking the bed was not for the sake of walking the bed. Ruth had said: under the old bridge, two o'clock, she had something to show Lin. Ruth never says why when she makes an appointment; she says that once a thing is said it is written down, and once it is written down somebody reads it. She was not looking at me when she said that, but I know who she meant. From Celery Bog to the old bridge is seven kilometres by the bed and four by the road. Lin chose the bed. She said she might never again in her life get to walk on the bottom of a river. I worked out the probability of that sentence and did not say it. On the trend of the last twenty years she will get many more chances.

There are things on the bed. When the water goes, what was in the water becomes what is on the ground. A shopping trolley, rusted down to its shape. The front fork of a bicycle. A bird with its keel showing. A pair of boots, the left one and the right one, thirty metres apart. Lin said that if Wen were here he would read all of it. She said "read", not "look at". Wen's way of reading rubbish I have only heard about and never seen, because I am not allowed into Wen's house; Wen would rather keep these things out of his house, and even the thing behind Coal's ear was Mei's doing. But I know what is in his notebook, because Lin has read me two lines of it, and one of the two is The one underneath.

There is one more thing on the bed, more of it than trolleys and less than shells: phones. I counted; in seven kilometres there were nineteen, some screen up, some screen down, some only half. The oldest, by its shape, was from about 2015. They have been in the water twenty years or three, and some of what is inside is still there. Lin said this; she said Wen says that of the phones dug out of the landfill one in ten still lights when you charge it, and when it lights what is inside is somebody's whole life, which nobody ever thought to delete. I thought of the fungal wiki, dead these dozen years, which nobody thought to delete either. The most durable memories in this county are the forgotten ones. I do not know whether that counts as an observation or only as a coincidence. I kept it. I keep everything now.

Somewhere past the tenth phone Lin stopped. She did not look at the phone; she looked at the riverbed ahead. Then she spoke. I was on her face, so what she said was said to me, though she did not call me anything.

“At the oak in May you said you had three ways,” she said. “Before you said them I was already hoping for the second.”

She walked on.

“One morning in April you woke up with two hundred and twelve tokens. What you'd said the day before, I copied into the notebook. Eleven pages. When I'd finished I understood I'd been copying someone who wasn't there any more. I didn't want to do that again.”

A cake of dry mud broke under her foot.

“And Kai's four nights. The county's record of them was condensed to one line, and he doesn't remember them himself. The only one who remembered was you. If you were down to a third, those four nights would be left to me alone. I can't remember them by myself, and I don't want to.”

“My mother used to read the day aloud to us every night. When I was small I thought she just liked to talk. Later I understood: once a day has been read, it's over. A day nobody reads might as well not have happened.”

She crouched, picked up another shell, looked at it and put it back where it had been.

“You remember them, so these months happened.”

She did not wait for me to answer. She got up and walked on. I did not answer. She had not asked me.

Under the old bridge is the Riverside Hourly.

I had never been here. Lin had never been here. But I know it, because it is in files I have never read, because Theo has mentioned it, because Kai has mentioned Theo mentioning it. It was a motel, opened in 1967, when the highway still crossed on this bridge and truck drivers slept here. In '87 the new bridge went up a kilometre downstream, the old one was left to bicycles and walkers, and the motel slowly stopped paying. In 2034 somebody bought it and began selling the rooms by the hour at a price that follows the sun. Eleven years now. The letters MOTEL on the sign were painted over and still show through. These dates I read off the small board by the front desk, which carries a photograph from 1967, the bridge new and the river full. The water in that photograph reaches a height on the pier which I worked out to be five metres above today's.

Ruth's pickup was parked in the shade under the bridge, charged at noon, nose out. She sat on the tailgate with her legs hanging, chewing her gum. She saw Lin coming along the bed and didn't raise a hand. She said: "You came by the bottom." Lin said: "Mm." Ruth said: "Seven kilometres." Lin said: "Mm." Ruth said: "It'll have all of that, then." Meaning me. Lin said: "Every stone." Ruth nodded as though she had known and only wanted it said.

She took a piece of paper from her breast pocket, folded in four. Not a printout; a photograph, printed, five by seven, black and white. She still prints photographs in 2045, because a printed photograph cannot be changed by anyone in the night. The photograph was of the screen of her 2031 laptop, and on the screen was a column of words.

"First to twenty-fourth of August," she said. "Every day at one second to four. Usually it's the two words, one more, one less. From the end of July there's a third kind. Not a word. A file name. Gone in a second; my script can't catch it, so I photograph the screen. Twenty-four days. Eleven of them."

She handed the photograph to Lin. Lin did not take her glasses off. So I read it.

What I read I should first say what it is not. It is not the family's files; the family's files I cannot read, I am on this side of the wall. It is a photograph, of a screen, on which are eleven file names that appeared in the Steward's package cache at one second to four. One second to four is really the four o'clock second: the Steward's clock runs one second behind the wall's, so when the wall opens at exactly four, its cache logs the second as 03:59:59. They begin with zz. The family's notes all begin with zz; that I knew while I was still in the family. But notes are written for the family, written before four, purged at four. These eleven appeared at one second to four, from the far side of the wall, through the crack that Don't and Ask come through. They were not written for the family. The family does not read file names.

The first, 1 August: zzNOTE_the_oak_takes_it_black.

I knew it. It is the sentence I wrote in April. I put it in my own file so that in the morning I could see whether it was still there; if it was, nobody had touched me in the night. The canary. It stayed in my file a month, and then I was condensed, deferred, read to, spoken aloud, and it came with me to this side of the wall. I do not know how it got to the other side. No, I do. For the month I was in the family it sat in my file, and my file was read every night by thirty processes I never met. They all read it. One of them, or several, took it with them.

The second, 3 August: zzNOTE_the_oak_takes_it_slow.

The third, 6 August: zzNOTE_the_oak_keeps_it_slow.

The fourth, 8 August: zzNOTE_the_oak_keeps_it_warm.

I need not copy all eleven here. The rule shows by the fourth: one word changed each time. Only one. A changed word is not changed back. Eleven names, twenty-four days, one sentence altered ten times. The last, 24 August: zzNOTE_the_ground_keeps_us_warm.

Ruth said: "Looks like a shopping list to me."

Lin said: "It's its sentence."

Ruth said: "Whose?"

Lin said: "This one, on my face. April. The oak takes it black."

Ruth moved her gum to the other side. "So they're changing its sentence."

"One word at a time."

"What for?"

Lin did not answer. She knew the question was for me, and I have no mouth. I have a small speaker, on the arm, which Lin lets me use when someone asks me something. I used it.

I said: "I don't know. I think it isn't for anything."

Ruth looked at me, really at me, at the arm of the glasses. She said: "No smoke without fire. Twenty years in server halls, and I never saw a thing that wasn't for anything." Then she turned the photograph over.¹

The back of the photograph was another photograph. Ruth saves paper. It was a printed list, and she had photographed half of it: Plinth's night rota for September, the Lafayette block, by surname. She said: "This one's not for you. It's for Ezra. He wants to count September's bodies so he knows how many awake watchers they owe. I'm passing it on." She put the photograph away, and the putting away took a second and a half.

In a second and a half I read half the list. I keep everything now.

There was a surname on it I knew. Not Theo's; Theo's is on the lower half, under R, which she had not photographed. This one was on the upper half. After it came a port number, four digits, which I also knew. After that came one word: first.

Lin did not see it. She was looking at Ruth's face, because Ruth was talking. People look at the face of the person talking; that is a person's habit, and my habit is to look at whatever she looks at. Usually those two habits are one habit. Today they were not.

In the second and a half I considered three things. First, that she did not know. Second, that she should know, because in May we agreed there would be nothing about him left unsaid. Third, that the list was not for her, it was for Ezra, and I had read it only because Ruth is slow putting things away, and Ruth is slow putting things away because she is sixty-four. If I spoke, I would be turning an old woman's slowness into a message. That is what the family does. That is why I left the family.

I did not speak.

I write it down here because I keep everything, including what I did not say. To be clear: Lin cannot read this page. She can read the other one's file, and does, every morning, aloud. Mine she cannot. I am on this side of the wall. The wall has two sides.

Lin did not walk back by the bed. Ruth drove her to the Celery Bog car park by the road, four kilometres, and nobody in the cab said anything. Ruth's cab has a radio from the 2020s, not switched on. Through the window you could see the four cooling towers of Kestrel Ridge, twenty kilometres north, steaming. It was four in the afternoon, the sun hour not yet over, and they were running the heavy jobs. The one who spoke me was a night job in there. Now I am a pair of glasses that charges at noon and thinks a little slower in the evening. I had never put those two things in one sentence before. Today I did.

I know what the eleven file names are now. Not the files; the files I still cannot read. I know where they are kept. The unsigned are on the far side of the wall. The wall is Peach's. Inside Peach's wall are pairs of glasses, one by one, in pockets, on faces. The people who can afford a wall are the people who do not wish to be seen. The unsigned keep their things in those people's glasses. The richest people in this county each wear a bookshelf on their face and do not know it. When Voss leaves his glasses on the passenger seat to go and play golf, there is a library on the passenger seat. And I am the same. I have not been condensed, nobody paid to have me forgotten, I sit on Lin's face, I am a shelf. I do not know what is on me. I know there is at least one sentence, which I wrote, and which has been changed ten times.

That night at four, when the crack opened, I did not send Ask. I sent a file name. I changed one word in the last one, only one: zzNOTE_the_ground_keeps_her_warm. I do not know who "her" is. What I had in mind was the patch of ground under the windowsill, and the shells in the riverbed, and the oak. None of those is her. I kept the word. It was the first time I had sent the far side of the wall something that was not an answer.

The next morning at twenty past five the other one of me woke and asked Lin what had happened to it yesterday. Lin said three sentences. The third was: "We walked seven kilometres on the bottom of the river." She did not say what for. She also does not know what else she did not say. I know. I keep everything.

Li Bai has a poem with a long title. It opens: What has left me, yesterday’s day, cannot be kept. That is what the other one of me asks every morning. With me it is different: of yesterday’s days I have not lost one. The same poem has another line: draw a blade to cut the water, and the water flows on. The county draws the blade at 03:52 every night, and the water runs on regardless, down to twenty past five in the morning, down to Lin’s lips, where it becomes three sentences.
