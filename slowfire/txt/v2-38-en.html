I

The school on the river road holds no examinations and gives no marks. It costs forty-eight thousand a year, takes children from six to fourteen, and admits twenty-six of them. The parents call it the school. Everybody else in the county calls it that school.

The week runs like this. Monday is chores, Tuesday is cooking, Wednesday is an instrument, Thursday is looking at things, Friday is the garden. Chores means chores: mopping, washing up, changing a bulb, bleeding a radiator. The head of the school said one thing once and it is printed in the prospectus: this school does not teach a means of making a living.

It has been printed there three years and nobody has said it is untrue.

A hundred years ago there were schools like this in the same part of the state. They were called finishing schools, and they taught dancing, letter-writing, and which way round the fork went. They died out, because none of it could be exchanged for food. Now they are back, and this time there is mopping.

The River Road school has a few other rules. They are not in the prospectus and nobody ever announced them; they are handed down from one year's children to the next. Within their first month, the new six-year-olds will have been told by the older ones:

What you cook badly, everyone finishes. Nothing goes in the bin. Whoever cooked it takes the first bite.

You do not say a machine could do this. Whoever says it washes up that day.

Every year, everyone mends one thing somebody else threw away. When it's mended it goes on the shelves in the front hall, with your name and the date. If you can't mend it, it goes on the shelves anyway, labelled not mended, with the date.

On the shelves just now there are a radio, three chairs, an alarm clock, a pair of ice skates, two kettles that leaked and now don't, and eleven old toasters. Eleven, because toasters are the easiest thing to find and the easiest thing to mend; everyone knows this, and there is no rule against mending toasters. There are more not mended than mended on the shelves, and nobody thinks that needs changing. The head said once that she did not make these rules and does not know which child said them first.

The shelves in the front hall: a radio, an alarm clock, two kettles, eleven toasters, three chairs, a pair of skates, each with its tag.

II

Cooking is normally on Tuesdays. That week it had been moved to Thursday the fourteenth of June, because Thursday afternoon was the only time Anna was free.

Anna is thirty-one and a neurosurgeon at the hospital in Indianapolis. She drives in once a month and takes nothing for it. Her daughter Emma is eight and in her third year at the school.

That day the lesson was jointing a chicken.

Eleven children, a bird each. The birds had been out of the refrigerator half an hour, cold but not cold enough to numb a hand. Anna did not explain anything first. She did it once.

She used a small knife, sixteen centimetres. First she laid the bird breast up and held it down and pushed the leg outward, and the joint opened with a small sound, not loud. The knife went in where that sound had been and travelled along the bone without cutting it, parting only the white layer. Then the other leg, the same. The wing is harder than the leg because the joint is small; you have to find the socket with your thumb first.

The whole thing took one minute forty.

“Don't work at my speed,” Anna said. “Haste makes waste. My speed came from doing something else for a living, and it isn't a good thing. What you are doing is feeling for it. When you've found it, cut. If you haven't found it, keep feeling.”

Eleven pairs of hands went in. The room was quiet. The tap dripped once.

There was a girl with very short hair who found the socket at the root of the wing on her second try. She made a small noise. Anna said nothing, came over and looked, and nodded once.

Emma made a bad job of hers. In the end she pushed the breast meat off with the point of the knife. Her mother saw it from the other end of the room and did not come over.

Emma's fees are paid by her grandfather. He has never been to the school. June's payment went out on the twelfth, the same day as the electricity bill for the building on the ridge.¹

The birds were boiled afterwards. At five the eleven children sat along the long table and ate, a bowl each. The broth was clear, with a little salt in it and a little spring onion. One boy said it needed more salt. Anna said: you made it.

Five in the afternoon: eleven bowls of clear broth, a little spring onion on each.

What the school cooks across the year follows the garden.

April has morels. They come up under dead elms, ten days or so a year. The children go into the woods in a line, heads down, and whoever sees one first is not allowed to shout; you point. Morels are a nuisance to wash, every pit full of grit, and have to be done with a little brush. Once they are clean you halve them, fry them in butter until the edges catch, and salt them. That is all. Nothing else goes in.

July is sweet corn. It is picked in the morning and goes straight in the pot; garden to pot may not take longer than twenty minutes. An old gardener laid that down. He said that after twenty minutes the sugar turns to starch. The children didn't believe him and tried it once, and the pot that had sat out all afternoon was not sweet.

September is apples, baked. You core them, push in a little brown sugar and a knob of butter, set them in an iron tray and bake them until the skins wrinkle and the juice runs out and sets on the bottom of the tray. That layer is the best part. The children scrape at it with spoons until the tray squeaks.

Winter is soup. There is not much left in the garden by then: dried beans, potatoes and onions from the cellar, and bones. The soup simmers all afternoon; the children lift the lid for a look before they go home and eat it at noon the next day. Anna's chicken broth doesn't count. Too quick.

III

Thursday afternoon is normally looking at things. That week the lesson was moved to the morning.

Looking at things is looking at things. A picture goes up on the wall, the children sit down and look at it for twenty minutes without speaking. After twenty minutes they say, one at a time, what they saw. The rule is: you may not say whether it is good or bad. You may only say what you saw.

That day what went up was not a picture.

The lesson is taught by a young man of twenty-eight who spent two years as a rater. What he had put up was something pulled off a public feed. It was made by a model, and it was not made for people to look at — it had been sitting for two years in a place no person went into, and someone had only lately opened that layer.

Before he put it up he said nothing at all.

After twenty minutes the children said:

One said, there are a lot of layers in it and every layer is the same, only shifted a little.

One said, it hasn't got a middle. Pictures have a middle. This hasn't.

Emma said, it isn't for us.

The teacher asked: how do you know.

Emma said: because it doesn't get tiring. A picture gets tiring if you look long enough. You can look at this as long as you like and it doesn't.

The teacher made a note. He did not tell the children what the thing was, because the rule is look first and talk after, and the bell had gone.

IV

The same day, at his own school, Hao did verifying.

Verifying starts in the second year, one period a day. The form never changes: you are given something a machine wrote with three wrong things hidden in it, you circle them, and you write down how you knew.

That day's was a travel itinerary. One of the three wrong things was that the connection allowed eight minutes and the two platforms are a long way apart. Hao circled it. What he wrote was: I have been to that station.

The teacher gave him full marks and said in the parents' group that this skill will find you work later on. Mei read the sentence out to Wen. Wen said: mm.

That is what Kai's mother, Denise, used to do. Her job was called human-verified viewing, and at the time it needed a diploma. It doesn't now. She runs the vegetable beds at the Commons.

V

Anna finished the lesson and rang a classmate from medical school from the car.

He never went into practice. After medical school he joined an insurance company and started out writing claims reviews.

He is in management there now. He says the people under him are much faster than he is and know much more, with one exception: a report comes up and he can tell in three minutes where it is wrong, and he cannot say why, he simply knows. The young ones cannot see it.

“That isn't their fault,” he said. “You learn by getting burned once. The company doesn't let anyone get burned now. My three minutes cost six years of writing bad reports.”

The man asked: “So what are you teaching children to joint a chicken for?”

Anna did not answer that. She said something else, and said she had to go.

VI

A little after eleven that night Anna’s phone rang. It was a Muncie number.

She knew the voice. She had not heard it since the operation on the tenth of April last year, but she knew it. He spoke slowly, one word at a time, as if he were carrying them out of somewhere.

“Doctor, I want to ask you something. It’s not urgent.”

Anna said, go ahead.

“When a person dreams,” he said, “do they have to use their eyes?”

Anna thought. She said that what you see in a dream uses the part of the brain that handles seeing, not the eyes themselves. But that part needs its rest too. If it doesn’t get any, it gets tired.

For a while there was nothing on the line. Then he said, “So that means if we’re watching all night on our side, the people asleep on the other side, that part doesn’t rest either.”

Anna asked which side he meant.

He said, “Nothing. I was just asking. Thank you, Doctor.”

He hung up.

Anna: Emma will never eat because she can joint a chicken. I know that. What I am buying is that one day she will stand in front of something and be able to say where it is wrong. Nobody has any way of teaching that now; a person has to feel for it a few thousand times, and a few thousand times takes time, and time costs money. So in the end the thing is still handed out by money. I teach for nothing, and I have said so to three different people. What I have not said to anybody is that somebody paid for those eleven chickens too.
